> **Key Takeaways**
>
> - ISO 13485 certification for a Canadian medical device manufacturer is driven by Health Canada licensing rules, so the regulatory class of the device decides how urgent and how broad the project is.
> - ISO 13485:2016 is a regulatory-flavored standard: it asks for documented procedures, risk management across the product life cycle, design controls and traceability that an ISO 9001 system does not require.
> - Supplier qualification and device records are where many first audits find gaps, so build them early rather than in the last month.
> - A realistic plan treats Stage 1 and Stage 2 as two separate readiness tests, with time between them to close findings.

A medical device manufacturer in Mississauga, Burlington or Kitchener usually learns about ISO 13485 certification the same way: a license application, a distributor or a hospital buyer asks for the certificate, and the plant has to work backward from the date. The requirement is easy to state and harder to schedule, because the standard touches design, purchasing, production, complaints and regulatory reporting all at once.

This guide walks through the sequence a plant can follow, from the rules that make the certificate necessary to the audits that confirm it. [ISO 13485:2016](https://www.iso.org/standard/59752.html) is the current edition, and no newer edition has been published. Clause numbers below refer to that edition.

## The Regulatory Framework: ISO 13485, MDSAP, and Health Canada Requirements

Certification is a voluntary standard until a regulator or customer makes it a condition. In Canada, that condition comes from the federal Medical Devices Regulations. The [Medical Devices Regulations](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/sor-98-282/) set the licensing and quality system requirements, and devices are grouped into four classes by risk. Lower-risk Class I devices are handled differently from Class II, III and IV devices, which need a device license.

For a license application on a Class II, III or IV device, Health Canada expects the manufacturer to hold a certificate issued under the Medical Device Single Audit Program, known as MDSAP. That program audits a quality system against ISO 13485 plus the specific requirements of the participating regulators, which include Canada, the United States, Australia, Brazil and Japan. In practice, the certificate a plant needs is an MDSAP certificate, and the audit is built on ISO 13485.

> **Important:** ISO 13485 certification and MDSAP certification are not the same document. A plant that sells into Canada should confirm with its certification body which scheme the license application requires before the audit is booked.

Three questions settle the plan at the start:

- **Which class is each device?** The classification rules set the license path, and one product family can contain devices in different classes.
- **Which markets will the plant sell into?** Selling only in Canada is a different project from selling in Canada and the United States, because the audit scope grows with each regulator.
- **Who is the manufacturer of record?** The company named on the label carries the regulatory obligations, even when contract partners make parts of the device.

[Health Canada's overview of medical devices](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/medical-devices/about-medical-devices.html) explains how classification and licensing fit together, and it is a sensible starting point for the plant's regulatory lead.

## Core Quality Management System Elements for Medical Device Plants

The structure of ISO 13485:2016 will feel familiar to a plant with an ISO 9001 system, but the tone is different. Where ISO 9001 lets a company decide how much to document, ISO 13485 asks for specific procedures and records, and it excludes the continual-improvement language that ISO 9001 carries.

The elements that most often need new work are these:

- **The medical device file.** Clause 4.2.3 requires a file for each device type or family, holding the specifications, the manufacturing and inspection procedures, and the labeling and packaging details.
- **Document and record control.** Clause 4.2.4 and 4.2.5 require records to be kept for at least the life of the device, with a minimum retention period set by regulation.
- **Process validation.** Clause 7.5.6 requires validation of any production process whose output cannot be fully verified by later inspection, such as sealing, sterilization or certain welds.
- **Software validation.** Clause 4.1.6 requires validation of software used in the quality system, which catches spreadsheets and tracking tools that plants have relied on for years.
- **Complaint handling and reporting.** Clauses 8.2.2 and 8.2.3 require a documented process for complaints and for reporting to regulatory authorities within the required time frames.

## Integrating Risk Management and ISO 14971 into Daily Operations

Risk management is the thread that runs through ISO 13485:2016. Clause 7.1 requires the plant to document one or more processes for risk management in product realization, and a widely used framework for that process is ISO 14971, the international standard for applying risk management to medical devices.

The common mistake is treating risk management as a design-phase document that is filed and forgotten. An auditor looks for the opposite: evidence that risk information flows both ways. Design inputs should reflect known hazards, and production, complaints and field feedback should update the risk file.

> **Key Consideration:** Link the complaint log to the risk file. When a complaint describes a hazard that the file did not anticipate, the risk file needs a dated update, and that update is the record an auditor will ask to see.

A workable routine connects three points in daily operations:

- **Change control.** Every change to a design, material or process gets a short risk screening before approval, with the result recorded.
- **Nonconforming product review.** The disposition decision considers whether the defect could affect safety or performance, not only whether it meets the drawing.
- **Management review.** Clause 5.6 requires the review to consider feedback, complaints and regulatory reporting, so risk trends belong on the agenda.

**Illustrative example:** A contract manufacturer that molds housings for a diagnostic device notices a pattern of hairline cracks at one cavity. The quality team checks the risk file, finds that cracking in that location was never listed as a hazard, adds it with a severity rating and ties the new control to the mold maintenance schedule.

## Design Controls, Traceability, and Technical Documentation Standards

Clause 7.3 of ISO 13485:2016 is the design and development clause, and it is more prescriptive than its ISO 9001 equivalent. It requires planned design stages, defined inputs, verification, validation, design transfer, design reviews and a controlled process for changes. Each stage produces records, and those records fill the design and development file.

A plant that only builds to a customer's print may claim an exclusion for design, but that exclusion must be justified in the quality manual and must match the regulatory status of the company. A contract manufacturer is not the manufacturer of record in most cases, but a company that sells under its own label cannot exclude design.

Traceability is the other half of this section. Clause 7.5.9 requires procedures for identification and traceability, and for implantable devices the requirements are stricter, including records of the components and the conditions of the work environment. The test an auditor applies is simple: pick a finished unit and ask for the path backward through lot numbers, components, process records and the people who handled it.

| Record | What it shows | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|
| Design inputs and outputs | The device does what was intended | Design and development file |
| Device history record | This unit was made as specified | Production records |
| Lot and serial traceability | Which materials went into this unit | Production and receiving records |
| Complaint and feedback log | How the device performs in use | Post-market records |

![a quality technician scanning lot labels on a tray of machined titanium components in a controlled assembly area](/images/blog/the-medical-device-manufacturer-s-roadmap-to-iso-13485-certification-in-canada/inline-2.webp)

Plants often find that traceability works in their ERP system for finished goods but breaks at the component level. The fix is usually a receiving process that assigns an internal lot to every incoming material, so the chain does not depend on a supplier's paperwork alone.

## Managing Supply Chain Risk and Critical Component Suppliers

Clause 7.4 of ISO 13485:2016 requires the plant to evaluate and select suppliers based on their ability to supply product that meets requirements. The extent of control depends on the effect the purchased item has on the finished device. A supplier of a sterile barrier or an implant-grade alloy is controlled more tightly than a supplier of shipping cartons.

The approach that satisfies auditors has four parts:

- **A supplier classification.** Each supplier is rated by how much its product could affect device safety or performance, with the rating recorded in the approved supplier list.
- **Qualification evidence.** Critical suppliers are evaluated by audit, certification review or a documented assessment, and the evidence is on file.
- **Purchasing information.** Purchase orders specify the requirements, and a quality agreement obliges the supplier to notify the plant of changes to its process or materials.
- **Ongoing monitoring.** Incoming inspection results, delivery performance and supplier corrective actions feed a periodic re-evaluation.

The quality agreement deserves special attention. Many plants discover during the audit that a critical supplier has changed a resin grade or coating process without telling anyone, and that nothing in the contract required notice. Writing the notification duty into the agreement before the audit closes that gap. The approach also supports the traceability described above, because a supplier lot that arrives with its own identification is easier to follow through production.

## Stage 1 and Stage 2 Certification Audits in Ontario

A certification body accredited under the program described by the [Standards Council of Canada](https://scc-ccn.ca/accreditation/about-accreditation) conducts the audits. For an MDSAP certificate, the body must also be recognized to audit under that program, so confirm that before signing a contract.

The first audit, Stage 1, is mostly a documentation and readiness review. The auditor checks that the quality manual, procedures, medical device files and internal audit and management review records exist and that the system is ready for the second audit. The [guide to Stage 1 and Stage 2 audits](/blog/iso-certification-audit-stage-1-vs-stage-2-what-each-audit-checks) describes the general approach in more detail.

Stage 2 tests whether the system works on the floor. The auditor follows product and records through the processes, interviews operators, and samples design projects, complaints, supplier files and corrective actions. Medical device audits tend to spend considerable time on three areas: design controls, complaint handling and regulatory reporting, and process validation.

> **Did You Know?** ISO 13485:2016 requires internal audits and a management review to be completed before certification, so the plant needs at least one full cycle of each on record when Stage 2 begins.

The sequence that gives a plant the best odds is straightforward. Finish the internal audit first, hold the management review, close the findings that surface, and only then book Stage 2. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is ISO 13485 certification mandatory for medical device manufacturers in Canada?

The standard itself is voluntary, but the regulations make quality system certification a condition of licensing for Class II, III and IV devices. A manufacturer of those devices needs the MDSAP certificate, which is built on ISO 13485, before Health Canada will license the device. Class I manufacturers face different requirements, though customers often ask for certification anyway.

### What is the difference between ISO 13485 and MDSAP in Canada?

ISO 13485 is the international quality management standard for medical devices. MDSAP is an audit program that checks a quality system against ISO 13485 together with the additional requirements of participating regulators, including Health Canada. One MDSAP audit can serve several markets, and Canada relies on its certificate for device licensing.

### How long does it take an Ontario manufacturer to achieve ISO 13485 certification?

A typical estimate for a small or mid-sized plant starting with a basic system is nine to eighteen months, depending on the device class, the state of the design records and how quickly the team can complete validation work. A plant with an existing ISO 9001 system and clean records may move faster. The audit schedule of the certification body also affects the timeline.

### Can an ISO 9001 certified facility easily transition to ISO 13485?

An ISO 9001 system gives a useful base, since document control, internal audits, corrective action and management review already exist. The gaps are usually design controls, risk management, process and software validation, traceability, complaint handling and regulatory reporting. ISO 9001:2026 is now the current edition, and many certified plants still operate under ISO 9001:2015 during the transition, but neither edition covers those medical device requirements.

### What are the most common nonconformances during an ISO 13485 audit?

Common findings include incomplete design files, risk files that were never updated after complaints, unvalidated processes or software, weak supplier controls and gaps in lot traceability. Internal audits and management reviews that exist on paper but lack evidence of action also come up often. These areas line up with the sections above, which is why they are worth testing before the certification body arrives.
