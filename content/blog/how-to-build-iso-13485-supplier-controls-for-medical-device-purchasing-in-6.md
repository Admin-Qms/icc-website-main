> **Key Takeaways**
>
> - Clause 7.4 of ISO 13485:2016 expects supplier controls to match the effect each purchased product or service has on the finished medical device.
> - A written risk classification of suppliers is what lets a small quality team spend effort where it counts.
> - Purchasing information must say exactly what is being bought, and it must travel with the order, not live in someone's memory.
> - Monitoring and re-evaluation are ongoing, and the records they produce are what an auditor asks to see first.

**Illustrative example:** A contract manufacturer near Kitchener ships orthopedic instrument housings. Its machining is tightly controlled, yet the sterilization service that finishes each lot was approved years ago on a one-page form and never looked at again. Nothing is wrong with the housings, but the file for the one supplier that can affect patient safety is thinner than the file for the carton vendor.

That imbalance is what ISO 13485 supplier controls and purchasing requirements are meant to prevent. The standard asks for effort in proportion to risk, which means a sterilizer and a carton supplier should never be handled alike. The six steps below show how to build that difference into everyday purchasing.

The current edition is ISO 13485:2016, and the clause references in this article refer to that edition. The official standard page is on [iso.org](https://www.iso.org/standard/59752.html), and the purchasing requirements sit mainly in clause 7.4, with outsourced processes covered in clause 4.1.5.

## Step 1: Classify Suppliers by Risk to Product Quality

**What to do.** List every external party that supplies a product, material or service that touches the device or its quality system. Then sort them by how much their performance could affect device safety and performance. ISO 13485:2016 clause 7.4.1 says the evaluation and control of suppliers must be proportionate to the risk associated with the medical device and to the effect the purchased product has on it.

A practical sort uses three or four tiers:

- **Critical suppliers** provide components, materials, sterilization or other special processes where a failure could reach the patient or user.
- **Significant suppliers** provide items that affect fit, function or labeling but where incoming inspection can catch most problems.
- **Routine suppliers** provide standard items with little effect on the device, such as general fasteners or packaging cartons.
- **Non-product suppliers** provide services such as calibration or pest control that support the quality system without touching the device.

**What you need.** The product risk management file, the bill of materials, and a list of outsourced processes. Your regulatory status matters here too: the federal [Medical Devices Regulations](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/sor-98-282/) set the licensing and quality system context in which these decisions are made, and the device class drives how much rigor is reasonable.

**The common mistake.** Treating the approved supplier list as the classification. A list of names with no recorded rationale does not show proportionality. Write down why each supplier sits in its tier, so an auditor can see the logic.

## Step 2: Define Criteria for Supplier Selection and Qualification

**What to do.** Write criteria for selecting, evaluating and re-evaluating suppliers before the first purchase order goes out, then apply different criteria to each tier. The standard requires that the criteria be based on the supplier's ability to provide product that meets requirements, on performance, and on the effect of the purchased product on the device.

For a critical supplier, qualification usually draws on several kinds of evidence:

1. A completed quality questionnaire covering their own quality system and any certifications they hold.
2. A review of sample parts, first article results or process validation data.
3. An on-site or remote audit of the processes that matter to your device.
4. A written quality agreement that sets expectations for change notification, record retention and access for audits.

For a routine supplier, a short questionnaire and a successful trial order may be enough. In the example above, the sterilization provider would get an audit and a quality agreement, while the carton vendor would get a questionnaire and a trial order. A supplier's quality system certificate counts as one input, not the whole decision.

> **Key Consideration:** A certificate tells you a supplier's quality system was assessed. It does not tell you whether that scope covers the process you are buying. Check the certificate scope against the actual product before relying on it.

**What you need.** A supplier qualification procedure, a questionnaire template, an audit checklist and an approval form that records who decided and why.

**The common mistake.** Qualifying on price and lead time and doing the quality review afterward. If the quality evaluation comes second, it tends to become a formality.

## Step 3: Document Purchasing Requirements Clearly

**What to do.** Make sure each purchase order, drawing or specification gives the supplier everything needed to deliver the right product. Clause 7.4.2 of ISO 13485:2016 requires purchasing information to describe the product to be purchased, including, where appropriate, requirements for product acceptance, procedures, processes and equipment, personnel qualification, and quality management system requirements.

Two items are easy to overlook. First, the standard expects a **written agreement that the supplier will notify you before changes** to the purchased product take effect, so you can decide whether the change affects the device. Second, purchasing information must be reviewed and approved before it is communicated to the supplier.

> **Important:** Purchasing information must be kept as a record, because traceability depends on it. If a lot of housings fails in the field, the purchase order should lead straight to the supplier lot that produced it.

**What you need.** Controlled drawings and specifications, a purchase order template that carries revision levels, and a quality agreement template for critical suppliers.

**The common mistake.** Sending a part number with no revision. When the drawing changes and the supplier keeps building to the old one, the purchasing record cannot show who was responsible.

## Step 4: Verify Purchased Products and Services

**What to do.** Decide how each delivery will be checked, and match the check to the tier. Clause 7.4.3 of ISO 13485:2016 requires the organization to establish and implement inspection or other activities necessary to make sure purchased product meets the purchasing information.

Incoming inspection against a sampling plan is the most familiar. For critical items, review of the supplier's certificate of conformance and test data may be paired with periodic confirmation testing. For services such as sterilization or calibration, the equivalent is reviewing the service report and confirming the result against what was ordered.

If you or your customer intend to carry out verification at the supplier's premises, the purchasing information must state the intended verification arrangements and the method of product release.

**What you need.** Incoming inspection instructions per part or family, a nonconforming material procedure, and a way to hold unreleased lots separate from released stock.

**The common mistake.** Inspecting everything the same way. Heavy inspection of routine items drains time from the checks that protect the device, so the plan from Step 1 should drive how deep each inspection goes.

## Step 5: Monitor Supplier Performance and Address Issues

**What to do.** Track how each supplier performs after approval, and define what happens when performance slips. Clause 7.4.1 of ISO 13485:2016 requires the organization to plan the monitoring and re-evaluation of suppliers, and to keep records of the results.

Useful signals usually come from data you already collect:

- **On-time delivery and lot acceptance rates** show whether a supplier is reliable day to day.
- **Nonconformance reports and returns** show how often and how seriously product misses the specification.
- **Corrective action response** shows whether a supplier takes a problem seriously and fixes the cause.
- **Change notifications received or missed** show whether the supplier is following the agreement from Step 3.

When a supplier fails to meet requirements, the standard expects action in proportion to the risk and to the effect on the device. That may mean a corrective action request, tighter inspection, a supplier audit, or in serious cases, a move to a second source.

**What you need.** A simple scorecard, a defined review rhythm and a corrective action process that suppliers know about in advance.

**The common mistake.** Collecting the data and never acting on it. A scorecard with three consecutive poor results and no response tells the auditor that monitoring exists only on paper.

## Step 6: Re-Evaluate Suppliers and Maintain Records

**What to do.** Review each supplier on a schedule set by its risk tier, and update its status based on the evidence. Critical suppliers are normally reviewed more often than routine ones. The standard does not name a fixed interval; it asks for re-evaluation that makes sense for the risk, so write your interval down and follow it.

Each review should answer three questions: Is the supplier still capable of meeting requirements? Has anything changed in what you buy from them? Does the tier still fit? A supplier that began as routine may become critical when a design change makes its part more important.

Records matter as much as the review itself. Clause 7.4.1 requires records of the results of evaluation, monitoring and re-evaluation, and of any necessary actions. Many plants also keep the following together in one supplier file:

- The qualification package, including questionnaire, audit report and approval decision.
- Current quality agreements and the purchasing specifications they refer to.
- Performance data, corrective action requests and the review outcomes.

> **Did You Know?** Clause 4.1.5 of ISO 13485:2016 requires the organization to remain responsible for conformity to the standard and to customer and regulatory requirements when it chooses to outsource any process. Outsourcing does not move the responsibility to the supplier.

For Ontario manufacturers working toward licensing, [Health Canada's overview of medical devices](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/medical-devices/about-medical-devices.html) explains how devices are classified, which helps frame how deep supplier oversight should go. The article on [ISO 13485 and ISO 9001 differences](/blog/iso-13485-vs-iso-9001-differences-which-standard-does-your-plant-need/) shows how the medical device standard goes further on purchasing than a typical ISO 9001 system.

**What you need.** A review calendar, a supplier file structure and a status field that purchasing can see before it places an order.

**The common mistake.** Letting the approved status stay "approved" forever. Without a review date, nobody notices when a supplier has drifted out of the arrangement.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What supplier controls does ISO 13485 require?

ISO 13485:2016 requires a documented purchasing process, criteria for evaluating and selecting suppliers, controls proportionate to risk, purchasing information that describes what is being bought, verification of purchased product, and records of evaluation, monitoring and re-evaluation. The core requirements are in clause 7.4, with outsourced processes covered in clause 4.1.5.

### How should a medical device manufacturer qualify a new supplier?

Start with the supplier's risk tier. A critical supplier may need a questionnaire, sample or first article review, an audit and a quality agreement, while a routine supplier may only need a short questionnaire and a trial order. Whatever the tier, record the criteria used and who approved the supplier.

### Does ISO 13485 require every supplier to be audited?

No. The standard asks for evaluation and control in proportion to risk, so an audit is one option, not a blanket requirement. Many plants audit critical suppliers on site or remotely and rely on questionnaires, certificates and performance data for lower-risk ones.

### How often should suppliers be re-evaluated under ISO 13485?

The standard does not fix an interval. It requires planned re-evaluation, so the plant chooses a schedule based on risk and performance and then follows it. Annual review of critical suppliers is a common approach, with longer intervals for routine ones.

### What purchasing records should a manufacturer maintain for ISO 13485?

Keep the results of supplier evaluation, monitoring and re-evaluation, the purchasing information given to suppliers, verification records for received product, and any quality agreements. Records tied to traceability should be kept for the period the standard and the applicable regulations require.
