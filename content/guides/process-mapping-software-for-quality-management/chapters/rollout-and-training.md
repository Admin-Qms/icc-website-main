## Prepare role-based training

A rollout works better when people can practice the tasks they will actually perform. Start with the accepted process scope and identify the readers, process owners, supervisors, and quality staff who will use the information. The process owner can explain the workflow; the quality lead can prepare representative records; and the operations manager can help make time for the people who need to participate. The training plan should identify the decisions each role is expected to make and where questions go when the answer is unclear.

Build an exercise around the decisions each role will make. The [ICC process management page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/processes/) includes deployment and training in its published activities; agree separately on what support is proposed for the site. Ask an operator to find an instruction, a supervisor to follow an exception, and the owner to receive an unresolved question.

Build training around a representative process rather than a tour of every screen. An operator may need to find the instruction applicable to a step, while a process owner may need to explain how a disputed map is reviewed. A supervisor may need to identify the next decision when information is missing. Each exercise should start with a realistic question and end with an observable answer or an identified escalation route.

Use a suitable sample that participants are allowed to see. The process owner should confirm that the example reflects the agreed scope, and the quality lead should explain any limitations. A hypothetical or sanitized record may be more appropriate than a real production record for a demonstration. The objective is to practice the decision and route, not to expose information unnecessarily.

For the training exercise, record the participant's starting point, the task, and whether help was needed to find the applicable information. The [ISO auditing guidance collection](https://committee.iso.org/home/tc176/iso-9001-auditing-practices-group.html) offers educational material on evidence. If an access problem prevents the task, assign an investigation and repeat that portion under the intended role conditions before treating it as completed.

**Illustrative example:** During a hypothetical rehearsal, an operator locates the production map but cannot open its instruction. The supervisor repeats the route under the same access conditions and notes the failure. Assign an access investigation, give the participant an agreed interim source, and repeat that task before using attendance as evidence of readiness.

Define what a participant should be able to do before deciding how to judge the session. A useful observation might be that the person can find the correct process, explain the next step, and identify whom to contact when a record is missing. If the person needs help, record where the explanation or access arrangement was unclear. Attendance by itself does not show that the intended task can be performed.

The trainer should distinguish attendance from the participant's task outcome. Record the scope, sample, roles, and open questions; if access fails or the example conflicts with the current process, assign the next decision. Repeat the affected exercise after the issue is resolved before judging that role ready for the next stage.

## Pilot with representative users

A user pilot tests the proposed training and process view with people whose work reflects the intended rollout. Choose participants from the relevant roles and, where appropriate, include someone who did not help design the map. The goal is to learn whether the explanation supports the task without relying on the author to interpret every label. A small, representative exercise can expose a question before the organization expands the rollout.

The [ICC page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/processes/) describes process maps, flowcharts, owners, and links to controlled documents. Use those published descriptions to frame a practical session, but do not assume that a particular permission, notification, or training feature is available. Ask to see the proposed arrangement with the chosen sample and record what participants actually observe.

Agree on the pilot's scope and acceptance questions in advance. A participant might start at the process overview, locate the selected workflow, and explain where an instruction or decision record would be found. Another might examine an exception and identify the responsible role. The process owner confirms the expected answer, while the quality lead records the route and any help provided. An answer given only after prompting should be recorded with that context.

Represent the range of work that matters to the decision. If the process differs across sites, a pilot at one site may not establish that the same explanation works at another. If a role has different access from the presenter, test the route using an appropriate account or equivalent demonstration. Where a condition cannot be tested, record it as unknown and decide who will obtain the missing evidence.

**Illustrative example:** During a hypothetical user pilot, the operator uses a hypothetical production example without the map author's guidance. The supervisor then follows an exception route, and the process owner compares both explanations with the agreed workflow. The quality lead notes an ambiguous label and assigns a person to clarify it. Clarify the ambiguous label and repeat the affected pilot task before assessing the reader's route.

Feedback should identify the task and the point at which the reader became uncertain. "The map is confusing" is a useful signal, but it does not by itself tell the owner whether the label, the process explanation, or access to a linked document needs attention. Ask the participant what they expected to find and what they saw. Retain that distinction when recording the follow-up decision.

If a participant encounters an inaccessible destination, avoid assuming the cause. The destination may be unavailable, the role may not have access, or the sample may be incorrect. Record the test conditions and ask the responsible people to investigate. Until the issue is understood, the pilot can establish only that the participant could not complete that route under those conditions.

The process owner and operations manager should decide whether the pilot provides enough evidence to proceed, needs another targeted test, or should pause while an issue is resolved. Record the decision, its scope, and any conditions. A positive result for one exercise supports that exercise; it does not prove that every role or process is ready for rollout.

## Manage staged rollout

A staged rollout lets the organization introduce an agreed scope, examine what happens, and decide whether to expand. Define the processes and roles included in each stage and identify the person who can authorize the next one. The process owner provides the accepted workflow and outstanding questions; the quality lead brings the pilot record; and the operations manager considers the people and resources needed for the transition.

The [ICC process page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/processes/) presents deployment and training in broad terms. It does not establish a mandatory sequence or guarantee how a particular site's rollout will proceed. Ask the supplier what is proposed for the selected scope, which decisions remain with the organization, and what support is available. Record the answers without presenting an unverified workflow as a product feature.

Make the intended source of information clear for each stage. Participants need to know whether they should use an accepted process view, an existing instruction, or an interim route. If different groups transition at different times, explain the boundary and identify who resolves conflicting information. A staged plan should reduce uncertainty about the next action, not create two apparently authoritative sources without guidance.

Before expanding, review the evidence from the previous stage. Check whether representative users completed the intended tasks, whether important access questions were resolved, and whether the process owner accepted the relevant map. A remaining question may be acceptable for a limited scope if the authorized person understands and records the limitation. It should not be hidden in a general statement that the rollout is complete.

**Illustrative example:** For a hypothetical staged rollout, the operations manager proposes starting with the production workflow used in the pilot. The process owner confirms the intended scope, the quality lead lists the outstanding label question, and the supervisor explains which workers need the information. The team records who may authorize expansion and what evidence that person expects. The authorization decision should state the group included and the evidence required before expansion.

Decide how issues will be raised during the stage. A worker who cannot find a current instruction needs an agreed contact and a way to describe the task attempted. The receiving person should determine the next action and record any decision that affects the process. Do not assume that a software system automatically routes or resolves the issue; ask how the proposed arrangement supports the organization's chosen process.

If a critical question remains unanswered, the owner and authorized decision-maker should determine whether to hold, narrow, or reverse the stage under the organization's arrangements. Record the reason and the information needed for another decision. A date in a rollout plan is a target, not proof that the people, process, and information are ready.

At the end of a stage, retain the scope, training observations, unresolved items, decision-maker, and acceptance conditions. Make the result available to the people planning the next stage under appropriate controls. This record helps a later reader understand why the organization expanded or paused without claiming that the same outcome will occur everywhere.

## Evaluate use and feedback

After a group begins using the agreed process view, gather feedback about the tasks people actually try to perform. The process owner can review a small set of questions with operators and supervisors; the quality lead can compare their answers with the intended workflow and available records. The purpose is to identify where the explanation, access, or training needs attention, not to assume that every difficulty has the same cause.

The [ICC process management page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/processes/) describes process flowcharts and connections to controlled documents. It does not establish that a particular installation automatically measures use, collects feedback, or identifies obsolete content. Ask what information is available and decide how the organization will gather any additional observations it needs. Label supplier statements and demonstrated behavior accurately.

Use concrete prompts: What task were you trying to complete? Where did you begin? Which information did you expect to find? What happened next? A person may report that a map was hard to use because the label was unclear, the destination was inaccessible, or the process itself was disputed. Record the observation before deciding which explanation is correct.

Choose an appropriate sample of roles and situations rather than relying solely on the people who designed the rollout. Include someone who uses the process infrequently when that perspective matters. If feedback covers only one shift or location, record that limitation. A lack of reported problems is not proof that every intended user has been reached or that every route works.

**Illustrative example:** When hypothetical feedback arrives, an operator reports difficulty locating a hypothetical instruction from the production workflow. The supervisor repeats the route with the same sample, and the quality lead records the starting point and observed result. The process owner decides whether the next action is to clarify a label, investigate access, or seek more evidence. Investigate the observed route under the same access conditions before choosing a training or configuration response.

When feedback conflicts, preserve the different observations and compare the conditions. Two readers may have different roles, access, or process scopes. The owner should determine whether the difference is expected and whether additional explanation is needed. If the evidence is insufficient, assign a follow-up rather than declaring one account incorrect.

Record the decision and its owner, including any interim guidance to users and the condition for closing the issue. If the organization changes a map or training material, check the affected task with the people who rely on it. A correction to an explanation should be evaluated against the question that prompted it, not merely counted as a completed action.

Review the feedback with the person authorized to decide the next rollout step. Separate an isolated question from a pattern only when the evidence supports that conclusion; do not invent statistics or claim measured improvements. The team can decide whether another group is ready, whether a specific issue requires a pause, or whether more representative input is needed.

Use the pilot record to decide whether the next group can begin: identify the roles tested, the tasks they completed without coaching, and any access questions still open. The authorized manager can narrow or pause the stage if its conditions are not met.
