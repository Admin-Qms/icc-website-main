Chapter 4 of 9

Choose actions to close competence gaps

Assigning training and learning tasks in competency management software: choosing task-specific learning; assigning trainers and learners.

ISO Certification Consultants Editorial Team · 11 min read

Choose an action suited to the gap

A gap appears when the task requirement from the matrix is compared with a person's current evidence. The next decision is what to do about it. Many teams reach for a course by habit, but the Auditing Practices Group, in its guidance on auditing competence, lists a wider set of actions an organization may take to acquire or use the competence it needs: training, hiring people with specific competencies, on-the-job training, mentoring, reassigning tasks or people, adjusting the scope of a job, and subcontracting. The paper was written for ISO 9001:2015 clause 7.2 auditors and is used here as a description of options, not as a list of obligations.

The practical lesson is proportion. A new gauge may need a short demonstration and supervised practice. A recognized qualification may need an outside provider. A task that no one in the cell can cover may call for hiring or changing who does the work. The decision belongs to the supervisor who understands the schedule, working with the process owner who defined the task.

Match the action to the kind of gap

Start by naming the gap precisely. "Needs training" is rarely specific enough. Ask which element of the task requirement is missing: knowledge, a practical skill, a qualification, or simply familiarity with a changed method.

Kind of gapLikely actionTypical owner
Missing knowledgeLearning content with a quizTrainer or training coordinator
Missing practical skillCoached on-the-job practiceSupervisor or authorized coach
Changed method or equipmentShort briefing plus supervised useProcess owner
Required formal qualificationExternal provider or programQuality lead
No capacity in the cellReassign work, cross-train or adjust the jobSupervisor with process owner

Record, for each chosen action, the responsible person, the intended outcome, and the date by which it should be complete. The intended outcome should be phrased as something observable, such as "performs first-article inspection of the second product family without supervision," rather than "completes course."

Illustrative example: An unnamed Canadian manufacturer of machined components finds that its alternate inspector understands the gauge procedure but has never inspected the second bracket family. The supervisor chooses coached on-the-job practice on that family rather than a classroom session, names a coach from the evidenced inspectors, and records the intended outcome as independent inspection of the family, to be confirmed by a later practical assessment.

Weigh the cost of each action against the risk of the gap. A missed step in a safety-critical or customer-critical inspection justifies more preparation than a task with low consequence and frequent review. The supervisor should also consider who else is affected: a coach who spends two shifts with the alternate is a coach who is not inspecting, so the schedule must absorb the time. Writing that cost into the plan may reduce the chance that the action is dropped when production gets busy.

Where several people share the same gap, consider a single group action with individual sign-off, rather than separate plans for each person. A short briefing for a team is efficient, but individual practical evaluations are recorded when the task's agreed evidence rules require them.

Distinguish learning delivery from other actions

When comparing software, keep delivery of learning separate from the other ways of closing a gap. The ICC training and competency module page describes a training library with quizzes and pass scores and training campaigns delivered online, in a classroom, or on the job. Those describe delivery of learning. Hiring, reassignment or adjusting a job are different decisions. Ask the supplier how, if at all, the system would record that a gap was closed by reassigning work rather than by training. Do not assume a feature for it. If the system covers only learning delivery, the team needs a documented manual way to record the other actions, and that belongs in the results sheet from the evaluation chapter.

Decide when no suitable action exists

Sometimes no trainer, provider, or coach is available. Treat that as a recorded exception with an owner, not as a silent delay. The supervisor should state the interim staffing rule, such as keeping the task with evidenced colleagues, and the process owner decides whether the gap needs escalation to management for resources.

Assign people and record completion

Once an action is chosen, someone has to carry it out and someone has to confirm it happened. This is where records are created, and where the difference between a completion record and a competence record matters most.

Assign clearly and confirm receipt

Each assignment needs a learner, an action, a responsible trainer or supervisor, and a due date. Learners should be able to see what they owe. The ICC page describes a workforce directory built by importing employees and an employee-facing area for learners; confirm in a demonstration what a learner can see there, such as outstanding and completed training. Whatever the system, test the learner's view as well as the supervisor's. Ask the supplier to show a learner viewing the assignment and completing it, then a supervisor recording completion or sign-off, and note any limitations, including whether the learner sees the due date and instructions.

For on-the-job practice, the assignment should name the person who will watch and sign. The ICC page describes supervisor sign-off of on-the-job training in the system and says certificates are issued on completion. Treat both as published descriptions to confirm in a demonstration.

Record what the completion shows

A completion record documents that an action took place. It does not, by itself, prove that the person can perform the task. The APG paper makes the point directly: training records can be verified to show a course was completed successfully, but participation alone does not necessarily provide evidence that the trainee is competent, because it may show that knowledge was gained and not that it is being applied effectively.

The paper also lists records an organization may keep, including identification of training needs, training plans and training records, and evaluation of effectiveness. In practice, each assignment should leave behind a short set of facts.

  1. Who was assigned, to what action, and by whom.
  2. When the action was completed, and who confirmed it.
  3. What evidence supports completion, such as a quiz result, certificate, or supervisor sign-off.
  4. What remains outstanding, in particular any practical assessment.

The fourth item prevents a common error. If a record says "training complete" and the practical assessment is not yet done, the person is awaiting assessment, not evidenced. Make the outstanding item visible on the record so that the next reader cannot miss it.

Illustrative example: In the hypothetical plant, the coach signs off that practice on the second bracket family was completed over two shifts. The record shows the sign-off, the coach's role, and the date, and it states that the practical assessment is outstanding. The alternate remains awaiting assessment, and the supervisor does not assign independent inspection of that family.

Keep the assignment record and the completion record connected. A reviewer who opens a person's history should be able to move from the original gap, through the chosen action, to the confirmation, and see the same task named at each step. If the system stores assignments in one place and sign-offs in another, ask the supplier how the two are linked, and test it with the fictional records from the evaluation. Disconnected records can make it hard to reconstruct why a person was allowed to work.

Handle disputed or incomplete sign-off

A sign-off can be disputed. A coach may believe the practice was sufficient while the process owner thinks it was too short. Decide in advance who resolves such disputes and what the person's status is meanwhile. A reasonable rule is that a disputed or incomplete sign-off is recorded as unresolved, with its owner, and independent assignment is withheld until the dispute is settled and the resolution is recorded. The person's status stays where the agreed evidence rules put it; the dispute does not redefine the state. In a demonstration, ask the supplier to show how a sign-off is withdrawn or corrected and whether the earlier entry remains visible. If the system cannot show this, the team needs a manual procedure and should write it into the evaluation results.

Key Consideration: Certificates and quiz results are useful evidence of learning. They answer the question "Did the person complete the learning?" not the question "Can the person do the work?" Keep the two questions apart in records and in conversation.

Handle overdue or unavailable learning

Plans slip. People are absent, sessions are canceled, and trainers are pulled onto production. A workflow that works only when every action is completed on time is fragile. This section defines what happens when an action is overdue or cannot be delivered.

Define a local escalation

Write a simple escalation ladder and agree who acts at each step.

TriggerFirst responseEscalation
Action not started or not finished by due dateSupervisor confirms the reason and reschedulesProcess owner informed if rescheduled twice
Trainer or coach unavailableSupervisor names an alternate coach from evidenced staffQuality lead decides whether an outside provider is needed
No evidenced replacement for the taskKeep the task with evidenced colleagues or hold the workManagement decides on staffing or scheduling
Learner repeatedly misses sessionsSupervisor discusses constraints with the learnerWorkflow owner reviews the plan

The ladder is a local design, not a description of any software behavior. The supplied evidence does not establish whether the software sends reminders or notifications, so do not infer them. During evaluation, ask how an overdue action would be found. Some teams rely on a weekly review of open assignments, and some rely on system alerts. Whichever applies, test how an overdue action appears to the supervisor, who is told, and what manual steps remain. Confirm whether notifications exist rather than assuming them.

Keep the ladder short enough that supervisors use it, and review unused steps for risk before removing any; keep routes for serious exceptions, such as a lack of evidenced replacements, even if they have not yet been needed.

Protect the staffing decision while learning is late

An overdue action must not quietly become permission to work. While learning is outstanding, the person's status should stay where the evidence puts it, and the supervisor should select an evidenced replacement. Record the revised plan with its new date and the exception owner who accepted it. This keeps the staffing decision honest and gives a reviewer a trail from delay to decision.

Illustrative example: A coaching session for the hypothetical alternate inspector is missed twice because of a production rush. The supervisor reschedules it for the following week, records the exception owner, and keeps the usual inspector assigned to first-article checks on the second bracket family. The alternate stays awaiting assessment and the revised plan is filed with the assignment.

Rescheduling should never be silent. When an action moves, record the reason and the new date, and tell the learner and the coach. A pattern of repeated rescheduling for the same task is itself information. It may show that the task is under-resourced, that the coach is overloaded, or that the plan was unrealistic, and the process owner should review it at the next matrix check rather than treat each delay as an isolated event.

Consider people outside the organization

The APG paper notes that people under the organization's control may include individuals at external providers, and that an organization may need to communicate its competence requirements to them where processes are outsourced. If a trainer, installer, or contract inspector works for another company, the same questions apply. What competence does the task need, how was that communicated, and what evidence will the plant hold that the person meets it? A purchase order or agreement can carry the requirement, and the plant should keep the evidence it needs rather than assume the provider holds it.

When the only available provider is external, add the provider's evidence to the record just as for an employee, and note the scope of the work it covers. If no suitable provider can be found at all, treat it as an unresolved exception for the process owner and management to decide.
