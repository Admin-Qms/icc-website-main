Training and competency management software: showing who is qualified for each task

How to choose and implement competency management software: map the workflow, compare capabilities, configure reviews, migrate records and train teams.

ISO Certification Consultants Editorial Team · 8 min read · 9 chapters

The operational problem

Managing training and competence in compliance management software can give a team one place to answer the question of who may do a task from current evidence instead of from memory, spreadsheets, and the habits of whoever is on shift, provided the team configures its own evidence rules and confirms what a given system actually does.

Illustrative example: On a production floor, a supervisor looks at the schedule, sees who is available, and decides who may run a task without help. The trouble appears later, when someone asks what the decision rested on. The training certificate is in one folder, the practical assessment is on a paper checklist, the qualification expiration date sits in a spreadsheet owned by a different person, and the supervisor's own knowledge of the operator was never written down.

That scatter is the problem competency management software is meant to address. The weakness is not necessarily a shortage of training. It is the link between training, assessment, qualification, and the assignment itself. When the link is informal, three things go wrong in predictable ways:

  • A completed course is treated as proof that a person can do the work, even though the course may have shown only that knowledge was gained.
  • An absence forces a quick substitution, and nobody can show afterward why the replacement was suitable.
  • Records exist but cannot be found, compared, or trusted when an auditor, a customer, or an incident review needs them.

The ISO 9001 Auditing Practices Group, a joint effort of ISO/TC 176 and the International Accreditation Forum, describes competence as the ability to apply knowledge and skills to achieve intended results. Its paper, Guidance on auditing Competence, was written for auditors working with ISO 9001:2015 clause 7.2. It is not an additional standard, and it does not describe the requirements of any later edition. This guide treats it as a clear statement of what auditors tend to examine, not as a source of obligations.

The paper is helpful because it keeps attention on people doing real work. It says an auditor may sample people in a process, observe their performance, interview them about the competence the task needs, and check that someone with adequate competence is available when the usual person is absent. It also says the existence of training plans and records is not sufficient on its own to show how an organization ensures competence. A system that stores certificates but cannot support the staffing decision has only digitized the folder.

Software cannot define competence for a team. The questions to put to any candidate system are whether it can hold the team's own definitions, such as task requirements, levels, evidence rules, and qualification conditions, and whether it applies them consistently when a decision is made. Ask whether it shows what is missing as plainly as what is present, and whether it routes a review to the right person and records who confirmed what and when. Treat each answer as something to see demonstrated and confirmed in writing before relying on it. Where a system offers AI assistance, treat it as a draft that a person reviews before it enters a controlled record, never as a decision.

How to use this guide

The guide has nine chapters and this introduction. The chapters follow the order in which a team typically works, from understanding the current decision to operating the new system. Each chapter has its own scope, so you can read the whole guide in sequence or go straight to the stage you are in.

Several terms recur, and they are used the same way throughout. A competency matrix relates people and their assessed levels to tasks, and the organization must define what each level means. A qualification is a recognized qualification where one applies, which is distinct from the broader ability to perform a task competently. Documented information is the evidence the organization retains, and the Auditing Practices Group paper says the organization decides what evidence of competence is appropriate. A results sheet records each claim made by a supplier, the test applied, what was observed, and the disposition. The chapters also use three local decision states for a person and a task: evidenced, awaiting assessment, and evidence missing. These are working labels a team can adopt or adapt. They are not terms from any standard.

The chapters run in this order:

  1. Map the qualification decision at the production cell. Write the staffing question in plain words, trace the evidence and handoffs, and define the decision states and the rules for accepting a record.
  2. Evaluate ICC against the actual decision. Turn published product descriptions into demonstration tests, check evidence, access, and human review, and separate commercial and technical unknowns from what has been shown.
  3. Define task requirements and the competency matrix. Describe the competence each task needs, define levels and qualification conditions, and assign ownership and change control for the matrix.
  4. Choose actions to close competence gaps. Match learning, coaching, or practice to the gap, assign people and record completion, and handle overdue or unavailable learning.
  5. Verify competence in practical work. Select an assessment method, separate completion from effectiveness, and respond when an assessment is unsuccessful.
  6. Maintain validity and retrieve evidence. Review qualification conditions, trigger reviews after change or over time, and retrieve evidence for a sampled person.
  7. Migrate records without creating false qualifications. Inventory and classify legacy evidence, map and test transfers, and approve cutover with a fallback.
  8. Pilot the workflow with supervisors and learners. Prepare role-specific tasks, test normal and exception paths, and decide whether to expand.
  9. Review performance and prepare the next decision. Review decision quality, answer common questions without overstating compliance, and prepare a focused demonstration.

Planning evaluation and implementation

Before reading further, decide who needs to be involved. A competency decision touches more people than most software purchases. The quality owner usually owns the definitions and the records. The operations owner owns the work and the staffing decisions. Supervisors and assessors are the people who will use the system under time pressure, and their view of whether it fits a normal shift is worth more than any demonstration script. IT will have questions about access and data export, and a training coordinator will know where the records really are.

Then agree on success in operational terms. One example to adapt is this: a supervisor can see, for a named person and task, whether the evidence supports independent work, what is missing, and who must act. A second example is this: a sampled person's evidence can be retrieved and explained without searching several systems. Both are examples to adapt, not targets from any source, and writing your own statements keeps the later evaluation honest.

A practical sequence for planning looks like this:

  1. Describe the current decision. Capture the roles, inputs, decisions, records, and exceptions for one cell, one task family, or one department. Start small enough that you can finish.
  2. List the questions a system must answer. Include the awkward cases: an assessment that has not happened, a qualification that has lapsed, an absent worker with no evidenced replacement.
  3. Define what counts as evidence. For each task and level, decide which records are enough. The Auditing Practices Group paper leaves this to the organization, so the decision is yours to make and to record.
  4. Test candidates against a realistic scenario. Use your own records, including incomplete ones, rather than a supplier's sample data.
  5. Plan the move. Decide which legacy records can be trusted, which need review, and which stay outside the system.
  6. Pilot before expanding. Run normal and exception paths with the supervisors and learners who will use the system, and record what the pilot showed.
  7. Review after go-live. Check the quality of the decisions the system supports, and use what you learn to decide what to change next.

When you evaluate software, separate three things that are easy to blur: what a supplier publishes, what a supplier demonstrates, and what a supplier confirms in writing. A published description tells you what the supplier says a product does. A demonstration tells you what you saw it do with your scenario. A written statement tells you what the supplier is prepared to stand behind. A capability that has only been described is an evaluation question, not a finding. The same discipline applies to this guide's own subject: nothing here promises that a particular product will behave in a particular way.

ICC publishes a description of its training and competency module as one option to examine when comparing products. As with any supplier, availability and configuration of a capability should be confirmed in a demonstration with your own scenarios before a team relies on it. The evaluation chapter shows how to prepare for that, and the final chapter closes with an invitation to request a software demonstration.

Finally, be realistic about what software does for an audit. A mapping of a module to clauses in ISO 9001:2015, ISO 13485:2016, or ISO/IEC 17025:2017 shows where its records may be relevant. It does not show that software alone satisfies a requirement, and it does not predict an audit result. Conformity depends on how your organization defines competence, acts on gaps, and keeps appropriate evidence.
