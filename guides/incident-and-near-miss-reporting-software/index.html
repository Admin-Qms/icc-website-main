Software guide

Incident and near-miss reporting software for Canadian quality and operations teams

How to choose and implement incident and near-miss reporting software: map the workflow, compare capabilities, rate severity, link actions and train teams.

ISO Certification Consultants Editorial Team · 8 min read · 9 chapters

The operational problem

Quality and operations teams are often asked a simple question: when something goes wrong, or nearly goes wrong, where is the record, who rated it, and what was done about it? The answer should take a minute. Where incidents and near misses are reported on paper or by email, it may take longer, because the information can sit in clipboards, inboxes and shift handover notes. Reports may then go unlogged, unrated for severity or unlinked to corrective action, so the plant should test whether its own log is complete.

In general, the gain a team seeks from managing this work in compliance management software is a single place where reports, ratings, investigations and actions are recorded, so that people can find them, trust them and see what remains open. That is a goal to test, not a promise. This guide explains how to test it with the plant's own reports, and it treats every product statement as a question for a demonstration.

The guide is written for quality and operations teams in Canadian manufacturing and service organizations who are considering incident and near-miss reporting software, or who already have a system and want to use it better. It treats ISO 45001 and other requirements as supporting context, not as the structure of the work. The main structure is the workflow: who reports, who reviews, who rates, who investigates, who acts and who checks.

Two sources shape the guide's factual statements about products and rules. The first is the published ICC Safety module page. It describes incident and near-miss reporting with severity set from the level of harm, an anonymous report form opened by a QR code on the floor, a health and safety dashboard with its own permissions, and incidents that need corrective action carried into the Improvements module. It also describes an auditor view and a mapping to three clauses of ISO 45001:2018. These are published descriptions. The guide does not treat any of them as a verified capability, and it asks the reader to confirm each one in a demonstration.

The second is the Government of Ontario's guide to health and safety committees and representatives. It describes how joint health and safety committees and representatives work in the Ontario workplaces it covers. Its rules are Ontario's, and the guide uses them as an example. Plants in other provinces, and federally regulated sites, need to confirm the rules that apply to them.

Several matters are not covered by the research behind this guide, and the guide treats them as open questions for a demonstration: pricing, notifications or alerts, injury classification beyond severity, regulatory or authority reporting, how existing logs could be imported, and how external reviewers would be given access.

Throughout, scenarios that begin with Illustrative example: are hypothetical and name no business. They show how a decision might look in practice and do not describe any customer or result. Where artificial intelligence is discussed, the published ICC page states that every AI output in the software is a suggestion a person reviews before it enters a controlled record, and the guide uses that as a design principle: a draft is not a decision.

Four ideas recur. A report is the start of a record, not a note. Severity is a judgment that should be recorded with its reasons, and changes to it should be visible. An investigation looks for conditions before it looks for people. An action is not finished when it is completed, but when someone has checked that it worked.

How to use this guide

The chapters follow a proposed sequence, but each can be read alone. A quality lead beginning an evaluation may start with the first two chapters. A coordinator designing the reporting form may go straight to the third. A team that already has a system may read the chapters on investigation, actions and committee visibility to see what to improve. Chapters generally describe roles, inputs, decisions, records and exceptions, with questions that can be used in a demonstration.

  1. Map how incidents and near misses are reported today. The first chapter describes how to define the reporting problem, trace one report with its handoffs, assign owners and backups, and write acceptance criteria before any vendor is contacted.
  2. Evaluate incident and near-miss reporting software. The second chapter explains how to build a workflow-based shortlist, test the decision points with real reports and exceptions, examine permissions, evidence and the use of AI, and compare setup, cost and support.
  3. Design reporting intake. The third chapter covers the report fields, anonymous floor reporting, access points on the floor and the first review of a new report.
  4. Rate severity and triage. The fourth chapter covers a harm-based scale, routing by rating, disputed ratings and the separation of regulatory duties from the internal log.
  5. Run investigations from a structured record. The fifth chapter covers assigning and scoping an investigation, collecting evidence, analyzing causes without blame and recording a reviewed conclusion.
  6. Link incidents to corrective action and follow-up. The sixth chapter covers the decision to act, owners and due dates, the link between an action and its incident, and checks of effectiveness.
  7. Give committees and workers visibility into incident data. The seventh chapter covers dashboards, regular pattern review, committee recommendations and worker participation, using Ontario's guide as an example of provincial rules.
  8. Prepare audit evidence and migrate existing incident logs. The eighth chapter covers what an auditor sees, how to map clauses without overclaiming, how to clean and import old logs and how to plan cutover and training.
  9. Review performance, answer common questions and prepare a demonstration. The ninth chapter covers periodic review, answers to common questions and preparation for a focused demonstration.

Chapter headings generally follow the work in the order it happens, and tables appear where they help a decision. Where a chapter touches on regulation, it separates what a source states from what a plant must confirm for itself.

Readers who need to make a quick start can use the guide in a different order. Begin with the definitions and the acceptance criteria from the first chapter, then use the second chapter's scoring approach in a first vendor conversation. Return to the remaining chapters as questions arise, such as how to handle an anonymous report or what an auditor will want to see.

Planning evaluation and implementation

An evaluation works best when it is planned like a small project. Name a lead, set a timeline and agree what decision the work will support. A short list of roles helps: an evaluation lead, a safety lead, a safety coordinator, an operations manager, a floor supervisor and, where one exists, a committee member. Not every plant will have each of these roles, and the guide uses the names to describe responsibilities that someone must hold.

Begin with the present state. Before any software is considered, trace how reports are actually made and what happens next, using real recent reports. The first chapter describes this in detail. The outcome is a map, a decision table and a set of acceptance criteria that tie the evaluation to the plant's own work.

Then run a structured evaluation. Use the acceptance criteria to shortlist, send the same sample reports to each vendor and score what is shown. Record the open questions that published descriptions do not answer, and ask for written responses. Keep a clear line between what was demonstrated, what was described and what was promised for later. Record the decision owner and the unresolved evaluation questions so the team can follow up with each vendor before choosing a system.

Decide the configuration before the switch. Reporting fields, severity levels, routing, permissions and the way anonymous reports are handled are design choices, and the plant should make them deliberately and write them down. The chapters on intake, severity and visibility set out the decisions and the records they should produce.

Plan the move. If existing logs will be imported, decide the scope, clean the data, run a pilot and check it. Plan cutover and training by role, and agree the signs of success and of trouble in advance. The eighth chapter covers these steps and the exceptions that should be prepared for.

Keep the work in proportion. A small plant may use short versions of many steps, such as one page for the definitions and a short routing note, while a large or multi-site operation may need more structure. The test is whether the records show who decided what, when and why, and whether the plant can find them quickly.

Finally, plan for review. A system that is not reviewed may drift, and the final chapter describes a simple quarterly sample with baselines and an exception log. A review is also the point at which the plant decides what to improve next and what to ask the vendor to change.

The guide makes no claim that software alone produces safer work or satisfies a standard. It offers a way to ask better questions, to test answers against real reports and to leave a clear record of what was decided and why.
