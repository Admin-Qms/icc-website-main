Map current processes: process mapping software for quality management

Map current processes in process mapping software for quality management: define the mapping purpose; identify process boundaries; trace inputs and outputs.

ISO Certification Consultants Editorial Team · 11 min read

Define the mapping purpose

Trace a single release boundary before choosing a display format. Start at the production order, stop at the release decision, and mark the separate complaint process as an interface. Ask the receiving role what inspection evidence it needs; a missing answer is a boundary question for the owner, not a reason to extend the map indefinitely.

The process owner is responsible for agreeing on the purpose with the people who use the process. The inputs are the current work, the records it produces, and the questions the organization needs answered. The owner decides which workflow to examine and records that choice with the map. If the people involved cannot agree on the purpose, record the disagreement and resolve it before treating the map as a basis for a software decision.

A map for day-to-day operation needs enough detail for a person to find the next step and know who receives the output. A map for an audit rehearsal needs a clear route from a process step to evidence that the step was performed. A map for software evaluation needs representative decisions and exceptions that a vendor can demonstrate. These uses overlap, but each asks a different question of the same process.

A reader evaluating the boundary needs an overview entry, a release step, and the applicable instruction. The ICC Processes page describes categorized maps, flowcharts, owners, and document links; use the sample to ask how those elements would be shown. Compare the proposed route with the plant's own evidence before deciding whether it describes current work.

For an audit rehearsal, ask which record would show what the supervisor considered at release. The ISO auditing guidance collection offers educational material on evidence collection. Give the reviewer the sample and the stated boundary; if the record does not answer the question, mark the gap and ask the process owner who can supply the missing explanation before the map is accepted.

Start with a small scope that can be traced end to end. A single production workflow can expose a missing owner or unclear handoff without requiring the team to map the entire business at once. Record what the map includes, what it excludes, and the decisions it is intended to support. If an adjacent process is important but outside scope, name the interface rather than silently treating the other process as part of the work. Ask the receiving role whether the stated output is sufficient to proceed.

Illustrative example: On a hypothetical production order, draw two marks: one where the order enters the workflow and one where the supervisor decides release. Place the complaint interface outside those marks. If inspection evidence arrives after release, the team must decide whether the boundary or the actual handoff needs clarification; neither conclusion follows from the diagram alone.

It can name the process, the owner, the intended readers, the start and finish, and the question the map must answer. If the team cannot explain why a step belongs on the map, ask whether it is a process step, supporting instruction, or evidence linked to a step.

Before contacting a vendor, turn the purpose into acceptance criteria. For example, ask whether a reviewer can open the top-level map, locate the selected process, identify its owner, and follow a link to the instruction used at a decision point. Include an exception, such as an instruction that is unavailable or awaiting revision. Record what was shown, what was described, and what remains to be confirmed.

Identify process boundaries

A process boundary is an agreement about where the mapped work starts and ends. It is not a claim that work outside the diagram is unimportant. A boundary helps the team identify the inputs it receives, the outputs it hands to another role, and the decisions for which its owner is responsible. Without it, a diagram can expand until no one can maintain it or shrink until the most difficult handoff disappears.

Ask the people doing the work to identify the event that starts it. That event may be a request, a schedule, a completed upstream task, or an exception requiring a decision. Then identify the output and the person or process that receives it. State each boundary in ordinary language and include the condition under which the output is accepted or returned.

Boundary questionEvidence to examineDecision to record
What starts the work?A representative request or scheduleThe event and its source
What leaves the process?A completed record or physical outputThe recipient and acceptance point
Who can decide?Current role assignments and actual handoffsThe owner and any delegated role
What falls outside scope?Adjacent workflows and interfacesThe named process that receives the issue

The table is a working aid, not a prescribed software configuration. Use a recent example to test each answer. If the written procedure says a supervisor accepts an output but actual records show that another role routinely does so, do not choose one account silently. Ask the owner to resolve the difference and record whether the process, instruction, or role assignment needs correction.

Boundaries can differ across sites or product lines. A shared map may provide a useful overview, but it should not erase a local decision that affects evidence or responsibility. The owner should decide whether a difference belongs on the common map, in a linked local instruction, or in a separate process. The record should explain that choice so a later reader does not mistake a deliberate variation for an omission.

A practical exception is work that crosses several departments without a clear receiving point. Trace one instance from its initial request to the next decision, noting where a person waits, asks for clarification, or creates a new record. The aim is to expose the interface, not to blame the role that happens to hold the item. If the evidence is incomplete, mark the boundary as unresolved and assign someone to confirm it.

Test the boundary with the intended readers. A floor supervisor should be able to explain what arrives and what leaves. A quality lead should be able to identify the records associated with the decision. A process owner should be able to say which exceptions must be escalated. Where their answers differ, retain the difference as an open item until the responsible people agree on an answer.

The vendor's top-level map description can be a useful demonstration starting point. Ask whether the organization can show its own boundaries and open a flowchart from an appropriate process. Ask who can change the grouping and how a reader knows which map is current. Record the answers as vendor statements or demonstrated behavior, not as a guarantee that every configuration is available.

Trace inputs and outputs

An input is more than an arrow on a page. It may be a document, an instruction, a physical item, a customer requirement, or a decision from another process. An output may likewise be a record, a product, a decision, or a request for further work. For each important input and output, record where it comes from, who checks it, and what makes it usable for the next step.

The process owner can assemble a sample with the people who create and use the records. Begin with a completed example, then trace backward to the information used and forward to the person who accepted the result. Where the process uses controlled documents, identify the instruction or form that guided the work and the record that shows the decision. A document title alone is not evidence that the correct version was available when the work occurred.

Separate an instruction from the record produced by following it. An instruction tells someone how work should be done; a completed record shows what was recorded for a particular instance. A map can point to both, but the reader should be able to tell which is which. If a record links to an instruction, ask how a later reviewer can identify the instruction relevant to that instance.

The ICC page describes controlled documents and standard clauses linked to processes. Ask the vendor to demonstrate a process-to-document link using a sample chosen by the plant. Then ask what happens when that document is revised, withdrawn, or temporarily unavailable. The published description is a starting point for the test; it does not establish the behavior of every revision or permission scenario.

For each handoff, record the sender, recipient, required information, and acceptance decision. A handoff is complete only when the receiving role has what it needs to act. If the output is incomplete, show how it returns for correction and who decides whether work can proceed. Keep the exception close to the normal path so that a diagram does not imply every item follows the ideal route.

A useful test is to follow one output back to its input without relying on the person who created it. Ask a colleague to find the triggering request, the relevant instruction, and the record of the decision. Note where the colleague needs an explanation that is absent from the map. That gap can become a requirement for the map or a question for a software demonstration.

Do not assume that an imported diagram carries all of its relationships. The vendor page describes review of supported PDF charts before acceptance, but the plant should ask what a sample import preserves and what must be checked or entered again. Keep the source chart available for comparison and have an owner verify a representative sample before accepting the result. Import capabilities and formats should be confirmed for the actual file in the demonstration.

Record handoffs and exceptions

A process map earns its place when it helps someone act on an ordinary day and when the ordinary path fails. Start by showing the normal sequence in enough detail to identify responsible roles, inputs, decisions, and outputs. Then add the exceptions that change who acts or what evidence is required. Avoid putting every instruction on the map; link to a controlled instruction when it supplies detail that would make the flow unreadable.

Ask each role what it does when an input is missing, a decision is rejected, or the next person is unavailable. Record the escalation route and the point at which the item may return to normal work. If the route is not agreed, mark it as an open decision and assign a resolution owner. A diagram that hides an unresolved exception can give a false impression of control.

For a software evaluation, prepare a normal case and an exception case using representative records with sensitive details removed. Ask the vendor to show how a reader finds the process owner, opens a linked instruction, and follows the handoff. Then ask how the map is updated when responsibility changes and how a reader distinguishes an approved map from a draft. Record whether each behavior was shown, described, or left unanswered.

Access also needs an explicit decision. The people who consult a map may not be the same people who edit it or approve changes. Ask who can view, propose, review, and release a change, and how those decisions are recorded. The public description of a named owner is not evidence of a particular permission model, so treat permission detail as an evaluation question until demonstrated.

Close the mapping exercise with a review of the actual workflow. The process owner, a person performing the work, and a person receiving its output should check the map against a recent instance. Record the date, the evidence examined, the disagreements found, and who will resolve them. A reviewer should be able to trace each unresolved item to a named owner and next action.

Before moving to software evaluation, give the bounded map and its decision record to someone who did not draw it. Ask that reader to identify the release decision, the recipient, and an unresolved interface. Record where the route needs explanation; those observations become demonstration criteria. The processes module overview provides a vendor starting point, while the sample record remains the basis for checking this particular map.
