Connect documents and evidence: process mapping software for quality management

Connect documents and evidence in process mapping software for quality management: identify controlled documents and link records to process steps.

ISO Certification Consultants Editorial Team · 11 min read

Identify controlled documents

A process map can help a reader find the information needed to perform or review work, but a link is useful only when its purpose and destination are understood. Begin with the process scope and ask which instructions or other controlled documents people actually need at the relevant steps. The process owner, quality lead, and workers can examine a representative process record together and distinguish a document that guides work from a record that shows what happened in one instance.

At a process step, first decide whether the reader needs an instruction, a blank form, or evidence of a completed instance. The ICC process management page describes links to controlled documents and clauses. Check the destination's scope with the responsible people; the published link description does not determine which document applies locally.

Make a list of candidate documents with their purpose and the process step each supports. The process owner can ask the operator which instruction is needed to carry out the task and ask the quality lead where the organization maintains the accepted version. If the answer depends on a product line or site, record that scope. A generic title may not be enough for a reader to choose the applicable document.

A candidate link should answer a clear question. It may help a reader find the instruction for a process step or identify the information required before a decision. Avoid adding a document merely because it mentions the same topic. Too many loosely related destinations can make the relevant one harder to find. The owner should decide whether the connection helps the intended reader and record why a disputed item was included or left open.

For the traceability test, ask whether the destination instructs someone how to perform the step or records what happened in a completed instance. The ISO auditing guidance collection provides educational material on evidence. If the destination does not answer the reader's question, record the mismatch and ask the process owner which information belongs at that connection.

Illustrative example: A hypothetical production map links to an instruction marked for an older product line. The current record refers to a different line. Ask the quality lead to identify the accepted destination and the process owner to decide whether the map's scope needs correction. Until that decision is made, the reader needs an agreed interim route, not a substitute link chosen by resemblance.

If a document is missing, inaccessible, or inconsistent with the work described by participants, record the exception and identify the person responsible for the next decision. Do not fill the gap with a similar document just to make the map look complete. The team may need to resolve scope, obtain access, or use its normal process for correcting information before accepting the connection.

Link records to process steps

A completed record can provide evidence about a particular instance of work. Before connecting one to a process step, decide what question the reader is trying to answer. A quality lead may need to understand what information informed a decision, while an operator may need to know which blank form to use. These are different uses and may call for different destinations or access arrangements.

The ICC page describes links to controlled documents. It does not confirm the exact mechanics of linking individual completed records to steps, nor does it establish access or retention behavior. Ask the supplier to demonstrate the proposed route using an appropriate sample, and record what was shown. Treat unsupported mechanics as evaluation questions rather than promises.

Start with a representative step and an example record that is appropriate to share for the review. The process owner identifies what the record is intended to show; the quality lead checks whether the example belongs to the selected process and scope. An operator can then explain whether the record contains the information needed for the next action. If the sample is incomplete, note the limitation rather than describing the connection as verified.

A useful association should help a reader understand the relationship between the step and the evidence. State whether the destination is an instruction, a blank form, a completed example, or another kind of record. A link to a form does not prove that the form was completed, and a completed record does not necessarily explain every decision made. The label and surrounding explanation should avoid implying more than the evidence supports.

Illustrative example: To examine a hypothetical decision record, the process owner selects a hypothetical record associated with a production decision. The operator explains which field was considered before passing the output on, and the quality lead records how that field relates to the step. If a second sample differs, the team notes the difference and decides whether further investigation is needed. The record should explain which field informed the decision and who can investigate a conflicting sample.

Consider who should be able to see the information and whether the example contains sensitive material. Use the organization's controls and a suitable test sample. Ask how a proposed configuration handles a reader who can see the process but cannot access the destination. Do not assume that a warning, alternate view, or notification exists; those are questions for the supplier and the organization to resolve.

The review record can capture the step, the sample, the intended reader, and the observed result. Distinguish a route demonstrated with the sample from an approach described in discussion. If the route relies on a manual lookup, record that fact. A decision-maker can then assess whether the proposed arrangement is sufficient for its intended use without mistaking a demonstration for proof of all future cases.

When a record is not available, identify the person who can determine the next action. The process owner may ask for another example or raise a question about the expected evidence. The absence of a sample does not necessarily show that the process failed, but it does limit what this review can establish. Keep that limitation visible until the responsible people decide how to address it.

Handle revisions and obsolete links

A connection that worked when a map was reviewed may later point to information that has changed or is no longer appropriate. Decide who will examine a process link when the destination is revised, withdrawn, or moved. The process owner and the person responsible for the document may be different roles, so the organization should agree how the change is brought to the attention of the person who can decide what the map should show.

The ICC process description mentions connections to controlled documents, but it does not establish exactly how every revision, withdrawal, or broken link is handled. Ask the supplier to demonstrate a representative change and the resulting reader experience. Record whether the answer was shown or only described; do not assume automatic updates or alerts.

Use a sample revision to test the decision. Identify the process step, the destination before the change, and the proposed destination afterward. The quality lead checks the organization's applicable document information, and the process owner decides whether the process description needs an update. If the revision changes the meaning of a decision or a role, consult the people who perform the work rather than treating the change as a simple link replacement.

An obsolete link is different from an inaccessible link. A reader may lack permission even though the destination still exists; a document may also have been replaced, moved, or withdrawn. Record what the reviewer actually observes and seek the information needed to identify the cause. The resolution should address that cause under local controls rather than assuming that every failure requires the same action.

Illustrative example: After a hypothetical revision, the quality lead examines a sample instruction after a proposed revision. The process owner compares the revised scope with the workflow step, and an operator tests whether the applicable information can be found. If the reader reaches an older copy or cannot open the destination, the team records the observation and assigns follow-up. Resolve the older destination against the accepted revision before allowing readers to rely on the route.

Decide how the organization will handle the period before the issue is resolved. A responsible person may identify an approved alternative route, restrict reliance on the draft, or escalate the question. The choice depends on the organization's own arrangements and the consequence of the missing information. Record the interim decision, its owner, and what must be checked before the issue is closed.

A useful change record connects the decision to the process and the affected destination. It should identify the sample or version examined, the person making the decision, and the outcome of any follow-up test. If access remains unavailable, retain that limitation rather than marking the route verified because the destination exists for a privileged user.

Ask a normal reader to repeat the route after the correction. The reviewer should be able to explain which process step led to the information and why the destination applies. A successful test of one route is evidence about that route and sample; it is not proof that every related link throughout the guide or organization remains current.

Test traceability during review

Traceability is useful when a reviewer can follow a process explanation to the information that supports or guides it and understand the limits of that connection. Select a representative process step, a relevant document, and a completed example where appropriate. The process owner explains the relationship, a worker follows the route, and the quality lead records what was observed and what remains uncertain.

Give the reviewer a specific question before opening a destination: does the linked item guide the step, or does it show an instance of the work? The ISO auditing guidance collection offers educational papers on evidence collection. Compare the answer with the selected sample, and record what remains unverified if access or the record is missing.

Begin at the process view and ask the reader to locate the information needed for a stated task. Then ask the owner why that information is relevant and which scope it covers. The reviewer should be able to distinguish the map's description from the linked instruction and from a record of a completed instance. If the route or relationship is unclear, note where the reader needed help and assign a person to decide how to clarify it.

The ICC page describes process-to-document and standard-clause links. This provides a basis for testing a proposed route in a demonstration, but it does not establish that a particular clause interpretation or document applies to a site. Verify applicability using the organization's own evidence and responsible people. A label or visible link is not a substitute for that decision.

Include an exception in the review. Ask what a reader should do when the destination is unavailable or the record does not match the map. The process owner should identify the next decision and the person who can make it. Record the observation and any interim route; do not assume the system automatically resolves a discrepancy or prevents an obsolete destination from being shown.

Illustrative example: For the hypothetical traceability check, an operator follows the hypothetical production step to a sample instruction while the quality lead examines a completed example. The process owner explains the scope and notes a question about another product line. The team records the evidence examined, the roles involved, and the open question. Check whether the second product line needs a separate instruction before accepting the traceability result.

Agree on acceptance criteria before the walkthrough. A practical criterion might be that the intended reader can find the identified information and explain why it is relevant without relying on an undocumented presenter explanation. Another might be that an unresolved access issue has an assigned owner and a recorded next action. These are choices for the organization, not a universal checklist supplied by the product.

The owner can accept the tested connection only for the scope and conditions the review supports. Record the sample, participants, and observations; identify what missing evidence prevented the test from establishing. When the document or step changes, use that decision to select the connection that needs another check.

Close the traceability review by recording the step, destination, intended reader, and test result. If a reader cannot open the instruction, distinguish an access question from an obsolete destination before choosing a remedy. Assign the open issue rather than substituting a similar file.
