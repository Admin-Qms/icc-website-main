Evaluate process mapping software: process mapping software for quality management

Evaluate process mapping software in process mapping software for quality management: set evaluation criteria; compare maps and flowcharts.

ISO Certification Consultants Editorial Team · 11 min read

Set evaluation criteria

A selection meeting needs a record that colleagues who missed the demonstration can inspect. Begin by naming the decision the quality and operations team must make. Select a representative workflow and identify the people who will use its map, inspect supporting records, or maintain it. Ask each person what information is needed to complete a real task. A process owner might need to understand an unresolved interface; an operator might need to find the current instruction for a production step. These are separate tests, even when the same map supports both.

The input to the evaluation should include a current map or draft, an example record, and a list of known exceptions. The process owner agrees on what the team will test and who will judge the result. The quality lead records the decision, including evidence observed and questions left open. If there is no agreed process owner, record that gap before treating a demonstration as a reliable indication of operational readiness.

Separate a requirement from a preference. A requirement describes an outcome the organization has decided it needs, such as finding an applicable instruction from a process view. A preference might describe how the link should appear. Both can matter, but the evaluation record should show which decision each item supports and who authorized it. That distinction gives the team a way to compare alternatives without assuming that every attractive screen addresses a real problem.

Use the ICC process management page to select an overview and a controlled-document link for a comparison. Give both presenters the same sample and ask the test reader to locate the applicable instruction. Record the number of prompts needed as an observation for that exercise, along with any access condition; the vendor's page does not confirm a site's configuration.

Agree on a small set of observable outcomes before the session. For each one, name a user, a starting point, and an output the user should be able to find or verify. Include an exception: a missing document, an unclear owner, or a record that belongs to another process. If the demonstration cannot cover that exception, record it as open rather than marking the criterion satisfied by inference. A practical evaluation can use the following prompts:

  • Can a reader locate the relevant process from a starting view without relying on a presenter to explain the route?
  • Can the team identify the owner shown for the example and determine whom to contact when that owner is unavailable?
  • Can a reviewer trace the example to its supporting instruction and distinguish the information shown from information that still needs confirmation?
  • Can the team document what was not demonstrated, along with a person responsible for following up?

Compare the evidence available for each proposed route, using the same sample and reader role. Educational material on evidence collection is available from the ISO auditing guidance collection. Record whether the presenter showed the relevant destination or only described it, then ask the decision-maker whether an unanswered access question prevents a fair comparison of the two approaches.

Illustrative example: Compare two hypothetical views of the same incomplete production record. In the first, the receiving role is visible from the overview; in the second, the reader must open a separate detail. The process owner states the expected answer beforehand and the quality lead records the route, including any coaching. Decide which representation supports the agreed task rather than treating appearance as the result.

Compare maps and flowcharts

A comparison should test whether a person can move from the overview to enough detail for the decision at hand. Begin with a representative process, rather than asking the presenter to choose only a polished example. The team can ask where the process sits in the overall view, how a reader identifies its scope, and how the detailed flow is found. Record the route actually shown and any explanation that was necessary to make the route understandable.

The ICC description presents a level-one process map and a level-two flowchart for each process. This gives an evaluator a concrete starting point for a demonstration. It does not answer every question about navigation, permissions, versions, or a site's own hierarchy. Ask those questions directly and record the response as a statement or observation, not as an assumed capability.

Use the same sample and task for each approach being compared. Give the reader a question such as where a completed production record should go after a decision point, and let the reader explain the route. The quality lead observes whether the labels and connections support the answer. If the route relies on context that appears only in the presenter's explanation, record that dependency. It may be addressed through map design, training, configuration, or another decision; the demonstration alone does not establish which remedy is available.

The process owner should also consider the amount of detail. An overview that shows every decision may be difficult to scan, while a flowchart that omits the meaningful exception may leave a reader with no usable next step. Rather than score visual complexity in isolation, ask whether the chosen level answers the test question and whether the next level is accessible to the intended user. The result can be recorded as a decision about the example, with a separate question for any other workflow that differs materially.

Compare the way the sample represents interfaces as well as activities. A reader should be able to tell what output is passed and which role receives it, or see that the information is not yet agreed. Ask how the team would recognize an unresolved interface during preparation of a map. Do not treat a diagram as evidence that the actual handoff works; the operational team still needs to examine its own records and practice.

Illustrative example: For a hypothetical side-by-side test, the operator can be given the same starting point in each proposed presentation and asked to locate the next recipient of an output. The process owner compares the answer with the agreed sample, and the quality lead records discrepancies. A disagreement about the sample itself is not a software failure: resolve the sample with the people responsible for the work, then decide whether another demonstration is needed.

Test ownership and access

Ownership and access answer different questions. The first concerns who is accountable for a process decision; the second concerns what a person can see or do in the proposed environment. A name displayed beside a process can help a reader find a contact, but it does not by itself prove that the person has accepted responsibility or that access is configured appropriately. Test each question with the people who would use the information.

The ICC page describes named process owners. Ask to see how the sample owner appears and how a reader reaches the relevant information. Then ask how an organization would record a change of owner and what a user sees when the designated person is absent. These are questions for demonstration; the published description does not establish the exact permissions, notification behavior, or change procedure for an installation.

Prepare role-based scenarios using the agreed sample. An operator may need to read an instruction, a process owner may need to propose a correction, and a quality lead may need to examine the supporting record. Ask the supplier to demonstrate the proposed access for each role, using an appropriate test environment and non-sensitive material. Record the identity of the role tested, the action attempted, and what the observer actually saw. Avoid using a privileged presenter's account as the sole evidence of what ordinary readers can do.

Include an exception in the test. For example, ask what happens when a person cannot see a linked item, or when responsibility for the sample process has not been confirmed. If the team cannot test the situation during the session, list the unanswered question and assign follow-up. An unavailable item might reflect access, a missing link, an incomplete sample, or another cause. The evaluation should not assign a cause until it has evidence.

An access demonstration should also distinguish viewing a map from changing it. Ask who can propose a change, who can review it, and how a reader identifies the applicable information. Do not assume that a proposed workflow, automatic notification, or approval mechanism exists. If a supplier describes an arrangement, ask which parts are standard, which depend on configuration, and which require further confirmation. Record those qualifications alongside the answer.

Illustrative example: Consider the hypothetical access test: the process owner can select a sample instruction that the operator is authorized to use. The operator tries to reach it from the process view, while the quality lead notes whether the route was demonstrated and what information was unavailable. If a second role must verify a proposed change, the team asks to see that separate action. Verify that each proposed view is tested with the same role and starting point before comparing access.

Document gaps and commitments

A comparison is useful only if its conclusions can be revisited. Keep a record for each criterion that identifies the question, the sample, the observer, and the result. Attach or reference an appropriate evidence record under the organization's own controls. Distinguish a demonstrated outcome from a supplier statement, an internal assumption, and a question that remains unanswered. The person making the selection can then see the limits of the evidence rather than treating a checklist as a guarantee.

The ICC process page describes configuration activities and onboarding in broad terms. Ask how the proposed implementation would address the team's open questions, but do not convert the published description into a mandatory sequence or a promise about a particular deployment. Record any commitment with its scope, responsible party, and the conditions under which it would be confirmed. A verbal explanation can be useful input, but the team should label it accurately and seek the evidence needed for its decision.

A gap need not be a reason to reject an option immediately. First decide what consequence it has for the intended use. A missing demonstration of a routine reader's access may require another session; uncertainty about a rarely used exception may require an agreed temporary procedure. In either case, name the person who can accept the risk or request further evidence. Do not mark an item closed simply because the team has assigned someone to investigate it.

Keep commercial and operational decisions separate. A supplier may offer a configuration discussion or a proposed implementation step, while the organization must determine whether the result meets its own need. Record what was promised, what was shown, and what remains dependent on later design. If a commitment changes, retain enough context to understand which criterion it affected and who needs to reconsider the decision.

When evidence conflicts, preserve both observations and identify the next action. A presenter may describe a route that differs from what a test user observed. Record the test conditions, ask for clarification, and avoid choosing the more favorable statement without explanation. If the sample was incomplete, note that limitation. If the answer is still unknown, the decision remains open until the authorized person decides how to proceed.

Illustrative example: At the hypothetical comparison meeting, the quality lead compiles the demonstration record and reviews it with the process owner and operations manager. The record shows which sample tasks were completed, which questions were answered only in discussion, and which exceptions still need testing. The owner then decides whether the available evidence supports a selection or whether further information is required. That decision belongs to the organization, not to the demonstration itself.

Before comparing the answers, check whether both presenters used the same sample and whether the test reader had the intended access. If the conditions differed, record that limitation alongside the result.

After the demonstrations, give the decision-maker the sample, observations, open access questions, and any configuration dependencies in one record. If an essential exception was not shown, identify who will obtain that answer before selection. The process management information is a source for the vendor's published approach; the comparison record explains which claims the team actually tested.
