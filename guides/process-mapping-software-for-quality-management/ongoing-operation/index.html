Maintain process maps and improve: process mapping software for quality management

Maintain process maps and improve in process mapping software for quality management: review maps after change and measure the review workflow.

ISO Certification Consultants Editorial Team · 11 min read

Review maps after change

A process map remains useful only if the organization knows when its explanation needs another look. A change in a role, an instruction, a process boundary, or an interface can affect what the map tells a reader. The process owner should decide which changes require review, who needs to contribute, and how an unresolved question will be recorded. The goal is not to redraw the entire architecture after every event; it is to examine the part of the map whose meaning may have changed.

When an instruction changes, identify the affected step and decide whether the recipient or decision also changes. The ICC process management page describes owners, flowcharts, and document links. The organization should agree how the change reaches the responsible owner; automatic detection has not been established.

Start with the change and a representative process record. The process owner can ask the people who perform the work what is different, while the quality lead identifies the current map and related information. Compare the agreed scope, decision points, and handoffs with the changed work. If the change affects another process, include the person who can explain that interface. Record the decision and its owner, including any question that cannot yet be resolved.

A proposed change and an accepted change are different inputs. If an instruction is still under consideration, the team may need to evaluate its potential effect without presenting it as the current route. If the work has changed but the map has not, record the discrepancy and identify the next decision. The owner should not silently choose between conflicting accounts when the people responsible have not agreed which description applies.

When reviewing a changed handoff, identify the record showing the change and the person who accepted the revised explanation. The ISO auditing guidance collection includes educational material on improvement. If either is missing, assign an owner for the gap and retain an interim route; updating the diagram alone does not answer who agreed to the change.

Illustrative example: Triage a hypothetical production change in three entries: the receiving role needs a different field; the existing map names the old field; and the updated instruction is not yet accepted. The owner assigns a check to the receiving role and records an interim route. Once the instruction is accepted, a worker traces the changed handoff before the issue is closed.

If the relevant record is unavailable or ownership of the decision is disputed, document the limitation and the person responsible for resolving it. Agree on any interim instruction through the organization's normal controls. A map that still looks complete can conceal an open question, so make the status visible to the people who rely on it. Once a change is accepted, ask an affected reader to follow the revised route and explain what happens next.

Measure the review workflow

A review process can be assessed by examining the decisions and evidence it produces. The process owner and quality lead can select a set of review records and ask whether each identifies the change, the affected map, the people consulted, the decision, and any outstanding action. This is a practical way to find missing information; it does not require a claim that the organization has achieved a particular improvement.

The ICC page describes process maps and owners, but it does not establish that a specific installation automatically measures review performance or reports every overdue action. Ask what information is available and determine what the organization must record or examine through its own arrangements. Treat a supplier's explanation as a statement until the relevant behavior has been demonstrated.

Agree on a question before choosing a measure. A team may want to know whether decisions are assigned, whether representative users can follow an accepted map, or whether unresolved discrepancies remain visible. Define what counts in the sample and who will interpret the result. A count of open items, for example, is meaningful only when readers understand which items were included and what "open" means in that record.

Use the measure to support a decision, not as a substitute for examining the underlying work. A short review interval may hide a rushed decision; an older open question may be waiting for necessary evidence. Ask the responsible person what the record shows and what it does not show. Where the sample is incomplete, record that limitation instead of extrapolating to the whole organization.

Illustrative example: To inspect hypothetical change records, the quality lead reviews a hypothetical set of map-change records with the process owner. They check whether each identifies a decision-maker and whether an unresolved handoff has a next action. One record lacks the evidence used to accept a change, so the owner assigns follow-up. The missing acceptance evidence needs a named follow-up before that review record can be closed.

When feedback indicates a recurring problem, examine representative examples before choosing a remedy. A missing decision owner might reflect an unclear process boundary, an incomplete record, or an unavailable person. The team should decide which explanation is supported and record the action and owner. If the evidence does not distinguish the causes, obtain more information rather than assigning blame or changing the map on an assumption.

When the process changes, ask whether each selected measure still informs a decision. The owner should use the defined scope, sample, and observations to decide whether to retain or replace the question. Record uncertainty in that decision; a score that cannot guide follow-up is not a useful result for the next review.

Frequently asked questions

What should a process map show first?

Begin with the purpose and the agreed boundary: where the selected process starts, what output it produces, and who receives it. Use a representative record and ask the process owner and a worker whether the explanation supports the reader's task. Record a disputed boundary as open and assign a person to resolve it; do not let a polished diagram imply agreement that has not been reached.

Who owns a process?

The organization assigns a role accountable for decisions and review of the defined process. Describe the scope, decisions, input needed, and limits of authority, then obtain the appropriate acceptance. A displayed name is useful for contact but does not prove that responsibility has been agreed. Record a backup or escalation route where the organization needs one.

How should process interfaces be recorded?

Identify the output passed, the receiving role, and the information needed for the recipient to act. Ask both sides to examine a representative example. If their expectations differ, record both views and the person who will decide the next action. The map can show the relationship without reproducing every instruction used by either process.

Which documents should be linked?

Choose information that helps an intended reader perform or review a defined step, and confirm its scope and relevance with the responsible people. Distinguish an instruction, a blank form, and a completed record. The ICC page describes links to controlled documents; the organization still needs to verify which destination applies and whether the intended reader can reach it.

How should existing maps be migrated?

Inventory candidate maps, identify their scope and owners, and select a representative sample for a pilot. Compare the tested result with the agreed source, including meaningful relationships and a reader's ability to follow the route. The ICC page describes review of supported PDF process charts before acceptance; confirm the formats and procedure for the proposed implementation rather than assuming that every file or link transfers automatically.

How should changes trigger review?

Agree which events, such as a changed role, instruction, boundary, or handoff, should bring the map back to the process owner. Record the change, the affected scope, and the person who decides the next action. If the evidence is incomplete, keep that limitation visible and use the organization's agreed interim arrangement. Do not assume that a tool detects every relevant change.

What should an auditor see?

Provide an understandable process explanation and access to the relevant evidence under the organization's arrangements. A reviewer should be able to distinguish the map, an instruction, and a record of an instance of work. The ISO auditing guidance collection offers educational papers on evidence; select a sample and ask the process owner to explain how each item relates to the decision being examined.

Does software guarantee certification?

No. A software view can help people organize or find process information, but a link or map alone does not establish that work meets applicable requirements. Evaluate the organization's processes and evidence, clarify responsibility for decisions, and use the relevant certification and audit arrangements. The vendor's published descriptions are not a guarantee of an outcome for an individual site.

What should a demonstration show?

Use an appropriate sample and ask the presenter to show the route a representative reader needs: find the process, open relevant detail, and identify the next action when information is missing. Test a role and exception that matter to the organization. Record what was shown, what was described, and what remains unanswered before making a selection decision.

Prepare a software demonstration

Start with an issue discovered after people began using a process map. A changed handoff, a disputed owner, or an instruction that a reader could not reach gives the discussion a concrete purpose. The process owner can choose the issue with the greatest effect on the next decision and bring the current map and a suitable sample. If the issue is still disputed, describe the competing interpretations rather than presenting either one as settled.

The ICC process management description offers a published reference for discussing process information. Ask how the proposed arrangement would help the team examine the selected change, without assuming that a particular reminder, permission, or automatic update exists. If the question cannot be shown in the session, identify what additional information is needed before the organization relies on an answer.

A focused agenda can follow the lifecycle of the issue. First, show the point where the current explanation became uncertain. Next, ask who should assess the effect on the connected process and its readers. Finally, consider how the organization would confirm that the revised explanation reaches the people who use it. These are organizational decisions to discuss; the demonstration is an opportunity to explore the proposed support, not evidence that every step is automatic.

Illustrative example: A hypothetical production handoff changes when the receiving role needs a different field on a record. The existing map names the earlier field, and the revised instruction is awaiting acceptance. The process owner brings that unresolved condition to the discussion and asks how a reader could identify the explanation that applies. A worker then describes what would be needed to check the updated route once the responsible people agree on it.

Prioritize the open condition rather than trying to cover every possible feature. If the immediate risk is that readers may follow an outdated instruction, ask what they would see while the change is pending and where the agreed interim route would be communicated. If the issue is an unclear decision owner, ask how the team would identify the person responsible for resolving it. Keep each answer within the scope actually discussed.

Consider the follow-up that will be useful after the session. The process owner may need to consult the receiving role, verify an instruction's scope, or bring a disputed assignment to the operations manager. A proposed product response may depend on a later configuration decision; if so, the organization should identify who will confirm it before the route is relied upon. An answer left open is a next action, not evidence that a capability exists.

The quality lead can bring the change-review record so participants understand what prompted the discussion and which decision remains outstanding. Avoid sharing sensitive production details when an appropriate sample will serve. If a supplier needs further information to respond, agree on a safe way to provide it and identify who will check the answer against the actual process. The team can then decide whether another focused discussion is needed.

The purpose of the demonstration is to help the team decide whether the proposed approach supports its work, including maintenance after change. Bring the map, the relevant roles, and a representative exception to the discussion, then keep the resulting evidence with the organization's evaluation record. To explore the published approach with a representative sample, book a demo.
