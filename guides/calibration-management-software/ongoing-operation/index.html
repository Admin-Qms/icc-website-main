Review performance, answer common questions and prepare a demonstration

Maintaining calibration management software: review the workflow and evidence, answer common questions and prepare a demonstration for next steps.

ISO Certification Consultants Editorial Team · 11 min read

Review the workflow and evidence

A calibration program does not finish when the register goes live. It continues as a set of routines, and the quality lead's job is to check that those routines still work and that the evidence they leave is complete. This section describes a simple review rhythm that fits a small team, and explains how to prioritize improvements without claiming results the team has not measured.

Set a rhythm and keep it. A monthly look at overdue items and a quarterly sample of records are workable starting points, and the team can change it if experience shows a different pace is needed. At each review, the quality lead works from three inputs: a sample of instrument records, the exception log and the list of overdue or recently extended items. The aim is to answer two questions. Is the evidence on each sampled record complete? And are the exceptions telling the team something about the program?

Sample records on purpose. Pick instruments from different classes, including at least one that has had an exception and one that has been recently maintained. For each, check that the certificate is attached and reviewed, the interval and owner are current, the status is correct and any maintenance or inspection events are recorded with signatures where required. Record what was checked and what was found. If the evidence is too incomplete to assess, treat that as a finding in its own right and note which records could not be assessed and why.

The published ICC calibration and maintenance page describes an auditor view with the equipment register, calibration certificates, completed inspection records and maintenance history. The same set of items makes a sensible basis for the internal review, so the team looks at its records the way an auditor would. The team should ask what views a system provides for review or plan to review from the register directly.

The ISO 9001 Auditing Practices Group page, published by ISO/TC 176 and the International Accreditation Forum, describes its papers as non-definitive. An August 2024 item on the page says resources are a key element of a quality management system and that auditors should consider how the resource requirements in clause 7.1 relate to other requirements. For the quality lead, the practical lesson is to review the calibration program in the context of the wider system: how measuring equipment connects to training, suppliers, corrective action and the processes it supports.

Prioritize improvements by looking at patterns. A cluster of late calibrations in one area, repeated exceptions on one class or recurring gaps in certificates point to a cause. Pick the one or two issues with the greatest effect on the work and assign an owner and a date. A short list that is completed is more useful than a long one that is not.

Set a baseline before comparing anything. If the team wants to know whether retrieval is faster, how often instruments become overdue or how long certificates wait for review, it needs a starting measurement taken the same way each time. Write down the measure, how it is collected and the starting value. Without that, any claim that a change helped is an impression. The team should also avoid quoting savings or returns it has not measured.

Involve the instrument owners in the review. Share the findings that concern their instruments, ask for their view of the cause and agree the action with them. Sharing results may help owners keep records current between reviews, and the conversation may surface practical obstacles that the register alone does not show.

Keep the review record short. A page for each review, listing what was sampled, what was found, what was decided and who owns each action, is enough. File it where the next reviewer can find it, and begin the next review by checking whether the last actions were completed.

Illustrative example: At an unnamed Canadian manufacturer, the quality lead samples ten instruments each quarter. In one review, two certificates are attached but show no reviewer decision, and one instrument has an inspection with a single signature. The lead records the findings, asks the coordinator to complete the reviews and records that two of ten sampled certificates lack a reviewer decision as a starting count, to be measured the same way next quarter. All situations in this chapter are hypothetical, and none describes a customer result.

Answer common questions without overstating compliance

Teams evaluating calibration management software ask a recurring set of questions. The answers below stay within the sources used in this guide and avoid promises about standards or outcomes. Answers marked with a source link rest on that source; the rest are internal recommendations. The quality lead approves the answers, keeps them with the evaluation records and defers any question that needs interpretation of a standard to a qualified specialist.

What problem should calibration management software solve first?

This is an internal recommendation. Start with the problem the team can describe from its own workflow map. A sensible first problem is finding, in one place, which instruments are in use, which are in date and where the certificates are. Write the problem as a testable statement, such as being able to retrieve any instrument's status and latest certificate without asking another person, and judge candidates by whether they can show it.

Which records should be migrated into the register?

The ICC page says the register can be loaded in bulk from Excel but gives no import detail. As an internal recommendation, migrate the records for instruments in current use first, with their certificates and intervals. Classify retired instruments and unresolved items separately, as described in the migration chapter, and keep the old sources read-only. Do not invent certificates for instruments that lack them. Flag those instruments as lacking evidence and resolve them deliberately.

How should calibration intervals be set and changed?

As an internal recommendation, set a default for each instrument class from the sources the team trusts, such as manufacturer guidance, supplier advice and the team's own history of results. Record the reason beside the interval, and name who may change it. When results suggest a change, make the change through that owner and record the date and reason. The register should keep the earlier value.

What does traceability mean for a calibration certificate?

The National Institute of Standards and Technology adopts the international vocabulary definition: traceability is the property of a measurement result whereby it can be related to a reference through a documented unbroken chain of calibrations, each contributing to the measurement uncertainty. A reviewer therefore looks for the documented chain and, under the team's review rule, the stated uncertainty, not only for the word traceable.

Does a calibration certificate alone make a measurement traceable?

No. NIST states that traceability is a property of a measurement result, not of an instrument, a report or a laboratory, and that merely having an instrument calibrated is not enough. The provider of a result supports the claim, and the user assesses it. Accreditation of a laboratory shows competence but does not certify the traceability of a specific result.

What should happen when an instrument is found out of tolerance?

As an internal recommendation, remove it from service and tag it, record the exception and identify the period of doubt from the last good calibration. The quality lead then assesses the effect on affected work against that work's acceptance criteria and records the decision. Close the exception only when the evidence for the repaired or replacement instrument is reviewed and the status is decided.

How should due dates and overdue instruments be handled?

Define status values and the lead time before a due date, and name who is told at each stage. Treat an overdue instrument as not to be used for acceptance decisions until the quality lead decides. Record any extension as a decision with an approver, a reason and an end date. The ICC page does not state due-date alerts or reminders, so ask any vendor what notification exists.

What evidence should be ready for an auditor?

The ICC calibration and maintenance page lists an auditor view with the register and next due date, certificates, completed inspection records and maintenance history. As an internal recommendation, also be ready to show any exception and how it was closed. Practice a retrieval with a colleague, set a target time in minutes and log any record that cannot be found.

Does software guarantee ISO certification?

No. The published ICC page names ISO 9001:2015 clauses 7.1.5 and 7.1.3, ISO 13485:2016 clause 7.6 and ISO/IEC 17025:2017 clause 6.4 as the clauses the module is mapped to. Such a mapping does not mean software alone satisfies a clause. The team and its quality lead remain responsible for judging whether their own system meets the requirements.

What should be prepared for a software demonstration?

Bring a real instrument with its certificate, one instrument with a missing certificate and one overdue instrument. Prepare written questions on alerts, import, pricing, access and support, and a scoring sheet. The final section of this chapter sets out the preparation in more detail.

Prepare a focused ICC demonstration

A demonstration is most useful when the team arrives with its own material and its own questions. The evaluation lead runs this step, using the acceptance criteria from the first chapter and the scoring sheet from the second.

Bring three records. The first is a real instrument with a complete certificate, so the team can see the normal path. The second is an instrument with a missing certificate, so the team can see what the register shows when evidence is absent. The third is an overdue instrument, so the team can see what a user is told and what happens next. Ask the presenter to work with these records and not only with sample data.

The published ICC calibration and maintenance page describes the equipment register, calibration records with attached certificates and next due dates, maintenance recorded against the same equipment record, inspection checklists with e-signatures, QR labels, PIN access and an auditor view. Ask to see each of these working with the team's records, and note where a description is demonstrated, where it is only described and where it is unavailable.

Request written answers to the questions that the page leaves open. These include whether due-date alerts or reminders exist and how they work, how the Excel import handles identifiers and errors, what pricing applies, how access and attribution work for PIN users and what support and setup are included. A written answer can be compared with the others and checked later. If an answer is given verbally, ask the vendor to confirm it in writing.

Include the people who will use the system in the session. A reviewer, an owner and an operator each see different things, and a coordinator may not notice what slows them down. Give each a task to attempt during the demonstration, and ask them to say what they would need to see to trust the result.

Define the outcome in advance. After the demonstration, the team decides whether to proceed, seek clarification or reject. Write the criteria for each choice before the session, so the decision follows the evidence. If a critical path cannot be shown, such as a missing certificate or an overdue instrument, record that as a limit and decide whether it is acceptable and whether a follow-up session can address it.

Capture the results while they are fresh. Within a day, circulate notes with what was shown, what was described, what remains open and who owes which answer. Ask the vendor to correct any statement that misrepresents what was shown.

Illustrative example: The unnamed manufacturer's evaluation lead asks to see an overdue instrument and a missing certificate. The presenter shows the overdue status on the register but cannot show a notification, and the team records alerts as an open question with a written follow-up due in a week. The missing certificate case is shown, and the team scores it as demonstrated.

To see the calibration and equipment maintenance workflow run against your own instruments, certificates and exceptions, book a demo and bring the three records described above. A demonstration with your own material is the clearest way to judge whether the workflow fits how your team works.
