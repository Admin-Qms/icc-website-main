## Select an assessment method

Learning ends with a completion record. Verification begins when someone checks that the person can do the work. The Auditing Practices Group, in its [guidance on auditing competence](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-Competence.pdf), names evaluation techniques an organization may use, including role-play, peer review, observation, review of training and employment records, and interviews. The paper was written for auditors working with ISO 9001:2015 clause 7.2, and it describes options rather than a required method.

For a production task, observation of real or representative work is usually the strongest option because it shows the person applying knowledge and skill to achieve the intended result. Other methods have a place. An interview can reveal whether a person understands when to escalate. Peer review can test a judgment call. A review of records can confirm prior experience. The choice depends on the task, and the process owner should record the reasoning.

### Choose the method from the task requirement

Return to the task definition from the matrix chapter and ask what evidence would convince a careful reviewer that the intended result is achieved. Then choose the least burdensome method that provides that evidence.

| Task characteristic | Method that often fits | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Hands-on, repeatable operation | Observation at the cell against written criteria | Shows the result directly |
| Judgment under unusual conditions | Role-play or scenario discussion | The condition may not occur on demand |
| Decisions reviewed by others | Peer review of completed work | Uses an existing control |
| Prior experience claimed | Review of employment and training records plus interview | Confirms background |
| Knowledge element only | Interview or quiz, combined with observation | Knowledge alone does not show application |

Write the acceptance criteria before the assessment, not after. An assessor who decides afterward what counted as good enough cannot be checked by a reviewer. The criteria should reference the current work instruction, so that the person is judged against the method in use.

Write down, too, who may waive an assessment condition and when; the default answer is that no one may do so without a recorded reason. Keep the assessment proportionate. A short observation of a routine task may take twenty minutes, while a rarely performed task may need a scenario prepared in advance. The aim is enough evidence to support the decision, not a formal examination for every task, and the process owner should say how much is enough before assessments begin. Review a sample of completed assessments each quarter to check that criteria were applied consistently. Where several assessors will apply the same criteria, a brief calibration session helps. Have two assessors observe the same demonstration and compare their conclusions; if they disagree, investigate why; unclear criteria are one possible cause.

### Name the assessor and the conditions

An assessor should be designated by the organization as competent to judge the task and should not be assessing their own work. Record the assessor's name or another unambiguous identifier and role, the date, the method used, the criteria applied and the outcome. If the task cannot be observed because it is rare, or no authorized assessor is available, treat that as an exception. The process owner then decides whether a simulation, a delay, or another route is acceptable, and records the decision.

**Illustrative example:** An unnamed Canadian manufacturer of machined components has the quality lead observe the alternate inspector inspecting representative output from the second bracket family against the approved criteria. The criteria were written beforehand and reference the current inspection instruction. The assessor records the method, the criteria, the date and the outcome, and notes that the observation covered one shift.

Be careful about who assesses whom. A supervisor who needs the person to be available for the next shift may feel pressure to pass them. Where that pressure is likely, involve a second assessor from quality or a peer from another shift, and record both names. The extra step adds effort that varies with staffing, but it can protect the integrity of the record.

### Test what the system lets an assessor see and keep

During evaluation, test whether assessors can open the criteria at the point of assessment and preserve enough information to explain the outcome later. The [ICC training and competency module page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/training-competence/) describes supervisor sign-off of on-the-job training and quiz results with pass scores. It does not, in the supplied evidence, describe how assessment criteria or observation notes are stored. Ask the supplier to show where an assessor would record the method and criteria, and confirm what a reviewer sees afterward. Verify product support in a demonstration rather than assuming it.

## Separate completion from effectiveness

Completion and effectiveness answer different questions. Completion answers "did the person finish the action?" Effectiveness answers "did the action produce the competence the task needs?" The [APG paper](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-Competence.pdf) states that training records can show a course was completed successfully, but that participation alone does not necessarily provide evidence that the trainee is competent, because it may show only that knowledge was gained and not that it is applied effectively. It also says the existence of training plans and records is not sufficient, on its own, to show how an organization ensures competence.

### Treat certificates and quiz scores as one kind of evidence

The ICC page describes a training library with quizzes and pass scores, and certificates issued on completion. These are useful completion evidence. A quiz can show that a person recalled a procedure on the day. A certificate can show that the learning was completed on a date. Whether either is adequate for a given task is a matter of organizational judgment, and the quality reviewer should decide that when defining the task's evidence rules.

A practical way to apply the rule is to write, for each task, which evidence types are required and which are supporting.

| Evidence | Role for a hands-on inspection task | Sufficient alone? |
|---|---|---|
| Completion certificate | Supporting: shows learning finished | No |
| Quiz result with pass score | Supporting: shows knowledge at a point in time | No |
| Supervisor sign-off of practice | Supporting: shows practice took place | Not for independent work |
| Practical assessment against criteria | Required: shows application | Not alone; it supports the decision with the other locally required evidence |

The table is an example of local rules and not a description of any product setting. A task with low consequence might accept less; a task with high consequence might require a second assessor.

**Illustrative example:** The hypothetical alternate inspector holds a completion certificate and a passing quiz score for the measurement course. The quality reviewer sees only those two items for first-article inspection of the second bracket family. The reviewer records the rationale: attendance and recall are evidenced, application is not. The alternate remains unapproved for independent inspection pending observation.

Evidence can also be too old to carry weight. A practical assessment recorded before the work instruction was revised may no longer describe the person's performance under the current method. When the reviewer sees such an item, mark it as needing reconfirmation rather than discarding or accepting it silently. The next chapter returns to how validity is maintained over time.

### Build the decision from the combined evidence

The quality reviewer should read the evidence together. Ask four questions.

1. Does the task's agreed evidence set include a practical element, and is it present?
2. Does each item refer to the current task definition and instruction?
3. Is any item missing, dated before a relevant change, or recorded by someone not authorized to assess?
4. Does the combined evidence support the specific decision, such as independent assignment for one product family?

Record the rationale and the evidence references. When only attendance or a certificate is available for a task that needs more, state that plainly in the record and keep the person out of independent work.

### Test the rule with a certificate and no assessment

In a demonstration, present a fictional person with a completed course and no assessment and check that the decision process does not automatically call the person competent. Watch what the screen shows. If a certificate turns a status green on its own, the system can mislead supervisors, and the team will need either a configuration that prevents it or a manual control. Record the finding in the results sheet. The test is about the local decision process as much as the software: even a well-configured system depends on people applying the evidence rule.

> **Key Consideration:** A checkbox labeled "trained" invites supervisors to read it as "competent." Use wording in procedures and training that keeps the two ideas apart.

## Respond to an unsuccessful assessment

Some assessments will not succeed. A workflow that has no honest path for that outcome pushes people to pass candidates who are not ready, or leaves the result unrecorded. Plan the response in advance so that an unsuccessful assessment is handled as information, not as an embarrassment.

### Record the outcome and the gap

When the assessor concludes that the person did not meet the criteria, record the outcome, the specific criteria not met and what was observed. Specificity matters. "Did not pass" tells the next reader nothing, whereas "did not select the correct gauge for the second feature type" points to an action. Also record who decided and when, using the same fields as a successful assessment so that records are comparable.

The result of an unsuccessful assessment must lead somewhere: further coaching, a different action from the earlier chapter, a reassessment, or a decision that the person is not suited to the task.

Support the person as well as the schedule. An unsuccessful assessment is a result about the evidence at that time, not a verdict on the individual. Tell the person what the criteria were, what was observed and what will happen next, and agree on the date of the reassessment with them. A transparent process encourages people to ask for help before an assessment and not to hide uncertainty.

### Control assignment while the gap is open

The supervisor and assessor decide together how to cover the task in the meantime. The person should not be assigned independent work on that task until the criteria are met, and the task must be covered by someone whose evidence supports it. If no evidenced person is available, escalate to the process owner as an exception rather than relaxing the criteria.

| Situation | Response | Escalation |
|---|---|---|
| First unsuccessful assessment | Plan further action and a reassessment date | None beyond the supervisor |
| Repeated unsuccessful assessments | Review whether the action suits the gap | Process owner and quality lead |
| No evidenced coverage during the gap | Hold or reschedule the work | Management decision |
| Assessor disagreement | Second assessor or process owner decides | Quality lead |

**Illustrative example:** In the hypothetical plant, the alternate inspector misreads a marginal result during observation. The assessor records the outcome and the criterion missed; the supervisor arranges additional coaching and the usual inspector keeps first-article checks. The alternate is reassessed before any independent assignment.

Decide in advance how many attempts the process allows before a different action is considered. A reasonable local rule is that after a repeated unsuccessful assessment, the process owner and quality lead review whether the action fits the gap, whether the task definition is realistic and whether the person might be better placed on another task. Write that rule down so that decisions do not depend on who happens to be on shift.

### Retain appropriate evidence of the decision

The [APG paper](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-Competence.pdf) says clause 7.2 d calls for appropriate documented information as evidence of competence, and that the organization decides what is appropriate. For an unsuccessful assessment, that usually means keeping the outcome, the criteria, the owner of each follow-up action, the reassessment plan and the eventual result, so that the history shows how the person moved from not yet competent to competent. Do not delete the earlier failed record when the person later passes. The sequence is evidence of the decision and follow-up history, not proof that the process is effective.

When evaluating software, walk through a failed assessment and confirm that the decision, the follow-up and the interim coverage can be explained from the records. Ask whether an earlier outcome stays visible after a later pass, and whether the reassessment links back to the first result. If the system cannot show this, record it as a limitation and decide what the team will keep outside the system.
