## Start with the staffing question

Every competence workflow begins with a decision that someone makes under time pressure: who may do this task, on this shift, without supervision? Before any software enters the conversation, write that decision down in plain words. Name the task, the shift or cell where it happens, the person who makes the assignment, and the evidence that person looks at today. A team that cannot state the question in a sentence will not be able to test whether a system answers it.

The ISO 9001 Auditing Practices Group, a joint effort of ISO/TC 176 and the International Accreditation Forum, frames competence as the ability to apply knowledge and skills to achieve intended results. Its paper, [Guidance on auditing Competence](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-Competence.pdf), was written for auditors working with ISO 9001:2015 clause 7.2. It is not an additional standard, and it does not describe the requirements of any later edition. It is used in this guide as a clear description of what auditors tend to examine, not as a source of obligations.

That description is useful here because it starts with people doing real work. The paper says an auditor may sample people in a process, observe their performance, interview them about the competence they need, and confirm that they can do their duties. It also says an auditor may verify that, when the usual person is absent, someone with an adequate level of competence is available to replace them. That last point is the staffing question in audit language. The supervisor who covers an absence is making the same decision an auditor later checks.

Use the five elements that carry through this guide: role, input, decision, record, and exception.

| Element | What to capture | Example prompt |
|---|---|---|
| Role | Who makes the staffing decision | Production supervisor, shift lead or cell coordinator |
| Input | What that person consults today | Task schedule, availability list, existing competence evidence |
| Decision | The choice that results | Assign a person the evidence supports, or hold the assignment |
| Record | What remains afterward | Who decided, and which evidence was consulted |
| Exception | What happens when the usual path fails | No evidenced replacement is available |

The APG paper notes that clause 7.2(d) calls for retaining appropriate documented information as evidence of competence, and that the word appropriate leaves the organization to decide which records have value. A team should therefore decide locally how much of the decision itself needs a record. A routine reassignment between two people who have worked a station for years may need little. Assigning an alternate to inspect output at a station they have never covered needs more.

Mapping every task in the plant at once produces a diagram nobody trusts. Pick one cell, line, or service step where an incorrect assignment would matter, and where the staffing question comes up often enough to observe. Good candidates are a final inspection station, a process that needs a recognized qualification, or a cell with frequent absences. Watch how assignments are actually made at shift start.

**Illustrative example:** An unnamed Canadian manufacturer of machined components relies on one inspector for first-article checks at a cutting cell. When that inspector is away, the supervisor usually asks whoever seems most experienced. The team chooses this cell for mapping because the substitution happens several times a month and the supervisor cannot show, on the spot, why a particular alternate was chosen. All of the situations in this chapter are hypothetical, and none describes a customer result.

Two questions expose most weak points. Who is allowed to say a person may do the task, and what happens when that person is unavailable? The APG paper notes that qualifications are not always required but competencies are, citing welders for specific welding conditions as a case where a qualification from a qualified authority may be required. Mark every step where a recognized qualification matters; those steps need a different kind of evidence from steps that rely on internal assessment.

## Trace evidence and handoffs

With the decision described, follow the evidence behind it. The goal is a current-state map showing how a task requirement becomes a trained, assessed, and recorded person, and who handles each step. The APG paper describes a cycle that makes a useful skeleton: determine the necessary competence, take actions to acquire it, evaluate whether those actions worked, and retain documented information. Draw those four boxes and fill them with what actually happens at the chosen cell.

For each step, collect the document, system, or conversation that carries it today, and name the person who owns the handoff to the next step.

1. **Determining necessary competence.** Look for job descriptions, work instructions, process specifications, and customer or regulatory requirements that say what the task demands. In some plants this lives in a single controlled document. In others it exists as the supervisor's knowledge of the task.
2. **Actions to acquire competence.** The paper lists training, hiring people with specific competencies, on-the-job training, mentoring, reassigning tasks or people, adjusting the scope of a job, and subcontracting. Record which of these the cell really uses. Many teams discover that on-the-job coaching is the main route while the formal training plan describes classroom sessions.
3. **Evaluating effectiveness.** The paper says organizations should select evaluation techniques suited to their needs, such as role-play, peer review, observation, review of training and employment records, or interviews. Find out who evaluates, what they observe, and where the result goes.
4. **Retaining evidence.** Locate every place that holds proof: personnel files, training sign-in sheets, quiz results, supervisor checklists, certificates, and notes about practical assessments.

Expect the records to sit in more than one place. The APG paper observes that documented information on competence may be held locally at each process or by a central function such as Human Resources. Record each location, who maintains it, and its format, whether paper, spreadsheet, shared drive, or learning platform. A person asked to prove one individual's competence must be able to assemble the pieces, and later migration work depends on this list.

One distinction needs special attention during mapping. The APG paper explains that records can show a training course was completed, but participation alone does not necessarily provide evidence that the trainee is competent, because it may demonstrate that knowledge was gained and not that it is being applied effectively. It also says the existence of training plans and records of effectiveness evaluation is not sufficient, on its own, to show how an organization ensures the competence it needs.

For the map, this means marking which records show attendance or completion and which show demonstrated ability. A sign-in sheet belongs to the first group. A supervisor's observation of the person performing the task under normal conditions belongs to the second. If the cell has only the first kind, the map should say so.

| Evidence type | What it shows | What it does not show on its own |
|---|---|---|
| Attendance or completion record | The person took part in learning | That the person applies it at the task |
| Quiz or test result | Knowledge at a point in time | Practical performance under real conditions |
| Supervisor observation | Performance at the cell | Whether the result holds after a change |
| Recognized qualification | Standing with an outside authority | Familiarity with this plant's equipment |

## Define decision states and acceptance

The final step in mapping is to say, in advance, what each possible answer to the staffing question means. Without agreed definitions, two supervisors read the same record differently, and any system built around it inherits the ambiguity. The states below are local design choices, not fields of any product.

Three states are enough to start for most cells.

- **Evidenced.** The record set for this person and task meets the rules the team agreed. The supervisor may assign the work.
- **Awaiting assessment.** Learning is complete or under way, but the practical evaluation required by the team's rules is missing or not yet recorded. The person may work only under conditions the team defines, such as with a qualified colleague present, or not at all.
- **Evidence missing.** No record supports the assignment. The supervisor must not treat this as approval.

Resist adding states until the team has used these for a few weeks; a long list invites arguments about which one applies. Treat expiry of a recognized qualification as a separate flag.

A state is only useful when the rule for entering it is clear. Write each rule in a sentence that a supervisor can apply without interpretation.

| Transition | Rule to define | Who confirms |
|---|---|---|
| Evidence missing to awaiting assessment | Required learning is complete and recorded | Training coordinator |
| Awaiting assessment to evidenced | Practical evaluation by an authorized assessor is recorded against the current instruction | Assessor or quality lead |
| Evidenced to awaiting assessment | A change to the task, equipment, or instruction requires re-evaluation | Workflow owner |
| Any state to evidence missing | A required record is found to be invalid or cannot be located | Quality lead |

The third row connects to a point in the APG paper: competence verification may need to be monitored or repeated, and corrective actions and internal audit results can show where people lack it. The paper also lists prompts for refresher training and revalidation of a qualification among the records an organization may keep. Later chapters cover renewal in detail. For mapping, it is enough to agree that the state can go backward and that someone owns that decision.

Every workflow needs a person who resolves uncertainty. When a supervisor cannot tell whether a record set is complete, who decides? If the answer is "whoever is around," the workflow has no owner. Name one role and one backup, and write down how long the operational team waits for a ruling before it uses a safe alternative, such as reassigning the task to a person already in the evidenced state or stopping the job.

The APG paper says an organization may need to ensure adequate replacements, and gives cross-training charts among the records that can help mitigate risks from staff shortages. A cross-training view is the practical tool here: it lets a supervisor see at a glance who else is evidenced for the task. If no one is, that is a staffing risk to escalate, not a gap to paper over.

Before accepting the definitions, run them against real, imperfect examples. Pull four or five records from the chosen cell and ask two people independently to assign each a state. Compare answers. Where they differ, the rule is unclear, and the team should rewrite it. Include at least one record with learning complete but no practical assessment, one with an assessment against an outdated instruction, and one with a qualification of unknown expiry.

**Illustrative example:** In the hypothetical plant, the alternate inspector has a completed measurement course and no recorded practical assessment. Two supervisors classify the record differently: one says evidenced because the course covered the method, and the other says awaiting assessment. The team resolves the disagreement by writing that completion alone never moves a person to evidenced for first-article inspection. The alternate stays awaiting assessment until quality records a practical evaluation, and the supervisor assigns the work to a colleague who is already evidenced.

The mapping work is complete when a group of people who were not involved in creating it can use it. A practical acceptance test has four parts:

1. A supervisor can state the staffing question for the cell and find the evidence for a named person in a reasonable, agreed time.
2. Every record location on the map has an owner.
3. Each state has a written entry rule, and two independent reviewers agree on test cases.
4. The exception owner and the interim safe action are named.

List unresolved questions rather than a tidy diagram: records that conflict, assessments nobody can locate, and approvals nobody is sure they own. Each becomes an input to later chapters, and the location list feeds migration planning.

Keep the finished map as the baseline against which every later claim, from any vendor or from ICC, is compared. The [ICC training and competency module description](/solutions/training-competence/) is one published description that can be tested against this baseline, and the evaluation chapter shows how.

> **Important:** Do not describe a state as supported by software just because it appears on the map. The team defines these states first; whether a given system can represent them is a question for the demonstration.
