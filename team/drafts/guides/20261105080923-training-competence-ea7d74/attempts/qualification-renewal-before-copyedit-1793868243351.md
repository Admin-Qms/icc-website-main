## Review applicable qualification conditions

A person can be competent on a task today and still become a risk tomorrow if a required qualification lapses, a scope changes or a condition attached to it is no longer met. This chapter covers how a team keeps a status valid over time and how it retrieves the evidence when someone asks. It does not repeat the design of the initial assessment, which the previous chapter covered.

Start with the qualifications. The Auditing Practices Group, in its [guidance on auditing competence](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-Competence.pdf), says qualifications are not always required but competencies are, and that where statutes or other requirements call for specific qualifications, the organization should determine them and be able to demonstrate that people have acquired and maintain them. It gives welders for specific welding conditions as one example. The paper was written for auditors working with ISO 9001:2015 clause 7.2 and does not itself create legal obligations.

That last point matters for a Canadian team. This guide does not state which qualifications any province, regulator or customer requires, and it does not give renewal periods. Those come from the applicable requirement itself. The team's task is to identify which requirements apply to its own work and to read the scope and validity conditions in them.

### Name an owner and a source for each requirement

For every task in the matrix that depends on a recognized qualification, record four facts.

1. **The requirement.** Which statute, standard, customer requirement or other source calls for the qualification.
2. **The scope.** What work, equipment or conditions the qualification covers, as stated by the body that issued it.
3. **The validity condition.** Whether it has an expiration, a renewal condition or a limit, read from the source and not assumed.
4. **The owner.** The qualification owner, who confirms validity and scope and records the outcome.

If any of the four cannot be confirmed, the team should not rely on the qualification. Record that the requirement, scope or validity is unresolved, and keep the person out of work that depends on it until the owner resolves it. This is a conservative local rule that follows from the guidance, which asks organizations to demonstrate that qualifications are maintained.

**Illustrative example:** An unnamed Canadian manufacturer of machined components has a task that its customer requires to be performed by a person holding a specific qualification. Before relying on a new hire's qualification, the qualification owner checks that the scope covers the equipment in the cell, reads the validity condition and records the result and the evidence consulted. The new hire is not assigned the task until that review is complete.

Review the requirement itself on a schedule, not only the person. Customer requirements, regulations and internal procedures change, and a qualification that satisfied last year's version may not satisfy the current one. The qualification owner should note the version or date of the source consulted, so that a later reviewer can see which edition was applied. Where the source is a standard, take its edition from the document in hand rather than from memory, and treat historical guidance as describing its own period.

### Keep qualification and competence records distinct

Record the qualification separately from the in-plant assessment. A person can hold a current qualification and still need an assessment on a particular machine, and a person can be assessed as independent and still lack a qualification the task requires. The matrix chapter described this distinction. In a review, check the two on their own terms and then together.

When evaluating software, ask how a reviewer would find the applicable evidence and tell an unresolved qualification from a current one. The [ICC training and competency module page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/training-competence/) maps the module to ISO 9001:2015 clause 7.2 and describes a competency matrix and certificates issued on completion. It does not, in the supplied evidence, describe how external qualifications, scopes or expiration conditions are recorded, so treat those as questions for the demonstration. Ask the supplier to record a fictional qualification with a scope and a validity condition and to show how an unresolved one appears.

## Trigger review after change or over time

A status is a claim about a moment. Something has to prompt the team to check that it still holds. The [APG paper](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-Competence.pdf) notes that competence verification may need to be monitored or repeated, and that corrective actions and internal audit results can show where people lack the competence they need. It also lists, among the records an organization may keep, prompts to monitor when refresher training is due for revalidation of a qualification.

### Define the triggers

Write down the events that cause a review. Two groups are useful: changes to the work and the passage of time.

| Trigger | Example | Likely response |
|---|---|---|
| Changed method or equipment | A revised inspection method or a new gauge | Review the task definition and the people holding a status for it |
| Audit or corrective action finding | A finding that points to a skill gap | Review affected people and the evidence rules |
| Observed performance concern | Repeated rework at the cell | Reassess the people concerned |
| Locally determined interval | A review date the team set for a task | Confirm that evidence is still current |
| Qualification condition | A validity condition read from the requirement | Confirm or renew before the date |

The intervals are local decisions made from the task's risk and from the source requirements. Do not copy an interval from another plant. For a task with high consequence, the team may choose a short review cycle. For a stable, low-consequence task, a longer one may be reasonable. Record the reasoning so that the interval can be revisited.

**Illustrative example:** A revised inspection method leads the hypothetical process owner to schedule a review of both inspectors' competence. The owner lists the two people, records the trigger and the planned action, and keeps the usual inspector on first-article checks until the alternate's evidence is confirmed against the revised method.

Keep the number of triggers manageable. Five or six clear triggers that the team applies are more useful than a long list that people stop reading. Review the list after each audit cycle: if an event keeps causing problems that no trigger covers, add one, and if a trigger never fires, ask whether it is needed or whether it is being missed.

### Decide what happens when a review cannot be done in time

Reviews will sometimes be late. The person may be away, or the assessor unavailable. Treat an affected person who cannot be reviewed before the next assignment as an exception. The process owner decides whether the person may work under supervision, whether the task goes to an evidenced colleague, or whether the work waits. Record the decision and the date by which the review will be done. An overdue review is not a silent extension of the old status.

Assign responsibility for the review calendar. Someone must look at upcoming reassessments each week or month, whether the list comes from the software or from a spreadsheet. Name that person and a backup, and write down what they do when a date is about to pass. Without an owner for the calendar, even the best reminders are ignored.

### Ask how review is initiated in the system

During evaluation, test a changed method and ask how review is initiated for the people affected. The APG paper mentions prompts for refresher training as one kind of record an organization may keep, but whether the [ICC module](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/training-competence/) provides reminders is an open question for the demonstration, because the supplied evidence does not establish it. Ask the supplier to show, step by step, how the owner would find everyone holding a status for the revised task, and note which steps are manual. If notifications exist, ask the supplier to demonstrate them and record who receives them and what they say. If they do not, decide how the team will track dates, for example a weekly review of upcoming reassessments.

## Retrieve evidence for a sampled person

Sooner or later someone will ask for the proof. It may be an internal reviewer, a customer or an auditor. The [APG paper](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-Competence.pdf) describes auditors sampling people in a process, observing their performance, interviewing them about the competence the task requires and checking whether adequate replacements exist when the usual person is absent. A good record set supports those activities. It does not replace them.

### Prepare for a sample

Choose a task and a person and try to answer the questions an assessor would ask, in the order they would ask them. Doing this before an external review is far better than discovering gaps during one.

1. What does the task require, and who approved the requirement?
2. What action did the person take to acquire the competence?
3. How was the effectiveness of that action evaluated, by whom and when?
4. What qualification, if any, applies, and is it current and in scope?
5. Who covers the task when this person is absent, and what supports that?

The quality representative should be able to find the evidence for each answer, explain how the decision was made and point out any gap openly. A record set that shows an unresolved gap with an owner is more credible than one that appears complete and cannot be explained.

Check consistency between records, not only their presence. A common weakness is two records that disagree: a matrix shows a person as independent while the assessment on file refers to a superseded instruction, or a certificate date precedes a role change. Reviewers should compare dates, versions and names across the items they retrieve, and record every inconsistency with an owner and a due date.

### Remember that records do not replace people

The APG paper repeats that sampling, observing and interviewing personnel about their competence is a key audit activity, and that the existence of training plans and records is not sufficient to show how competence is ensured. Prepare the people as well as the files. A supervisor should be able to explain how coverage is selected. An operator should be able to say what the task requires and what they have demonstrated. Software records support those conversations but cannot answer questions on behalf of the person.

Records may be held centrally or locally, and the guidance says documented information should be appropriate to the organization. Whatever the arrangement, the team should know where each piece of evidence lives and who maintains it. Use the location list from the mapping chapter to keep retrieval short.

Practice the explanation as well as the retrieval. Ask the quality representative to walk a colleague through the evidence for one sampled person in a few minutes, saying what each item shows and what it does not. If the explanation depends on knowledge that is not written anywhere, such as which supervisor usually covers, capture that rule in the record so it survives staff changes. Doing this once a quarter on a different task keeps the habit alive and reveals gaps early.

### Time a retrieval and check it

In evaluation and again after rollout, time a representative retrieval. Pick a fictional or real sampled person and ask a reviewer to assemble the evidence for one task, then compare what they found against the local criteria above. Record the time, the steps, the number of places consulted and what was missing. Do not claim a universal benchmark. The measurement is useful because it is comparable over time in your own plant, and it shows whether changes to records or to the system make retrieval easier.

**Illustrative example:** In the hypothetical plant, quality retrieves the alternate inspector's practical assessment for the second bracket family and explains how the supervisor selects coverage when the usual inspector is absent. The retrieval takes several steps across two places and reveals that the assessment references the previous instruction revision. Quality records the inconsistency, assigns it to the process owner and notes the time taken.

When the supporting assessment is missing or inconsistent, the record should say so, and the person's status should follow the local rules rather than the hope that the evidence exists somewhere.
