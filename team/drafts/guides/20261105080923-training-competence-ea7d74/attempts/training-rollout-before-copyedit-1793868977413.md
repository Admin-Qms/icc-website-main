## Prepare role-specific tasks

A rollout teaches people to use a workflow, not to admire a system. The people who will use the software are not one audience. A supervisor makes staffing decisions, an assessor records the outcome of a practical evaluation, a learner completes assigned steps, and a quality reviewer retrieves evidence. Each needs different practice. This chapter covers learning to use the new workflow and deciding whether it is ready to expand. It does not redesign the task criteria agreed in the matrix chapters, and it leaves long-term improvement for the final chapter.

Begin with the roles defined in the mapping work and write, for each, the three or four tasks that person must be able to do without help on the first day. A supervisor must be able to find a named person's status for a named task and explain what it means. An assessor must be able to record a method, the criteria applied and an outcome. A learner must be able to see an assigned action and complete it. A quality reviewer must be able to follow one decision back to its evidence.

### Build the plan around real tasks

The [ICC training and competency module page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/training-competence/) describes an employee-facing learning area and supervisor sign-off of on-the-job training, among other features. Those are published descriptions. Before building the training plan, verify each role's actual experience and access in the configured system. A supervisor who cannot reach the sign-off screen from the cell, or a learner who cannot open assigned content on a shared device, will not use the system however good the presentation was.

| Role | Practice task | Evidence of readiness |
|---|---|---|
| Supervisor | Locate the alternate's evidence and decide who covers the task | Correct decision explained aloud |
| Assessor | Record a method, criteria and outcome for a practice case | Record complete without prompting |
| Learner | Open an assigned action and complete it | Completion recorded correctly |
| Quality reviewer | Trace one decision to its evidence | Gaps identified and noted |
| Workflow owner | Review exceptions and open actions | Owners and dates assigned |

Use realistic scenarios drawn from the hypothetical or real cell, not toy examples. People learn the workflow faster when the practice looks like their day.

**Illustrative example:** An unnamed Canadian manufacturer of machined components has the supervisor practice locating the alternate inspector's evidence for first-article inspection of the second bracket family, and has a learner practice opening a learning assignment. The rollout lead notes what each person found confusing and updates the plan before the next session.

Provide a one-page reference for each role, written in the plant's own words. It should list the states and what each permits, the first step when a record is incomplete, and who to call. People rarely read manuals during a shift, but they will read a single page posted at the cell or saved on the device they use. Review the page after the pilot and correct anything that caused confusion.

### Teach the rules as well as the screens

Screens are the easy part. The harder lesson is what the states mean and what they permit. Spend as much time on the decision rules as on the clicks: what evidenced, awaiting assessment and evidence missing mean for the staffing decision, why completion is not the same as competence and what to do when a record is incomplete. The Auditing Practices Group, in its [guidance on auditing competence](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-Competence.pdf), says participation in a course does not by itself show a person is competent. A supervisor who has absorbed that point will read a certificate correctly.

Keep sessions short and close to the work. Twenty minutes at the cell, using the actual device and a realistic case, is usually easier to schedule than a long classroom session, and it exposes access and connectivity problems immediately. Pair each supervisor with a colleague during the first week so that questions are answered while the task is fresh. Where shifts differ, make sure every shift has someone who has completed the practice, since the people on nights or weekends are often the last to be trained and the first to need help.

### Observe, do not only present

Do not rely on a presentation or a vendor claim to judge readiness. Observe each role complete a realistic task, and record whether they did it unaided, with help or not at all. If a role cannot access or complete its required task, treat it as an exception: the rollout lead records the problem, assigns an owner and decides whether the pilot waits. Keep a training plan and a record of who completed which practice, since the plant itself is subject to the same expectation of evidence that it applies to its workers.

## Pilot normal and exception paths

A pilot tests the workflow under conditions close to real use, on a limited scope. The aim is to find out whether the people, the rules and the system together produce the decisions the team intended. A pilot that covers only the easy cases shows nothing. The [APG paper](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-Competence.pdf) describes auditors sampling people in a process, observing their work and checking replacement arrangements, which is a reminder that the test is the decision at the cell, not the number of records entered.

### Choose a small, representative scope

Pick one cell or task family and a handful of people. Include the cases that put pressure on the rules. At a minimum, the pilot should include three situations.

1. **Normal path.** An available, evidenced inspector is assigned the task, and the supervisor can show why.
2. **Missing assessment.** A person has completed learning but has no recorded practical assessment, and the supervisor must decide.
3. **Absent primary worker.** The usual person is away, and the supervisor must find an evidenced replacement or hold the work.

Add further cases that match the plant: an expired required qualification, a changed method, a disputed sign-off. Each should have an expected outcome written beforehand by the quality lead, so that the pilot is compared with the locally approved rules and not with an impression.

Brief the pilot participants honestly. Tell them the pilot tests the workflow and not their performance, that mistakes are useful information and that nobody is penalized for a wrong answer in a test case. People who fear blame hide confusion, and hidden confusion is exactly what the pilot is meant to surface.

### Compare decisions with the rules

Measure the quality of decisions, not just activity. For each scenario, record what the supervisor decided, what the rules say the decision should have been, and any difference. The question is whether an incomplete record was ever interpreted as authorization. If it was, that is the most serious finding the pilot can produce, and it should be traced to its cause: unclear wording, a confusing screen or a habit carried over from the old process.

| Scenario | Expected decision | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Evidenced inspector available | Assign | Reason can be explained from the record |
| Learning complete, no assessment | Do not assign independently; escalate or supervise | Status shown as awaiting assessment |
| Primary absent, no evidenced cover | Hold or reschedule the work | Escalation to the exception owner |
| Required qualification unconfirmed | Block dependent work | Block applied until resolved |

An evidenced decision and an escalation when evidence is missing are the two behaviors the pilot must demonstrate. If either fails, the pilot does not pass, whatever else went well.

**Illustrative example:** The hypothetical team tests the alternate's incomplete assessment when the usual inspector is unavailable. The supervisor correctly declines to assign independent inspection, escalates to the quality lead and arranges cover from another evidenced colleague. The record shows the escalation, the owner and the date.

Treat the pilot as a chance to improve the written workflow as well as the configuration. If three different supervisors ask the same question, the rule is unclear, and the fix is a clearer sentence in the procedure, not another reminder. Update the workflow document, note the version and tell the pilot participants what changed. Participants who see their feedback acted upon are more willing to report problems later.

### Record issues and owners

Keep a pilot log with the observed decision, the issue, the owner and the date for resolving it. Issues will include confusing labels, slow retrieval, access problems and gaps in the rules. Some are fixed by training, some by configuration and some by changing the written workflow. Decide which, and say who does it. At the end of the pilot, the pilot supervisor and quality lead accept the pilot, accept it with conditions or assign corrective actions before another trial. Do not call the pilot a success because the system stayed up.

## Decide whether to expand

Expansion adds tasks, cells or sites, and multiplies any weakness the pilot left. The decision is the rollout sponsor's, informed by the pilot log, the open issues and the support capacity the team can actually provide. Expanding is not the default outcome; repeating the pilot or pausing can be the right call.

### Review comprehension, workload and open exceptions

Before adding scope, review three things. First, do users understand the states and the rules? Test this by asking supervisors to classify a handful of unfamiliar records and comparing their answers. Second, what workload does the workflow place on supervisors and assessors, and can it be sustained? Third, which exceptions remain unresolved, and who owns them?

Use locally collected observations. Do not borrow adoption percentages or return-on-investment figures from other organizations or from marketing material. Neither the supplied ICC description nor the guidance supports any such number, and a figure from elsewhere says little about this plant. Count what happened: how many decisions were made, how many were correct against the rules, how long a retrieval took, how many exceptions are open.

Include the people who did not take part in the pilot in the comprehension test. Pilot participants have already practiced and may overstate how clear the workflow is. A supervisor from another shift, given the one-page reference and a few unfamiliar records, shows how well the workflow will work for the next group.

### Set thresholds in advance

Agree acceptance thresholds before the pilot begins, so that the result is not argued after the fact. A threshold might be that every supervisor classifies a set of test records correctly, that no incomplete record was treated as authorization, or that evidence for a sampled person can be retrieved within a time the team considers acceptable. These are local choices. If a threshold is missed, say how the miss affects expansion: repeat the pilot, add training, adjust configuration or pause.

| Pilot result | Typical decision |
|---|---|
| All thresholds met, exceptions owned | Expand to the next task or cell |
| Comprehension gaps only | Add focused training and repeat that part |
| Rule or configuration problems | Fix, then repeat the affected scenarios |
| Incomplete record treated as authorization | Pause and investigate before any expansion |

**Illustrative example:** In the hypothetical plant, expansion is paused until supervisors consistently recognize the awaiting-assessment state. Two of five misread it in the test set. The rollout lead schedules a short session using the misclassified records, and the group repeats the test the following week before the sponsor reconsiders.

Be careful about timing. Avoid expanding during a busy production period, an audit or a major change to the products or equipment in the cell, because the team will lack the attention to notice early problems. Choose a window in which supervisors can spend a few extra minutes on each decision, and agree how long the expanded scope will be watched closely before it is treated as routine.

### Plan support and ownership after expansion

Decide who answers questions once the project team steps back. Name a first point of contact in each area, such as a trained supervisor, and a route to the workflow owner for rule questions. Record decisions about expansion, with action owners and dates, so that the next review can see what was agreed. The [APG paper](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-Competence.pdf) reminds organizations that competence information may exist locally or centrally, so a plan should say who maintains what in each place as the scope grows.

When users cannot reliably identify who may perform a task, the workflow is not ready to expand, whatever the technology shows. Pause, find the cause and fix it, because a wrong answer to that question is the hazard the whole project exists to remove.
