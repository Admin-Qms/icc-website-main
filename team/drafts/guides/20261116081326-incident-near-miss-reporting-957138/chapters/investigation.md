## Assign and scope the investigation

An investigation begins when someone decides that a rated report deserves a closer look. That decision, and who makes it, shape everything that follows. The safety coordinator normally holds it. The input is the rated report; the decision is who investigates and how deeply; the record is an assignment entry that names the investigator, the scope and the date.

The [ICC Safety module page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/safety/) describes a problem many teams will recognize: after an incident, the investigation starts from a blank page. Treat that as the vendor's description of a pain point, not as measured data, and use it to ask a practical question of any software: when an investigator opens a new investigation, does the original report come with it, including its rating, location, attachments and any linked reports? A blank page means the investigator retypes what is already known, and every retyped fact is a chance for error.

Match depth to the rating. A rule of thumb helps the safety coordinator decide quickly, and the plant should write it down. One workable pattern is a short review by the area supervisor for lower-rated reports, a documented investigation by a trained investigator for medium ones and a team investigation for the highest ones. The depths and the people are the plant's choice. What matters is that the rule exists, that it refers to the severity scale and that the reason for any departure from it is recorded.

Choose investigators with care. The person who investigates should know the process well enough to ask good questions but should not be the person whose actions are under review. A conflict can be obvious, such as a supervisor investigating an event on their own line, or subtle, such as an investigator who designed the control that failed. The assignment step should include a short check: does the proposed investigator have any involvement that could affect their judgment? If so, assign someone else or add a second person.

Scope the investigation in a few sentences. State what question the investigation must answer, for example, what condition allowed a pedestrian and a lift truck to share a path, and what falls outside it. A clear scope keeps the work proportionate and prevents an investigation from sprawling into every nearby issue. Note issues found along the way as separate reports rather than expanding the scope without a decision.

Set expectations for timing. A target date for the investigation helps keep it moving, and the date can be adjusted with a recorded reason when evidence takes longer to collect. Ask the vendor whether the product shows investigations that are open past their target and who sees that list.

Decide who may reassign or widen an investigation, and require a reason when that happens. An investigation that quietly changes owner or scope midway is hard to review later, and the assignment entry should show every change with a date and a name.

**Illustrative example:** An unnamed Canadian manufacturer rates a near-miss at a pedestrian crossing as Medium. The safety coordinator assigns a maintenance planner who has no role on that line, records the scope as "how the crossing and the truck route overlap" and sets a date two weeks out. A second near-miss with a lower rating gets a short review by the area supervisor. The scenario is invented and does not describe a customer result.

## Collect evidence

Evidence is what separates a conclusion from an opinion. The investigator owns this step. The input is the scene, the people involved and the relevant records. The decision is what evidence is enough to support a conclusion. The record is an evidence list kept with the incident record. The exception to plan for is evidence that cannot be obtained, such as a witness who is unavailable.

Start with what can disappear quickly. The condition of the scene may change as soon as work resumes, so photographs, measurements and a sketch should be taken early, with the area made safe first. Equipment may be repaired or moved. Where an event involved equipment, record its identity and condition before anything is altered, and keep any damaged part if the plant's rules allow.

Then gather what the people involved know. Talk to the reporter, the people nearby and anyone who works in the area, and keep the tone open. The purpose is to learn what happened and why the situation allowed it, not to find fault. Take statements in the person's own words where possible, note the date and who took them, and avoid leading questions. When a person asks for a colleague or representative to be present, follow the plant's own policies and any applicable rules, and confirm those with the relevant authority if unsure.

Next, gather records that bear on the event. Depending on the event, these might include training records for the people involved, the work instruction or procedure, equipment maintenance and inspection records, previous reports from the same area and any related corrective actions. Records held in other systems should be identified on the evidence list even if they cannot be attached, so a reader knows where to find them.

Ask the vendor how evidence is held. Can photos, documents and notes be attached to the investigation record? Is the attachment kept with the incident so an auditor can open it from the same screen? Can the plant see who added or removed an attachment and when? The [ICC Safety page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/safety/) describes an auditor view with the incident log and linked corrective actions, but the research reviewed for this guide does not describe how investigation evidence is stored, so confirm it in a demonstration with a real sample.

Keep the evidence list readable. For each item, note what it is, who collected it, when and where it is held. A reviewer who opens the list should be able to tell in a minute whether the main questions have been covered, and which items are still outstanding.

Make a judgment about sufficiency. The investigator asks whether the evidence answers the scope question and whether another reasonable person would reach the same conclusion from it. If not, note what is missing and either collect it or record why it cannot be collected. An honest note that a witness could not be reached is better than a conclusion that quietly depends on a guess.

Protect sensitive information. Statements, photographs of people and medical details may need restricted access. Decide who can open the evidence for an investigation and make sure the software's permissions match that decision.

## Analyze causes without blame

Analysis turns evidence into an explanation. The investigator and the area supervisor work on it together, using the evidence list. The decision is what caused the event, in terms the plant can act on. The record is a cause statement. The exception to plan for is pressure to name an individual as the cause.

Use a method the plant already knows. Many organizations use simple techniques, such as asking "why" repeatedly or drawing the sequence of events, and some use more structured methods. The software should record the analysis, not dictate the method. Ask the vendor whether the investigation record has free-text space for the analysis, whether it supports attaching a diagram and whether the plant can add its own fields.

Look at conditions before individuals. A person's action is usually one step in a chain that included the design of the work, the tools provided, the instructions given, the training offered, the schedule and the supervision. If the explanation stops at "the operator did not follow the procedure," ask why the procedure was not followed. Perhaps the procedure was impractical, perhaps the equipment made it difficult, or perhaps the person had never been shown. Each answer points to something the plant can change.

Separate fact from inference. A good cause statement distinguishes what was observed, what was reported and what the investigator concluded, and it shows which evidence supports each point. Reviewers can then see how a conclusion was reached and challenge it where the evidence is thin.

Involve the people who do the work. The area supervisor and operators often know why a task is done in a particular way, and an analysis written without them may miss the practical reasons. Invite them to comment on the draft cause statement before it goes to review, and record any disagreement along with how it was resolved.

Be honest about uncertainty. Some events do not have a single clear cause, and some causes cannot be confirmed. A statement such as "the likely contributing conditions were these two, and the third could not be confirmed" is more useful than a confident but unsupported explanation.

Watch for the pressure to blame. A manager may want a quick answer, a witness may point at a colleague and the investigator may feel the same pull. Name the pressure openly at the start of the analysis and remind everyone that the aim is to prevent a repeat. If an individual's conduct really is in question, the plant's separate process for that matter handles it, and the investigation record should say where the matter was referred without turning the cause analysis into a disciplinary file.

The [ICC page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/safety/) states that every AI output in the software is a suggestion a person reviews before it enters a controlled record. If any AI assistance appears in an investigation, such as a suggested summary, ask the vendor what it does and where the person's review is recorded. The investigator remains responsible for the cause statement.

## Record conclusions and review

The last step is a reviewed conclusion that the plant can rely on. The safety lead normally reviews. The input is the draft investigation. The decision is to accept it or return it. The record is the reviewed conclusion, with the reviewer's name and the date. The exception is a conclusion that the evidence does not support.

Write the conclusion plainly. It should say what happened, what caused or contributed to it, what evidence supports that and what the investigator recommends. Recommendations should describe what needs to change, but the decision to act on them, with an owner and a date, belongs to the corrective action step covered in the next chapter. Keeping the two apart stops an investigation from closing before anyone has agreed to act.

Require a review before closure. A second person reads the investigation and asks whether the scope was answered, whether the evidence supports the cause statement and whether the recommendations follow. The reviewer should be free to return the investigation with a reason, and the return should be recorded. Ask the vendor to show a return and a resubmission with a sample investigation.

Where the software offers any drafting assistance, the reviewer's check matters more. The ICC page says AI output is a suggestion a person reviews before it enters a controlled record. The plant can use that as a rule for itself: no drafted text becomes part of an accepted investigation until a named person has read and approved it, and the record shows who did.

Keep conclusions proportionate to the event. A low-rated near-miss may need a few lines, and a serious event a full report. The reviewer should judge whether the depth matches the rating and the assignment, and the record should show that judgment.

Check that the investigation stays linked to the original report. An auditor should be able to open the incident and see the rating, the evidence, the analysis, the conclusion and the review without hunting. The [ICC Safety page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/safety/) describes an auditor view that shows the log with severity and status, and corrective actions linked to each incident; confirm in a demonstration whether and how the investigation itself appears there.

Close the loop with the people involved where appropriate. Tell the reporter, where known, that the investigation is complete and what it found, in terms the plant is comfortable sharing. Where a joint health and safety committee exists, its role is defined by the province; the Government of Ontario's [guide to health and safety committees and representatives](https://www.ontario.ca/page/guide-health-and-safety-committees-and-representatives) describes it for Ontario, and plants elsewhere should check the rules that apply to them.
