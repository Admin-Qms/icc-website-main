## Decide which reports need action

Not every report calls for a corrective action, and not every action needs the same weight. The decision should be made deliberately, by a named person, and written down. The safety lead usually makes it after the investigation or the first review. The input is the reviewed report or investigation. The decision is whether action is needed and, if so, of what kind. The record is the decision with its reason.

The [ICC Safety module page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/safety/) says that incidents that need corrective action are carried into the Improvements module. The words “that need” imply a decision before the handoff, so ask the vendor to show what that decision looks like. Who marks a report as needing action? What information moves across to the Improvements module? Does the original report stay visible from the action, and does the action stay visible from the report? These are questions for a demonstration, since the page describes the link without detailing the screens.

Write criteria that guide the decision. Useful questions include the following:

- Did the event cause harm, or could it reasonably have done so?
- Has the same or a similar condition been reported before?
- Does the condition remain open, such as an unguarded machine or a blocked exit?
- Does the cause point to something the plant controls?
- Is the cost of acting small compared with the possible harm?

The plant can weigh these as it sees fit. A low-rated report may still need action if it repeats, and a high-rated one may need only a record if the cause was an unrepeatable circumstance. What matters is that the reasoning is visible.

Distinguish a correction from a corrective action. A correction deals with the immediate problem, such as putting the guard back or cleaning up a spill. A corrective action deals with the cause, so the problem does not return, such as changing how guards are checked at shift start. Both may be needed, and they may have different owners and dates. Recording them separately makes it easier to see whether the cause was addressed or only the symptom.

The exception to plan for is a report where no action is needed but a concern remains, for instance, a worry that cannot be tied to evidence. Record the decision not to act, give the reason and state when the plant will look again, so the concern is not simply forgotten. Review a sample of “no action” decisions from time to time to make sure the reasons are sound.

Involve the area supervisor in the decision where possible. The supervisor knows whether the condition is isolated or typical, and whether a fix is practical. Record who was consulted, because a later reader may want to understand why one option was chosen over another.

**Illustrative example:** An unnamed Canadian manufacturer receives a low-rated near-miss about a loose guard fastener. The same condition appears in two earlier reports on the same line. The safety lead records that action is needed because the condition repeats, and the report moves to the improvement process with a note linking the earlier reports. The scenario is hypothetical and not a customer result.

## Assign owners and due dates

An action without an owner and a date is a wish. The owner is the person who will make the change or see that it is made. The input is the action description. The decision is whether the owner accepts the action and the date. The record is the action entry. The exception to plan for is an owner who lacks the authority or budget to do what is needed.

Write each action so a stranger could tell when it is complete. “Improve guarding” is vague, and “fit interlocked guards to the two press stations and add them to the shift-start check” is clear. Where a team member cannot tell what finished looks like, ask for a rewrite before accepting.

Choose the owner by authority, not by convenience. The owner of an action to change a procedure should be someone who can change it, and the owner of an equipment change should be someone who controls the equipment. Where the right owner is above the safety function, the safety lead should present the action and ask for acceptance, and record the answer. If the owner declines or cannot act, escalate to a named person rather than letting the action wait.

Agree the date with the owner and write down the reason for it. A date imposed without agreement may be missed, and a date that is too loose may let risk linger. Where the right fix will take months, split the action into interim controls with early dates and a permanent change with a later one, and record each as its own entry so progress is visible.

**Illustrative example:** In the same hypothetical scenario, the maintenance lead accepts the guard fix, agrees a date and records it in the action entry. The scenario is invented.

Ask the vendor how due dates behave. The [ICC Safety page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/safety/) does not state notifications or reminders, so find out whether the owner is told when an action is assigned, whether anyone sees actions approaching or past their due date and who is told when a date is missed. If the product does not do this, decide how the plant will cover it, for example with a weekly review of open actions in the safety meeting.

Think about change requests. Dates move for good reasons, and the record should show who moved the date, when and why. A date that is changed repeatedly without a reason is itself a signal.

Make sure the owner can do the work. Ask whether the owner has the time, tools, budget and approval needed. If any of these is missing, the action needs support, and the safety lead's role is to find it or to report that it cannot be found. Record that outcome, since unresolved resourcing is a risk the plant should see.

## Keep the link to the incident

The value of linking an action to its incident is that anyone can follow the chain from the event to the response. The safety coordinator keeps that chain intact. The input is the incident and its actions. The decision is whether the incident can close. The record is the linked set of records. The exception to plan for is an action tracked somewhere else, so the chain breaks.

The [ICC Safety page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/safety/) states that the auditor view shows the incident and near-miss log with severity and status, floor reports including anonymous ones, and the corrective actions linked to each incident. That description sets a useful standard for any system: an auditor should be able to open an incident and see what was done about it, without searching through other files. Ask the vendor to show that view with a sample incident that has two actions, one complete and one open.

Make closure a decision, not an accident. The safety coordinator should review each incident at closure and confirm that the linked actions, the investigation and the review are all present. A short closure checklist, completed in the record, takes little time and makes gaps visible before an auditor sees them.

Check how closure works. A sensible rule is that an incident does not close while any of its actions are open, unless someone with authority records a reason. Ask whether the product enforces such a rule, warns about it or ignores it. If it ignores it, the plant needs a check of its own, such as a regular list of closed incidents that still have open actions.

Keep one list of actions. Tracking safety actions in a spreadsheet, quality actions in another system and audit findings in a third may make it harder to see the whole. The ICC page describes actions being carried into the Improvements module, which suggests one place for them, but the research reviewed for this guide does not describe how actions from other sources appear there. Ask how actions from audits and other sources are handled, and decide whether to consolidate.

Consider how the plant's own rules shape records. Where a joint health and safety committee exists, its recommendations and the employer's responses have their own requirements. The Government of Ontario's [guide to health and safety committees and representatives](https://www.ontario.ca/page/guide-health-and-safety-committees-and-representatives) describes these for Ontario workplaces, and plants elsewhere should confirm what applies to them. Where an action responds to a committee recommendation, link the two so the response can be traced.

Name an owner for the link check itself, since a chain that nobody inspects tends to weaken over time. The safety coordinator is a natural choice, with a backup who can cover absences and a calendar entry for the sample review. If an action is tracked outside the system, the safety coordinator records a cross-reference in the incident, such as the other system's reference number, and checks that action's status before the incident closes.

**Illustrative example:** In the same hypothetical scenario, the incident shows two linked actions, one complete and one open, so it stays open. The scenario is invented.

Review the links as part of the regular record review. Take a sample of recent incidents and check that each action is linked, that its status matches the facts and that closed incidents have closed actions. Record the result.

## Verify effectiveness

Completing an action is not the same as solving the problem. Verification asks whether the change worked. The safety lead owns it. The input is the closed action. The decision is whether the action was effective or the problem needs reopening. The record is an effectiveness check, with the checker's name and the date. The exception to plan for is a similar report appearing after the action is complete.

Decide in advance how effectiveness will be judged. For a guard fitted to a machine, the check might be an inspection that the guard is in place and used, together with a review of reports from that station over a defined period. For a change to a procedure, it might be observation of the task and a conversation with the people who do it. Write the method into the action so that the checker knows what to look for.

Set the timing for the check. Too soon, and the change has not yet been tested by normal work; too late, and a problem may continue unnoticed. Choose a period that fits the nature of the change and the frequency of the work, and record it. The plant should adjust the period as it learns.

Use the log. If the plant can search its incident record by location, equipment or category, the checker can look for new reports similar to the original. Ask the vendor how search and filtering work, and whether results can be saved or shared for a review meeting. The [ICC Safety page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/safety/) describes a log with severity and status, but the research reviewed does not describe search features, so test it with real examples.

The check should assess both whether the hazard is controlled and whether similar reports have stopped over the chosen period. A repeat report triggers a review and, where the action did not work, reopening it.

**Illustrative example:** In the same hypothetical scenario, a new report of the same fastener condition appears after the check period, so the action is reopened. The scenario is invented.

Handle a failed check without blame. If the problem has returned, reopen the action or raise a new one, and record why the first change was not enough. This is valuable information. It may show that the cause was misjudged, that the change was not followed or that conditions changed.

Keep verification honest. An effectiveness check that is always “effective” is probably not testing anything. Have someone other than the action owner perform it where possible, and review a sample of past checks to see whether any later reports contradicted them.

Report the results to the people who need them. A summary of actions closed and verified, and of those reopened, gives the safety lead and the leadership team a view of whether the system is working. Keep it simple and based on the log, so it can be repeated each period without much effort.
