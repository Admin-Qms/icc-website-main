## Review the workflow and evidence

A system that works at launch can drift. Reports may arrive late, ratings may vary between reviewers and actions may sit unchecked. A regular review of the whole workflow keeps small problems from becoming habits. The safety lead owns it. The input is a sample of records and the exception log. The decision is which improvements to prioritize. The record is a set of review findings. The exception to plan for is evidence that is too incomplete to assess.

Set the rhythm at the start. A quarterly review fits many plants, with a lighter monthly check of open items. Choose the period, name the reviewer and put the dates in the calendar, so the review does not depend on someone remembering.

Sample records rather than reading everything. Pick a set of reports from the period, such as ten, and follow each through the chain described in earlier chapters: how it was filed, when it was first reviewed, how it was rated, whether it was investigated, what action followed and whether the action was checked. Include an anonymous report, one with a disputed rating and one closed without action where they occurred in the period, and say so when one did not. If the evidence is too incomplete to assess, record the gap, who will fill it and by when, and hold off on conclusions about that point.

Record what the sample shows against a short list of questions:

- Were reports reviewed within the target?
- Do ratings match the scale's own descriptions?
- Do closures follow the plant's adopted rule, with linked actions complete or an authorized reason recorded?
- Were effectiveness checks done, and by someone other than the owner?
- Did the dashboard give the committee what it needed?

Set baselines before judging improvement. A figure such as the share of reports reviewed within target means little until the plant knows where it started. Record the first quarter's results as the baseline, and compare later periods against it rather than against an ideal. The [ICC Safety module page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/safety/) describes an auditor view with the log, severity, status and linked actions, which can support this kind of sample, but the plant should confirm what the system can filter and export.

Report the findings to the people who own the decisions. The safety lead shares a short summary with the operations manager and the committee, covering what was sampled, what was found and what will change. Keep it brief and in plain language.

Keep an exception log. Note each time the process failed in a way that mattered, such as a report found on paper after cutover, a rating changed without a reason or an action closed without a check. Review the log for repeats, and treat a repeated exception as a prompt to change the process or the settings.

Compare the results with the plant's earlier decisions and records, such as its definitions, severity scale, routing table and access rules. If the sample shows that people are working around one of them, ask why before changing it. The workaround may point to a rule that does not fit the work, or to training that did not reach a shift.

Decide what to improve first. List the findings, estimate the effort and the benefit, and pick a few for the next quarter, with an owner and a date for each. Record the choices and revisit them at the next review.

In a hypothetical review, three have no effectiveness check recorded, so the safety lead assigns an owner for the checks and revisits the sample the following quarter. ## Answer common questions without overclaiming

Questions arrive from supervisors, committees, auditors and leadership. The safety lead answers them, drawing on the sources the plant has actually verified. The input is the list of questions. The decision is to answer within the evidence. The record is a set of approved answers. The exception to plan for is a question that needs legal or regulatory interpretation beyond the sources; the safety lead records it as unresolved and refers it to a qualified adviser before approving an answer.

### What problem should incident and near-miss reporting software solve first?

Start with the gap that causes the most harm: reports that are not consistently logged, rated for severity or linked to corrective action. A tool that captures every report in one log, records a severity rating and connects an action addresses the plant's basic need. Features such as dashboards and committee views are valuable, but they depend on that foundation being in place. Test this with one workflow first: take a real report and check that it can move from the floor to a rated record and a linked action without leaving the system. If it cannot, adding more features will not fix the gap.

### What is the difference between an incident and a near-miss?

An incident is an event that caused harm, damage or a stoppage. A near-miss is an event that could have caused harm but did not. Plants should write their own definitions, add examples and decide how hazard reports fit, since a clear boundary lets floor staff classify a report without help and keeps counts meaningful. Review the definitions after the first quarter, using the reports the plant actually received, and add any examples that settled an argument so the next reviewer does not need to repeat it.

### How should severity be rated?

The [ICC Safety page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/safety/) states that severity is set from the level of harm. Define a short scale in the plant's own words, rate near-misses by the harm that could reasonably have occurred, test the scale with two raters and keep a record of any change, including who made it and why. Where reviewers disagree, use the disputed report as a worked example in the scale, so the boundary becomes clearer over time and new reviewers can learn from real cases instead of adjectives.

### Should anonymous reporting be allowed?

It is a policy decision for the plant. The ICC page describes an anonymous report form opened by a QR code on the floor, with no login. If the plant accepts anonymous reports, it should decide who reads them first, avoid identifying details, and plan how outcomes are shared without pointing to a reporter. The policy should be written down, shared with the floor and applied consistently, since an anonymous route that is promised but not respected will lose people's trust quickly.

### Which old logs should be migrated?

Migrate what serves a purpose: open actions, recent history that supports pattern review and records an auditor may ask about. Clean the data first, pilot a small import and check it against the originals. Keep the source files as the archive and record what was left out and why. Treat open actions with extra care, listing each one with its owner and date so nothing is lost in the move, and keep the old list until the new one has been checked against it.

### How should incidents link to corrective actions?

The ICC page says incidents that need corrective action are carried into the Improvements module, and that the auditor view shows the linked actions. A plant should decide which reports need action, give each action an owner and a date, keep the link to the incident and check effectiveness before closing. Where an action responds to a committee recommendation or an audit finding, link those records as well, so the response can be traced from any starting point.

### What evidence should be ready for an auditor?

Be ready to show the log with severity and status, the investigation and review for chosen incidents, linked actions with checks and the way workers are consulted. Rehearse with a colleague, and keep the gap list. Records should show what happened, including late items, without tidying. Decide in advance how an external reviewer will access the records, because the research reviewed for this guide does not describe external reviewer access, and ask the vendor to confirm it in writing.

### How do safety committees use the log?

A committee may use the log to review patterns and open actions; whether the dashboard filters by location or category is a plant requirement to test in a demonstration. In the Ontario workplaces the [Government of Ontario's guide](https://www.ontario.ca/page/guide-health-and-safety-committees-and-representatives) covers, committees have defined meeting and inspection duties, and plants elsewhere should check their own rules. Share a summary with the committee ahead of each meeting, and record the follow-ups it decides so the next meeting can check them.

### Does software guarantee ISO 45001 compliance?

No. The ICC page maps its safety module to ISO 45001:2018 clauses 10.2, 5.4 and 6.1.2, but a mapping points to possible evidence and does not guarantee that a clause is met. The plant remains responsible for its processes, its records and its own interpretation of the standard. Keep any statements about compliance modest and tie each to a record the plant can show, and have a qualified person confirm clause details against the standard itself.

### What should be prepared for a software demonstration?

Bring real sample reports, including an anonymous one, a disputed rating and one that needs action, plus the plant's acceptance criteria and open questions. Ask the vendor to run the plant's samples and confirm in writing what was shown. The next section describes this in more detail. Agree who will score each part of the session, and decide beforehand what result would let the evaluation continue.

## Prepare a focused ICC demonstration

A demonstration is most useful when the plant sets the agenda. The evaluation lead owns the preparation. The input is a scenario and a list of open questions. The decision is to proceed, ask for clarification or reject. The record is the demonstration results. The exception to plan for is a critical path that cannot be shown.

Agree an agenda with sample reports and time to test exceptions, such as missing fields or overdue actions.

Prepare three sample reports from the plant's own history, with names and details removed: a routine near-miss reported from the floor, an anonymous report and a report that needs corrective action, plus an existing open action from the plant's log to test its path and follow-up. Ask the vendor to run each from filing to a linked action, and to show the rating, the review, the dashboard view and the auditor view.

Bring the open questions the published [ICC Safety page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/safety/) does not answer: pricing, notifications or alerts, regulatory or authority reporting, how existing logs can be imported and how external reviewers get access. Ask for written answers, and note which are standard behavior and which depend on configuration.

Invite the right people. A floor reporter, an investigator, an action owner and a committee member will each see different things, so ask each to score the parts that matter to them. Collect their comments straight afterward, and compare them with the acceptance criteria.

Ask the vendor how the plant's own terminology, severity scale and routing would be set up, who does that configuration and how a change is requested afterward. 

Decide the outcome in advance. State what the demonstration must show for the plant to proceed, what would lead to a request for clarification and what would end the evaluation. If a path cannot be shown, record it against the acceptance criteria, and let its effect on the decision follow from whether the criterion was mandatory. Record the result and the reasons, and share them with the decision owner.
