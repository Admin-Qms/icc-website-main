## Share the right dashboard

A safety committee, a health and safety representative or a group of supervisors cannot act on what they cannot see. At the same time, safety records can be sensitive, so visibility should be designed, not left to chance. The safety lead owns this decision. The input is the dashboard options the software offers. The decision is what each group may see. The record is an access rule. The exception to plan for is a view that exposes more detail than a group should have.

The [ICC Safety module page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/safety/) describes a health and safety dashboard with its own permissions, separate from quality, and says the module is switched on per organization. It also says anonymous reports appear in the dashboard marked as anonymous, and its auditor view shows severity and status. Confirm in a demonstration which fields the dashboard itself displays. Ask the vendor to show the dashboard as a committee member would see it, and then as a floor supervisor, a quality manager and an administrator.

Decide what a committee needs to do its job. Counts by area, category and rating over time, a list of open actions with owners and dates, and a list of reports awaiting first review are usually enough to prepare a meeting. Names of individuals are usually not needed for that purpose. Where detail is needed to understand a case, the committee can ask the safety lead for it through a defined route, and the request can be recorded.

Take particular care with anonymous reports. The page says anonymous reports appear in the dashboard marked as anonymous. The plant should decide whether the committee sees them in full, in summary or only as counts, and should make sure that details such as location or time do not make a reporter easy to identify. Make the choice part of the anonymity policy described earlier, and tell the floor what the committee can see.

Separate the safety view from other views where the plant wants that. The page's description of separate permissions suggests this is possible, and a demonstration should show it. A quality manager who needs to see actions linked to safety events may not need to see the detail of injuries, and the reverse may also hold. Ask whether access can be limited by role, area or site, and how changes to access are recorded.

Test the dashboard with a real meeting. Prepare the next committee pack from it, and ask the chair whether it answers the questions the committee usually asks. If the chair still has to build a separate spreadsheet, the dashboard has not yet replaced the old process, and the criteria should say what is missing.

**Illustrative example:** An unnamed Canadian manufacturer lets committee members see counts, ratings and open actions by area, while names and free-text descriptions stay with the safety lead. A member who wants detail on one report asks the safety lead, who decides whether to share it. The scenario is hypothetical and not a customer result.

## Review patterns on a set rhythm

The [ICC Safety page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/safety/) says a safety committee may not see patterns early. A regular review is how a plant turns a log into foresight. Without a rhythm, patterns may go unnoticed or be recognized late.

The committee chair owns this review. The input is the log and the dashboard. The decision is which patterns to raise with the plant. The record is the meeting record. The exception to plan for is that the rules about committees differ outside Ontario, so the plant should confirm what applies at each site.

The Government of Ontario's [guide to health and safety committees and representatives](https://www.ontario.ca/page/guide-health-and-safety-committees-and-representatives) describes how committees work for the workplaces it covers. As described there, a joint health and safety committee meets at least once every three months, and a worker member inspects the workplace at least once a month. Those figures are Ontario's and apply to the workplaces the guide describes. A plant should check the current official page and the rules for its own province, because requirements differ by jurisdiction and by workplace size.

Whatever the legal minimum, a plant may choose a monthly look at the log rather than a quarterly one. Choose a rhythm that matches how fast conditions change, and make sure the log is ready beforehand. Ask the safety coordinator to prepare a standard pack from the dashboard, so the meeting spends its time discussing and not assembling.

Look for patterns of several kinds. Repeats in one location may point to a physical condition. Clusters on one shift may point to staffing or supervision. Repeated categories may suggest a gap in training or equipment. A rise in reports can mean more events, or can mean that reporting has improved, so interpret changes in counts with care and look at ratings and content, not just numbers.

Use the inspection findings alongside the log. Where a worker member inspects the workplace, the inspection notes and the reports may point at the same conditions. Bring them into the same discussion and record where they agree and where they differ. Ask the vendor how inspection findings can be recorded alongside reports; the research reviewed for this guide does not describe that for the safety module.

Record the outcome of each review in the meeting record: which reports and actions were examined, which patterns were raised and who will follow up. Where the meeting record and the incident log refer to each other by reference number, a new member or an auditor can follow a discussion back to the underlying reports.

**Illustrative example:** In a hypothetical meeting, the chair lists three repeat locations from the dashboard for discussion and assigns each a follow-up owner. The scenario is invented.

Decide, at the end of each review, what will be raised and who will follow up. A pattern that is discussed and then left has the same effect as one that was never seen. Record each follow-up in the meeting record, with an owner and a date, and check the list at the next meeting.

## Handle committee recommendations

Committees may make recommendations, and in the Ontario workplaces the guide covers, the employer has duties when they are in writing. The plant's process should make sure recommendations are recorded, answered and tracked. The operations manager normally owns the response, with help from the safety lead. The input is the written recommendation. The decision is whether to accept it, accept it in part or explain why not. The record is the written response. The exception to plan for is a response that is overdue.

The Government of Ontario's [guide to health and safety committees and representatives](https://www.ontario.ca/page/guide-health-and-safety-committees-and-representatives) states that an employer must respond in writing within 21 days to written recommendations from the committee. That timeline applies to the Ontario workplaces the guide covers. Plants elsewhere should confirm the rule that applies to them, and all plants should check the current wording on the official page, since the research behind this guide could not retrieve the full page directly.

Build a simple process around the deadline. When a recommendation arrives, record its date and assign it to an owner. Set a reminder at an intermediate point and a target for the response well before the limit. Draft the response, review it with the safety lead and send it, then record the date. Check the current requirements for what a response must contain and when it is due.

Make responses useful. A response that says only "noted" does little for the committee. A better response says whether the recommendation is accepted, what will be done, who is responsible and by when, or why the plant will not act and what it will do instead. Where a recommendation leads to a corrective action, link the two records so the committee can follow the result.

Distinguish a recommendation from a report. A report describes an event or condition, and a recommendation proposes a change, so the record should link a recommendation to the reports that prompted it. That link lets the response address the reasoning directly.

**Illustrative example:** In the same hypothetical scenario, a written recommendation about the crossing is logged with its date, and the written response is recorded with a response date. The scenario is invented.

Track recommendations together with actions. A recommendation that is accepted becomes an action with an owner and a date, as described earlier. Review the list at each meeting, including recommendations still awaiting response and any that were declined, so the committee can see the whole picture.

Ask the vendor how the product handles recommendations. The research reviewed for this guide does not describe a dedicated record for committee recommendations, so the plant should ask whether they can be logged as a type of action, how dates are tracked and who is told when a response is due. If the product does not cover this, decide how the plant will track recommendations, perhaps with a simple register kept next to the incident log and linked by reference number.

## Support worker participation

A log is only as good as the willingness of people to use it. Participation depends on trust, on how easy reporting is and on whether people see results. The supervisor plays a large part, and the plant's leaders set the tone.

The ICC page maps its safety module to ISO 45001:2018 clause 5.4, consultation and participation of workers, as well as clauses 10.2 and 6.1.2. A mapping is a pointer to the module's possible evidence, not a statement that software alone meets a clause. Use it as a prompt to ask what evidence of worker consultation the plant needs and whether the log can supply part of it. See the [ICC Safety page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/safety/) for the published mapping, and confirm the details in a demonstration.

Close the loop with reporters. The supervisor is the natural person to tell a team what came of their reports, in a short and regular way. The input is the set of closed reports. The decision is what to share with reporters, within the plant's rules for privacy. The record is a feedback entry or a note in the team's meeting record. The exception to plan for is an anonymous reporter who cannot be reached.

Show results where people will see them. A board near the entry, a short item in the shift briefing or a monthly summary can say how many reports were received, what was fixed and what is still open. Keep names and sensitive details out. Where the plant wants to describe a specific fix, check that the description does not point to the reporter.

Involve workers in design and review. Ask a few floor reporters and the worker members of the committee to try the reporting form, the dashboard and the feedback routine, and to say what is awkward. Their comments may catch problems that managers miss, such as wording that is unclear or an access point that does not work in practice. The [Ontario guide](https://www.ontario.ca/page/guide-health-and-safety-committees-and-representatives) says at least half of the members must be workers who do not have managerial functions, in the workplaces it covers.

Check that feedback works: ask reporters whether what they see shows what action followed their reports.

**Illustrative example:** In a hypothetical plant, a board near the entry shows fixes made from floor reports, without names or locations that could identify anyone. The scenario is invented.

Watch for signs that participation is falling. A drop in near-miss reports after a stricter response to an incident may show that people are afraid of blame, and a rise in anonymous reports may point to the same concern. Look at the numbers together with what people say, and respond with changes in practice, not just reminders to report.

Train supervisors in how to receive a report. A supervisor who thanks the reporter, logs the report and says what will happen next supports the process, and one who responds with irritation undermines it. Include this in the supervisor's training and check it in the plant's regular reviews.
