## Build a workflow-based shortlist

The acceptance criteria from the previous chapter are the filter. A shortlist built from them looks different from one built from brochures, because the question changes from "what does the product list?" to "can the product carry one real report from the floor to a closed action?"

Start by deciding how many options the team can examine properly. Three is usually enough. Each demonstration takes preparation, sample data and the time of people who also have production work, so a long list produces shallow tests. Narrow first on the mandatory criteria: floor reporting, one log, a recorded severity, a linked corrective action and a view an auditor can follow.

Published descriptions are a legitimate starting point, as long as the team treats them as claims to be tested. The ICC [Safety module page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/safety/) describes incident and near-miss reporting, an anonymous report form opened by a QR code on the floor, a health and safety dashboard with its own permissions, and incidents that need corrective action to be carried into the Improvements module. Each is a sentence on a page. Each becomes a line in the demonstration plan, to be seen working with the plant's own sample reports.

Weigh the mandatory criteria above everything else. A product that passes nine desirable criteria and fails the linked corrective action has not met the plant's need, and the sheet should show that plainly. Mark mandatory rows in a distinct way so a long column of passes cannot hide one missing essential step.

Apply the same discipline to every vendor. Build a single comparison sheet with one row per criterion and one column per vendor, and fill the cells only with what was shown or promised in writing. A cell reading "described on website" is different from a cell reading "demonstrated with the plant's sample report," and the sheet should keep the two apart.

Ask each vendor three screening questions before booking a full session:

1. Can the product show a report moving from filing to a linked action, using the plant's wording?
2. Which parts of that path are standard, and which depend on configuration or custom work?
3. Who configures the product, and what does that cost in time?

A vendor who cannot answer the second question clearly is describing a product that may not match the path. Drop it early rather than discovering the gap in week six.

Pay attention to what the page does not say. The ICC page, for example, does not state pricing, notifications or alerts, injury classification beyond severity, or regulatory reporting. Those are not defects in the page; they are questions for the sheet. Listing them now prevents a pleasant demonstration from hiding a gap that matters to the plant.

Finally, check fit with the rest of the plant's systems. Safety records rarely live alone. They touch training records, equipment records and the action tracking used for audits. Note which of these the team expects the same product to cover, and which will stay elsewhere.

## Test the decision points

A demonstration is only useful if it tests decisions. Provide each vendor with the same set of sample reports in advance, and ask them to run those reports rather than their own tidy examples. Include at least four cases:

- A routine near-miss reported from the floor.
- An anonymous report.
- A report whose severity two people would rate differently.
- A report that needs corrective action and one that does not.

Then watch for the five elements that describe every decision: who acts, what they see, what they decide, what record remains and what happens in an exception. If a step cannot show a record of the decision, mark it as missing. Software that moves a report along without recording who decided what leaves the plant where it started, with less visible gaps.

Severity deserves particular attention. The [ICC Safety page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/safety/) says severity is set from the level of harm. That raises practical questions the demonstration should answer. How many levels exist, and can the plant define them? Who is allowed to set or change a rating? Is the original rating kept visible when someone changes it? Does a near-miss take a rating based on the harm that could have occurred? A page can state the principle; only a session with real reports shows how it behaves.

Test the exceptions as carefully as the main path. Ask the vendor to show a report that arrives without a required field, a report assigned to someone who has left, and an action that passes its due date. Ask what the user sees in each case. Many products work well until the first exception, and the plant's reports are full of exceptions.

Use a scoring sheet with three possible results for each criterion: shown, partly shown, or not shown. Add a notes column for the evaluator's own words. Score separately by at least two people, such as a safety coordinator and a floor supervisor, then compare. Differences between scorers are valuable, because they show where a feature looked adequate to one role and awkward to another.

**Illustrative example:** An unnamed Canadian manufacturer gives two vendors the same four sample reports. In the first session the vendor shows an anonymous report, a rating and a linked action, but a change to a rating overwrites the original with no record of who changed it. The team scores the criterion "partly shown" and records the gap in writing, then asks the vendor to confirm in writing whether rating history can be kept. The second vendor shows rating history but cannot show anonymous reporting from the floor. Neither is eliminated at this stage; each receives a list of written questions.

Give each session a fixed agenda and a time limit. Allow the vendor a short introduction, then move straight to the sample reports. A session that spends its first hour on slides leaves too little time for exceptions, and exceptions are where products differ.

Close each session by asking the vendor to confirm in writing what was demonstrated, what was configuration and what was not shown. That record protects the plant from memory drift when the decision comes weeks later.

## Examine evidence, permissions and AI use

Evidence and access decide whether a record can be trusted and who may see it. Evaluate both with the same sample reports.

Begin with permissions. Safety information can be sensitive: a report may name a person, describe an injury or touch a dispute. The [ICC Safety page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/safety/) describes a health and safety dashboard with its own permissions, separate from quality, and says the module is switched on per organization. Use that description as a prompt. Ask who can file a report, who can read it, who can rate it, who can investigate and who can close it. Ask whether those rights can differ by site or department, and how access changes are recorded.

Anonymous reports raise a particular question. The page says an anonymous report form opens from a QR code on the floor with no login needed, and that anonymous reports appear in the dashboard marked as anonymous. The plant must still decide who reads them first, how follow-up works when the reporter cannot be reached and whether anonymous reports are visible to a committee. Ask the vendor to show how an anonymous report appears to each role.

Record the answers on the comparison sheet in the evaluator's own words, with the name of the person at the vendor who gave them, so a later question about what was promised has a clear answer.

Next, examine evidence. For a sample report, ask the vendor to show everything an auditor could see. The page describes an auditor view with the incident and near-miss log showing severity and status, floor reports including anonymous ones, and corrective actions linked to each incident. Check that the view matches what the plant would want to show. Can a reviewer open one report and see the rating, the investigation, the action and the closure in one place? Can the history be exported or printed if an external reviewer asks?

Finally, ask how artificial intelligence is used. The page states that every AI output in the software is a suggestion a person reviews before it enters a controlled record. That is a useful design principle for any product, and the plant should ask for specifics. What does the AI do in the safety module, if anything? Where does a person review it? Is there a record of who reviewed it? Treat any AI feature as a draft assistant, not an approver, and do not accept a claim that it can decide severity or close an action without a person.

Think about retention and deletion too. Safety records may need to be kept for a defined period, and the plant should know how long the product keeps them, who can delete a record and whether deletion leaves a trace.

Check attachments as well. Photos from the floor often make up much of the evidence, so confirm that a photo added by a reporter stays with the report and is visible to the investigator who needs it.

Keep a list of unresolved answers. A criterion with an unresolved answer on permissions or evidence is not ready to pass, however good the rest of the demonstration looked.

## Compare setup, cost and support

Once the path works, compare what it takes to get there. The cheapest product to license can become the most expensive if the plant must configure and support it alone.

The [ICC Safety page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/safety/) describes setup in four steps: process mapping, with named owners; configuration of fields, checklists, terminology and routing; onboarding of standards; and deployment to the floor with training. It also says a certified consultant reviews the configuration through to audit. Use a description like this to build a comparison template for every vendor: who does each step, how long it takes, what the plant must supply and what the vendor takes responsibility for.

Pricing is not stated on the page, so ask for it in writing. Request the full cost over three years rather than a monthly figure alone, including setup, training, configuration changes and any extra charges for sites, users or modules. Ask what happens to the data, and in what format it is returned, if the plant leaves.

Support deserves its own questions. Who answers a question from a floor supervisor on night shift? What are the response times? How are changes to forms or severity scales requested, and who can approve them? Does support include help adjusting the workflow after the first audit?

Ask about training. The page describes training as part of deployment, so find out what the vendor provides for floor reporters, investigators and administrators, and how new hires are covered after the first rollout. A product that only the original project team understands becomes fragile as soon as people move on.

Check the vendor's own record of change as well. Ask how often the product changes and how customers are told about changes that affect safety records. A product that alters its forms without notice can undermine a log that auditors rely on.

Finally, test references carefully, and do so before the decision rather than after it. Ask for contacts at plants of similar size and process, and prepare the same questions for each: what took longer than expected, what was configured and what still depends on manual work. Treat a reference as a view from one plant, not as proof about every plant.

Compile the findings into a short recommendation for the decision owner. List the criteria that passed, those partly shown, the written commitments obtained and the open items. If the recommendation depends on an unanswered question, say so plainly and name who will resolve it. Provincial rules vary, and the plant should confirm with the relevant authority what its records must support; the Government of Ontario's [guide to health and safety committees and representatives](https://www.ontario.ca/page/guide-health-and-safety-committees-and-representatives) is one example of the provincial material the team may need to read alongside the vendor's answers. A good recommendation lets the owner decide with the gaps in view.
