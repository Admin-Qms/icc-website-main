## Set role-based learning tasks

As a proposed practice, make training follow the work. Each role touches the audit workflow at different points, so each role needs different practice. A planner builds the program, an auditor records observations and findings, an action owner submits evidence, a checker verifies it, and management reads reports.

The [ISO 9001 Auditing Practices Group guidance on internal audits](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-InternalAudit.pdf) says auditors of the internal audit process evaluate competence, objectivity and impartiality, risk-based planning, management involvement, and how audit outcomes are used. Training for the new system should therefore be recorded in a way that supports the team's own view of competence. The following is a proposed approach.

Start from the tasks the team mapped earlier. For each role, list the tasks it performs in the system, and turn each into a learning task: do the thing with sample records, then show the result to someone who can judge it. Reading a manual is not a task; opening a finding, attaching evidence and submitting it for checking is.

Include one exception per role, such as a rejected submission or an unavailable owner, so that people practice the awkward case as well as the normal one.

A typical set might include:

- **Planner:** create a scope, assign an auditor with a conflict check, and move an audit with a reason.
- **Auditor:** prepare from prior findings, record observations, link evidence to a criterion, and trace one audit plan reference through its finding to follow-up evidence.
- **Action owner:** accept an assignment, submit evidence, and respond to a rejection.
- **Checker:** review evidence, accept or reject with a reason, and check an effectiveness item.
- **Management:** read a program report and trace a figure to its records.

The [audit planning guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-Audit_Planning.pdf) says to consider evening and night shifts. If auditors or owners work those shifts, schedule learning tasks at times they can attend, or provide an equivalent route. Ask the vendor about training materials and a sandbox whose sample records can be reset between sessions.

### Roles, inputs and decisions for this step

| Item | What to define |
|---|---|
| Role | The workflow owner, here the quality manager, with a trainer who knows the system |
| Inputs | The responsibility table, the task list per role, a sandbox with sample records and the shift pattern |
| Decision | Whether a person may use the system for a role; the quality manager accepts that person when each learning task is completed and observed, and returns the person for more practice otherwise |
| Record | A learning record per person, with tasks, dates, observer, decision and, if returned, the reason |
| Exception | A person who cannot attend or does not pass a task; the quality manager decides whether to reschedule, give a different route or limit access, and records the disposition |

**Illustrative example:** An unnamed Canadian manufacturer asks a vendor to demonstrate role-based practice with representative sample records: an auditor attaches evidence and a checker rejects it. The team then asks the vendor to run the exception, a night-shift owner who misses the session and receives an alternative route, with the quality manager's decision recorded.

## Pilot with representative users

A user pilot differs from the data pilot used for migration. Here the question is whether real people can do real work in the system, with the records and constraints they actually have.

Choose pilot users who represent the roles, shifts, and varied comfort with software. The [audit planning guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-Audit_Planning.pdf) says plans should be developed with the auditee where possible, so include at least one process owner who will be audited, not only the quality team. Include someone from an evening or night shift if the audit program covers those shifts.

Run one real audit, or a close rehearsal, end to end: plan, prepare, record, find, assign, submit evidence, check and report. As a proposed approach, log each issue with the role, the task, what happened and the effect, then sort issues into three groups: the system cannot do it, the configuration needs changing, or the user needs training.

Ask pilot users also to attempt exceptions, such as a rejected action, a reassigned owner and a reopened finding, to see how the system handles them. Also trace one audit plan reference from plan to finding to follow-up evidence, and test a finding with no plan reference.

Pilot users can also test the controls the team chose earlier. Ask the auditor to try to close a finding they raised, and ask an owner to look at another area's records. Whether the system blocks these actions depends on the rules the team set, so the point is to confirm that the rules work as intended.

Decide the pilot's scope and limits in advance. A small pilot may not reveal every problem, especially volume or timing issues, and the pilot report should say so. Define what would count as success before the pilot begins, such as every pilot user completing each task in their role without outside help or with a logged exception.

### Roles, inputs and decisions for this step

| Item | What to define |
|---|---|
| Role | The workflow owner, here the quality manager, who runs the pilot; pilot users perform the tasks |
| Inputs | The configured system, the learning records, a real or rehearsal audit and the success measures |
| Decision | Whether the pilot succeeded; the quality manager accepts it when each success measure is met or a logged exception is approved by top management, and extends or repeats it otherwise |
| Record | The issue log with role, task, effect, group, owner and fix; the pilot report with outcome, decision-maker, rationale and approved exceptions |
| Exception | A pilot user cannot complete a task; the quality manager decides whether it is a training, configuration or system problem and records the disposition, and top management decides if a system problem blocks rollout |

**Illustrative example:** The hypothetical team asks a vendor to show a pilot audit using representative users: a planner, an auditor, an owner from the evening shift and a checker. It then asks the vendor to run the exception: the owner cannot find the submit-evidence step, the quality manager classifies it as a configuration problem, and the decision is recorded.

## Manage staged deployment

Staging may limit the effect of problems and let the team learn first, but it must not delay priority audits, which can stay in the old process where needed. A staged deployment brings in groups of users or parts of the program in a planned order.

There are several ways to stage. The team might start with one area and add others, start with one audit type and add more, or start with planning and reporting before findings and follow-up. The [audit planning guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-Audit_Planning.pdf) says important areas, areas with recent change, and areas with past nonconformity should be scheduled as a priority, so one option is to begin with an area that has few open findings and add priority areas once the process is steady. That is a proposed approach, and the team should choose the order that fits its risk.

For each stage, define what must be true before it starts and what must be true before the next one begins. A stage plan typically has an entry check, a set of users, a start date, a support route, and an exit check. Keep the old process available for work already in progress, and say which audits finish in the old process and which begin in the new one.

Decide in advance how each stage will be judged, for example by the number of users who complete their tasks and the issues still open. Write those measures down before the stage starts.

Name a person users can ask first. Ask the vendor what support is available during rollout, which channels exist, and how urgent problems are handled. Keep a list of known issues and workarounds.

For each stage, tell affected users what changes, from what date and where to get help. Confirm that audit plan references carry across, so findings and follow-up evidence already in progress stay linked.

The [internal audit guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-InternalAudit.pdf) points to management involvement. Rollout is a reasonable place for it: management can approve each stage, read the status, and decide when a problem is serious enough to pause.

### Roles, inputs and decisions for this step

| Item | What to define |
|---|---|
| Role | The workflow owner, here the quality manager, who runs the stages; top management approves each start |
| Inputs | The pilot report, the stage plan, the support route and the known-issues list |
| Decision | Whether to start the next stage; top management approves when the stage's entry check and the previous stage's exit check (for the first stage, the pilot report) are met and support is in place, and holds the stage otherwise |
| Record | The stage plan with entry and exit checks, dates, approvers and the status of each check |
| Exception | A serious problem appears during a stage; top management decides whether to pause, roll back that stage to the old process or continue with a workaround, and the quality manager records it |

**Illustrative example:** The hypothetical team asks a vendor to demonstrate a plan for two stages: machining first, then assembly. It then asks the vendor to run the exception: findings from machining fail to appear in a report, top management pauses the second stage, and the decision is recorded.

## Evaluate adoption and workload

After rollout, the team needs to know whether the system is used as intended and whether it has changed the effort of audit work. This guide does not claim any time saving, cost reduction, or other result for any product, so the team should measure its own.

A proposed adoption indicator is that records are created in the system, not in side files, apart from approved exceptions. Before evaluating, define the measures, targets, review period and treatment of approved exceptions. Useful measures include whether a plan reference leads to its findings and follow-up evidence. Useful indicators include whether audits are planned in the system, whether findings have linked evidence, whether actions are submitted and checked there, and whether reports are drawn from it. Side spreadsheets and email threads suggest part of the workflow has not moved.

Workload is harder. Before the rollout, estimate how long the main tasks took, using the team's own records or a short log, and repeat the log afterward. Label numbers as the team's own estimates and state the method. Two weeks of logging by a few people is a possible starting point, but the team should check that its sample and both periods are comparable in audit activity. Do not generalize from one team.

Ask users directly, too. A short survey or conversation can show which tasks still feel awkward and why. Combine the answers with the issue log, and decide which items need training, configuration or a request to the vendor.

Review the rollout against the [audit planning guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-Audit_Planning.pdf), which says a program can change while achieving its intent. If adoption is low in an area, the team may need to adjust the stage plan, add support, or change the configuration. Record each change and the reason.

### Roles, inputs and decisions for this step

| Item | What to define |
|---|---|
| Role | The workflow owner, here the quality manager, with top management reviewing the results |
| Inputs | Adoption indicators, workload logs, user feedback, the issue log and the stage records |
| Decision | Whether rollout is complete; top management accepts it when the adoption indicators are met and open issues have owners and dates, and requests further action otherwise |
| Record | The evaluation report with indicators, workload method and estimates, outcome, decision-maker, rationale, approved exceptions and actions agreed |
| Exception | A group still using side files or a workload that rose; the quality manager decides whether to retrain, reconfigure or ask the vendor for help, and records the disposition |

**Illustrative example:** The hypothetical team asks a vendor to show an adoption view for one stage: audits planned, findings with linked evidence, and actions checked. It then asks the vendor to run the exception: a group still tracking actions in a spreadsheet, with the quality manager deciding on retraining and recording it.
