## Distinguishing findings and observations

As a working definition for this guide, an observation records what the auditor saw, read, or was told in enough detail for someone else to check it, and a finding is a judgment drawn from observations and measured against criteria. The software should keep the two apart, because a reviewer needs to see what the judgment rests on.

The [ISO 9001 Auditing Practices Group guidance on internal audits](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-InternalAudit.pdf) says auditors of the internal audit process look at how audit outcomes are used to evaluate QMS effectiveness and identify improvement. As a proposed working approach, the team's own procedure sets the categories, for example, a nonconformity, an improvement opportunity, or a positive practice. Ask the vendor whether categories can be configured, renamed, and retired, and whether a changed category keeps its history.

ICC publishes a [description of its audits and management review module](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/audits-management-review/) that mentions findings with owners and closure status. These are published descriptions only. Treat them as evaluation questions: can a finding be created from an observation, and can the product show the observation behind it?

Consistency matters more than the label. Give two reviewers the same three sample observations and ask each to classify them using the team's definitions. If they disagree, investigate the definitions, the sample observations, and the reviewers' understanding before configuring anything.

A clear finding statement names the process, the criterion, and the evidence in plain language. Ask whether the product offers structured fields for those parts or only one text box, and how a reviewer sees a statement that omits one of them.

### What to ask the vendor to show

- A finding created from one or more observations, with the link visible from both sides.
- A category changed after review, with a record of who changed it and why.
- An observation that does not become a finding, kept as part of the audit record.
- A finding tied to the audit plan reference, so it can be traced from the plan and later to its follow-up evidence.

### Roles, inputs and decisions for this step

| Item | What to define |
|---|---|
| Role | The workflow owner, here the quality manager, with the lead auditor proposing findings |
| Inputs | The observations, the criteria, the team's category definitions, and the audit plan reference |
| Decision | Whether an observation becomes a finding and which category applies; the quality manager accepts it when the cited criterion and observations fit the category definitions, and returns it to the lead auditor otherwise |
| Record | The finding, its category, the linked observations, the decision-maker, and, if returned, the reason |
| Exception | An observation that fits no category or sits between two; the quality manager decides the category or requests more evidence; if neither resolves it, the observation is held and escalated to top management, and the disposition is recorded |

**Illustrative example:** An unnamed Canadian manufacturer asks a vendor to turn two observations from a machining audit into one finding, using representative records. The team then tests the exception: an observation that fits no category is held for review, and the quality manager's decision is recorded. The scenario is hypothetical and describes no customer result.

## Confirming supporting evidence

Before a finding is issued, the team should confirm that the evidence supports it. The [internal audit guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-InternalAudit.pdf) notes that the organization defines audit frequency, duration, and scope, and that auditors of the audit process look at objectivity and impartiality. A confirmation step gives the team a way to show that findings rest on evidence, not on impressions.

The confirmation should also keep the audit plan reference, so the finding stays tied to the audit that produced it and to the follow-up evidence that will later answer it.

A confirmation check can be simple. The reviewer asks whether the evidence is relevant to the criterion, whether it is sufficient to support the finding, and whether it can be found again. Ask the vendor to show that evidence stays attached to the finding and that the reviewer can see who attached it and when. Sufficiency remains the reviewer's recorded judgment.

The ICC [module description](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/audits-management-review/) mentions a single audit workspace for checklist, findings, and evidence. Ask the vendor to show how a reviewer, starting from a finding, reaches the attached evidence, the observation behind it, and the criterion, in the same place. The question is whether the reviewer must collect those items from separate screens or files.

Also ask how the product handles a finding with weak evidence. The reviewer should be able to return the finding to the auditor with a reason, and the return should be recorded. A product that allows a finding to be issued with no evidence attached makes the confirmation step easy to skip.

Sufficiency is a judgment, so record who made it. Evidence attached to a finding should show its source, its date, and the person who attached it. If the evidence is a file, ask whether reviewers can open it without special software, and what happens to the finding if the file is later replaced. A replaced file should leave the earlier version recoverable if the team's procedure requires it.

### Roles, inputs and decisions for this step

| Item | What to define |
|---|---|
| Role | The workflow owner, here the quality manager, with a second reviewer who did not collect the evidence |
| Inputs | The draft finding, the attached evidence, the linked observations, and the criterion |
| Decision | Whether the evidence supports the finding; the second reviewer accepts when the evidence is relevant, sufficient in the reviewer's judgment, and retrievable, and returns the finding with a reason otherwise |
| Record | The confirmation entry with reviewer, date, decision, and, if returned, the reason and the auditor's response |
| Exception | Evidence that cannot be retrieved, or a reviewer who disagrees with the auditor; the quality manager decides whether to collect more evidence or withdraw the finding, and records the disposition; a finding is issued only with accessible evidence and a recorded review of its relevance and sufficiency, and is otherwise held or withdrawn |

**Illustrative example:** The hypothetical team asks a vendor to issue a finding with no evidence attached. A useful result is a visible warning and a block on issuance until evidence is attached and reviewed, so the finding is held. The team then tests the return path: the second reviewer returns a finding because a photograph has no date, and the auditor's reply is stored beside the return.

## Assigning action ownership

A finding needs someone responsible for the response. The [audit planning guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-Audit_Planning.pdf) says audit plans should be developed with the auditee where possible, and the [internal audit guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-InternalAudit.pdf) says the organization should use past audit results when planning later audits. As a proposed approach, assign response ownership to the area concerned and keep it visible over time.

Ownership has several parts that software should keep separate: the person who owns the area, the person who owns the response, and the person who approves it. In some organizations these are the same person; in others they differ. The product should allow the team's pattern, and it should show the current owner without hiding earlier ones.

Each assignment should carry the finding and audit plan references, so follow-up evidence added later attaches to the same chain.

Ask the vendor to show the following with representative records:

1. A finding assigned to a role and then to a named person, with the role still visible if the person changes.
2. An owner who disagrees with the assignment and requests reassignment, with the request recorded.
3. A finding with a response due date, and how the product shows that date to the owner.
4. A backup owner who can act when the owner is absent.

The planning guidance notes that audits may be timed to suit production cycles. Owners face the same constraint, so the response date should be agreed with them where possible and changes to it should leave a record. A response date set only by the auditor may be missed for practical reasons the auditor did not know.

Ask also how the product shows workload. An owner with many open findings may need a different due date from one with few.

Keep ownership of the response separate from verification. The person who proposes and carries out the action should not be the only person who verifies it.

### Roles, inputs and decisions for this step

| Item | What to define |
|---|---|
| Role | The workflow owner, here the quality manager, who assigns; the process owner accepts or contests |
| Inputs | The confirmed finding, the responsibility table, current workload, and the production calendar |
| Decision | Who owns the response and by when; the quality manager accepts the assignment when the owner agrees and has authority over the area, and reassigns it otherwise |
| Record | The assignment with owner, backup, due date, the decision-maker, and any reassignment reason |
| Exception | No one accepts ownership, or the owner is unavailable; top management decides who takes it, and the quality manager records the disposition |

**Illustrative example:** The hypothetical team asks a vendor to assign a finding to the machining supervisor role, then replace the person in that role. The finding should keep its history. For the exception, the supervisor declines ownership and no one else accepts it, so top management decides who takes it and the decision is recorded.

## Resolving disputed findings

Auditees will sometimes disagree with a finding. The product should help the team handle disagreement openly, with a record of what was said and what was decided, so that a dispute does not turn into a silent change.

The [internal audit guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-InternalAudit.pdf) points to objectivity and impartiality as qualities auditors of the audit process examine. A dispute process can support both when the person who decides is not the auditor who raised the finding and when the auditee's view is recorded in full. As a proposed approach, the team defines its own dispute procedure.

A workable path has four parts: the auditee states the disagreement and the reason; the auditor responds; a reviewer who was not involved decides; and the decision is recorded with its basis. Possible outcomes are that the finding stands, is revised, is withdrawn, or is held while more evidence is collected.

Ask the vendor to show a dispute with representative records:

- The auditee raises a disagreement with the finding, and the product keeps their wording.
- The auditor's response and the reviewer's decision appear in the same record.
- A revised finding keeps the original text visible.
- A withdrawn finding remains in the audit record, with the reason.

A disputed finding keeps its audit plan reference and its follow-up evidence link, so a revision or withdrawal changes what the owner must do without breaking the chain.

The ICC [description of findings with owners and closure status](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/audits-management-review/) suggests that status is shown, but it does not describe a dispute path. Ask whether a disputed status exists, and whether it pauses the response clock or leaves it running. Either may suit a team, but the choice should be deliberate and recorded.

The team should decide in advance how long an auditee has to dispute a finding and who is told when the dispute is closed. The product should record the date each step happened.

### Roles, inputs and decisions for this step

| Item | What to define |
|---|---|
| Role | The workflow owner, here the quality manager, who appoints an independent reviewer; the auditee and auditor state their positions |
| Inputs | The finding, the evidence, the auditee's disagreement, and the auditor's response |
| Decision | Whether the finding stands, is revised, withdrawn, or held; the independent reviewer decides, and the quality manager accepts the decision when the basis is recorded and returns it for more detail otherwise |
| Record | The dispute entry with each position, the decision, the basis, the decision-maker, and the date |
| Exception | The quality manager is the auditor or the auditee: top management appoints another reviewer and an uninvolved person to accept or return that decision, and records the delegation |

**Illustrative example:** The hypothetical team asks a vendor to record an auditee's disagreement, the auditor's reply, and a reviewer's decision to revise the finding, with the original text retained. For the exception, the quality manager raised the finding, so top management appoints another reviewer and an uninvolved person to accept or return the decision, with the delegation recorded.
