## The operational problem

An internal audit is only as useful as the chain of records behind it. Someone decides what to audit and when. An auditor prepares, visits the work, and writes down what was seen. A finding is raised, an owner responds, and later someone checks that the response worked.

Each link in that chain can end up somewhere different: the schedule in a spreadsheet, the checklist in a document, the notes in a notebook, the findings in an email thread, and the follow-up in somebody's memory. The audit happens, but the connection between plan, evidence, finding, and closure is hard to see.

This guide is for Canadian quality and operations teams that want to repair that connection and are considering internal audit software to help. It treats software as a means to an end.

The end is an audit program in which every audit has a plan, every finding rests on recorded observations, every action has an owner, and every closure has evidence. Software can support that program, but it cannot create it. A team that has not agreed on who schedules audits, who may close a finding, and what counts as acceptable evidence will carry the same confusion into a new tool.

The vocabulary and expectations in this guide draw on published guidance from the ISO 9001 Auditing Practices Group, a joint effort of ISO/TC 176 and the International Accreditation Forum. Its paper on [internal audits](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-InternalAudit.pdf) explains that clause 9.2.2 of ISO 9001 asks the organization to plan, establish, implement, and maintain an audit program, including frequency, methods, responsibilities, planning requirements, and reporting.

The same paper notes that ISO 9001 does not set audit frequency, duration, or scope. The organization defines them, and it should consider the importance of the processes concerned, changes affecting the organization, and the results of previous audits.

Those points matter for software buyers. If the standard leaves frequency and scope to the organization, then a product cannot decide them for you. At best, it can record your decisions, remind the right people, and make the reasoning visible.

The paper also carries a disclaimer: it has not been through an ISO, TC 176, or IAF endorsement process and is provided for educational purposes. This guide uses it the same way, as a practical reference and not as a requirement.

A second paper, on [audit planning](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-Audit_Planning.pdf), separates two levels of planning: the audit program, which covers the period as a whole, and the audit plan for a single audit.

It suggests scheduling important areas, areas with recent change, and areas with past nonconformity first, and it recommends recording the justification when a lower-risk area is audited less often. Both levels of planning leave records, and both should be easy to find later.

> **Key Consideration:** Write your own operational problem in one or two sentences before reading further. Every chapter that follows is easier to use when you can compare it with a problem you have named.

**Illustrative example:** An unnamed Canadian manufacturer of machined components runs its internal audits from a yearly spreadsheet. Checklists are kept as separate files, findings are reported by email, and corrective actions are tracked in another log. When a customer asks how a particular finding was resolved, the quality manager spends an afternoon assembling the answer.

The team describes its problem as audit records that exist but cannot be connected, so the program cannot show what it found, what was done, or whether it worked. All situations in this guide are hypothetical and describe no real organization or result.

## How to use this guide

The guide has nine chapters and this introduction. They follow the life of an audit and the project to bring software into the workflow, and they are written to be read in order. Each chapter can also be used alone when a team reaches that stage, provided the reader has first written a short problem statement and a list of owners.

The first two chapters prepare the decision. The chapter on mapping the current workflow asks you to define the problem, trace the records and handoffs that an audit actually follows, assign owners to decisions, and set acceptance criteria. The chapter on evaluating software capabilities then turns those results into a requirements sheet, a scripted demonstration, a review of evidence and permissions, and a comparison that can be defended to management.

The next four chapters follow an audit through the workflow you have designed:

1. **Planning the internal audit program** covers scope, the audit calendar, auditor assignment, and the rules for changing the schedule when production or risk changes.
2. **Collecting and connecting audit evidence** covers checklists, observations, and the links between what was seen and the criteria it was measured against.
3. **Recording and reviewing findings** separates observations from findings, sets category definitions, and describes confirmation with the auditee and the handling of disputes.
4. **Following findings through closure** covers owner responses, follow-up criteria, effectiveness checks, and the conditions for reopening a finding.

The last three chapters cover the move to the new system. Planning migration and configuration deals with inventory, cleaning and mapping of existing records, a pilot import, and the cutover. Training teams and rolling out the system covers role-based learning, a pilot with representative users, and staged deployment. Maintaining the system and choosing next steps explains how to review the workflow after launch, evaluate improvements without invented return-on-investment claims, and prepare for the next decision.

Several habits run through every chapter:

- **Decisions come first.** Each step is described by the role that decides, the inputs it needs, the record it leaves, and the exceptions it must handle.
- **Evidence is separated from claims.** A statement from a source is cited to that source, and general advice is labeled as advice.
- **Examples are hypothetical.** Scenarios begin with a clear label, describe an unnamed business, and never claim real results.
- **Capabilities are questions.** Features discussed in this guide are criteria for evaluation, not promises about any product.

## Planning evaluation and implementation

Plan the project in stages and give each stage an owner. A typical sequence starts with mapping and criteria, moves to a shortlist and scripted demonstrations, and then to configuration and a pilot import of sample records. Training and a user pilot with representative users come next, followed by cutover and staged deployment, with a review after each stage. Each stage ends with a recorded decision to proceed, repeat, or stop.

Name a workflow owner at the start. This person answers for how audits move through the process, approves changes, and reviews performance. The same person should sit in every demonstration, because they will live with the configured system.

Add representatives from the groups the system will touch: auditors, process owners who answer findings, a production representative who understands the calendar, and an IT contact who can speak about access and data. Include someone who knows how work runs on evening and night shifts.

Decide what success looks like before choosing a product. Success is not the system being live. It is audits that follow a plan the team can explain, observations linked to the criteria they test, findings with owners and dates, and follow-up that actually happens.

Measure these from your own records, and be wary of any promise of savings or improvements that is not based on your own data.

Be careful about what software can and cannot do for certification. Confirm certification requirements with your certification body, since the sources used in this guide do not address them. No tool guarantees an outcome.

Treat the software as a way to keep consistent, retrievable records, and judge it by whether it meets your acceptance criteria.

ICC publishes a description of its [audits and management review module](/solutions/audits-management-review/) as one option to examine when comparing products. The published description mentions an annual audit program, a single audit workspace for checklist, findings, and evidence, a checklist library, findings with owners and closure status, a PDF report, and audit trends.

These are published descriptions only. As with any vendor, availability and configuration of a capability should be confirmed in a demonstration with your own scenarios before a team relies on it, so this guide treats each item as a question to ask. The final chapter explains how to prepare for that session, and it closes with an invitation to request a software demonstration.

**Illustrative example:** The unnamed machining manufacturer assigns its quality manager as workflow owner and adds a lead auditor, a production manager, a process owner, and an IT contact to the project group. The group sets a three-stage plan and agrees to judge success by whether each audit has a plan on file, whether each finding links to its observations, and whether each closure has checked evidence.

It writes those measures down before the first demonstration, so that it can record starting values for those measures before the pilot and compare later results against them.

Start with the chapter on mapping the current workflow, and keep your written problem statement beside you as you read the rest.
