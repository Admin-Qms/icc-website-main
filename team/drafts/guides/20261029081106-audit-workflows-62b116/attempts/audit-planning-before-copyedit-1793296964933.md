## Defining audit scope

Scope is the first planning decision, and products record it in different ways. A product may offer a single free-text field for scope, or it may offer structured fields for processes, sites, shifts and periods. Which one the team needs depends on how it plans.

The [ISO 9001 Auditing Practices Group guidance on internal audits](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-InternalAudit.pdf) states that ISO 9001 does not specify audit frequency, duration or scope. The organization defines them, and it should have a process for using past audit results in planning future audits. Software cannot supply these choices. What it can do is hold the team's choices in a form that planners and auditors can find, compare and revise.

The [audit planning guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-Audit_Planning.pdf) describes two levels of planning: the audit program and the audit plan for a single audit. Define scope at both levels. At the program level, scope answers which processes, sites and shifts are covered across the cycle. At the audit level, it answers what this audit will examine, against which criteria and in what period.

The same guidance advises planning around processes rather than clauses, and auditing linked processes together so that linkages and interdependencies can be explored. It also notes that some areas, such as leadership commitment or competence, have no standalone process and can be verified through related processes. A scope record should therefore allow several processes in one audit and should let planners show where an area without its own process is covered.

### What a scope record might hold

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Processes covered | Names the linked processes audited together |
| Sites and shifts | Shows whether evening and night work is included |
| Period examined | Sets the span of records the auditor samples |
| Criteria | Lists the requirements and internal procedures used as reference |
| Areas covered indirectly | Records where topics without their own process are verified |
| Exclusions and reasons | Keeps a visible reason when something is left out |

Treat this table as a prompt for evaluation, not a statement of what any product holds. In a demonstration, ask the vendor to create a scope for two linked processes, add a night shift and record an exclusion with a reason. Then ask how the planner starts next year's scope from this one: whether the earlier scope can be copied, edited and compared, and whether the copy keeps a link to the original so that changes between cycles stay visible. A planner who must retype scope each cycle may drop reasons and exclusions without noticing.

### Roles, inputs and decisions for this step

| Item | What to define |
|---|---|
| Role | The workflow owner, here the quality manager, with process owners reviewing scope for their areas |
| Inputs | The process list, last year's audit reports, open findings and recent changes |
| Decision | The final scope of the program and of each audit; the quality manager accepts a scope when it names its processes, period and criteria, and sends it back to the planner otherwise |
| Record | The scope entry, with its decision-maker, the date and the reason for any exclusion |
| Exception | A process nobody can describe, or a scope a process owner disputes; the quality manager decides, writes the disposition on the scope entry and, if unresolved, escalates to top management |

**Illustrative example:** An unnamed Canadian manufacturer tests scope entry in a vendor demonstration using representative records: machining and assembly as one scope, the evening shift, and paint-line records excluded with a reason. The team then asks the vendor to show the exception, a process owner disputing the scope, with the quality manager's disposition recorded. The scenario is hypothetical and describes no customer result.

## Assigning suitable auditors

Once scope is set, the program needs auditors who can examine it. The [internal audit guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-InternalAudit.pdf) says an auditor of the internal audit process would evaluate competence, objectivity and impartiality, risk-based planning, management involvement, and how audit outcomes are used to evaluate QMS effectiveness and identify improvement. Software for planning should help the team show that it considered competence and impartiality when it assigned each audit.

Start with the information the product would need. Competence is a judgment the organization makes, so a product should record the basis for it, such as training, experience or prior audits, in whatever form the team's procedure requires. The product should not be expected to judge competence itself. Check whether it can hold an auditor list with the processes each person is considered competent to audit, and whether the planner can see that list at the moment of assignment.

Objectivity and impartiality call for a conflict check. A team might adopt a rule that an auditor does not audit their own work, since the organization defines its own rule. Ask whether the product can flag an assignment that conflicts with the auditor's home area, whether the planner can override the flag, and whether an override requires a recorded reason. A product that cannot warn but can record the planner's confirmation may still serve a small team, while a larger team may want a hard stop.

The [audit planning guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-Audit_Planning.pdf) says plans should be developed with the auditee where possible, and that the audit of internal audit is best conducted last. For software, this suggests two practical questions: can the auditee see and comment on a draft plan, and can the planner place the audit of the audit process after the others?

### Small teams and shared roles

Smaller organizations may have too few people to separate every role. The point of the planning record is to make that limit visible. Where an auditor must audit an area close to their own work, the team can record the reason, the added control and the person who approved the choice. A recorded choice does not by itself establish objectivity, so the added control should be real, such as a second reviewer for that audit's findings.

### Roles, inputs and decisions for this step

| Item | What to define |
|---|---|
| Role | The workflow owner, here the quality manager, who assigns auditors; top management confirms assignments that involve a limited pool |
| Inputs | The scope entry, the auditor list with competence basis and each candidate's home area |
| Decision | Which auditor takes each audit; the quality manager accepts an assignment when the recorded competence basis supports the candidate's suitability and the assignment is impartial or carries an approved added control, and sends it back otherwise |
| Record | The assignment, with the competence basis, the conflict check result and the decision-maker |
| Exception | No impartial auditor is available; top management decides whether to use an auditor with an added control, delay the audit or bring in outside help, and the decision is recorded on the assignment |

**Illustrative example:** In a hypothetical demonstration, the team enters four trained auditors, only one of whom is trained to audit receiving and works in that area. It asks the vendor to assign that auditor to a receiving audit, then to show the conflict flag, the recorded second reviewer and top management's approval of the exception.

## Scheduling representative activities

A schedule should reflect what actually happens in the plant, not only what is convenient for the auditor. The [audit planning guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-Audit_Planning.pdf) says audit timing may need to suit production cycles, and it asks teams to consider what happens on evening and night shifts. It also says important areas, areas with recent change and areas with past nonconformity should be scheduled as a priority, while a risk-based approach may audit some areas less often if the justification is recorded.

Those statements translate into planning data. The planner needs to see production cycles, shifts and recent changes beside the audit calendar. If the product shows only a calendar of audit dates, the team will probably keep that context elsewhere, and the link to the schedule will depend on someone remembering it.

Ask for a representative scheduling exercise in the demonstration:

1. Enter a process with a recent change and one with an earlier nonconformity, and show that both can be marked for priority.
2. Schedule an audit around a production peak that the planner enters.
3. Include an evening or night shift activity in the same audit.
4. Record the reason a lower-risk process is audited less often.

The word representative matters. A schedule that samples only the day shift, or only the most accessible area, may not represent the process. Planners should be able to show what was sampled and why. During evaluation, check whether the product can report sampled activities after the audit, so management can see whether the sample matched the plan. Where the product allows it, a sampling note per activity is enough, for example which shift, which line and which records.

### Roles, inputs and decisions for this step

| Item | What to define |
|---|---|
| Role | The workflow owner, here the quality manager, who builds the calendar; process owners confirm timing |
| Inputs | The scope entries, auditor assignments, production calendar, shift pattern and the priority list |
| Decision | The dates and sampled activities; the quality manager accepts the calendar when each priority area is scheduled, the sampled activities cover the relevant production cycles and shifts, and each reduced-frequency area carries a recorded reason, and sends it back otherwise |
| Record | The calendar, the sampling notes, the justification for reduced frequency and the decision-maker |
| Exception | A process owner cannot release the area on the proposed date; the quality manager decides the new date or a sampling change, records the disposition and informs the auditor |

**Illustrative example:** The hypothetical team asks the vendor to schedule the assembly audit ahead of a seasonal peak, with one day-shift and one night-shift activity sampled and a lower-risk packaging process moved to every second cycle with its justification. It then asks the vendor to show an unavailable area: the process owner declines the date, and the quality manager's new date is recorded.

## Handling changed priorities

Plans change. A customer complaint, a new line or a serious finding can move an area up the list, and the program has to adjust without losing its history. The [audit planning guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-Audit_Planning.pdf) says an audit program can change with circumstances while still achieving its intent. The planning record should show that the change was deliberate and who approved it.

For software, the core need is a controlled change. Ask the vendor to show the following with representative records:

- A scheduled audit is moved and the original date remains visible with the reason.
- A new audit is added mid-cycle because of a nonconformity, and the program view shows the effect on coverage.
- An audit is canceled, with a recorded reason and a note on how the area will be covered instead.
- Past results can be viewed while the planner makes the change.

The [internal audit guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-InternalAudit.pdf) says that higher-risk processes, or those with nonconformities, should have priority. The planner needs past results at hand when priorities change. Check whether the product shows previous findings for a process on the planning screen, or whether the planner must leave the screen and search.

Also look at what the change does to people. If an audit moves, the auditor and auditee should be told. Ask whether the product notifies them, or whether the team must send its own message. If notification is outside the product, the procedure should say who sends it.

### Roles, inputs and decisions for this step

| Item | What to define |
|---|---|
| Role | The workflow owner, here the quality manager, who approves changes; top management is informed of changes that reduce coverage |
| Inputs | The trigger for the change, such as a complaint, a new line or a finding, together with the current program and past results |
| Decision | Whether to move, add or cancel an audit; the quality manager accepts the change when the reason and the effect on coverage are recorded, and rejects it otherwise |
| Record | The change entry with the original and new plan, the reason, the decision-maker and the people notified |
| Exception | A change requested at short notice or by someone outside the quality function; top management decides whether to accept it and records the disposition |

**Illustrative example:** After a hypothetical complaint about a welded part, the team asks the vendor to add an unplanned welding audit and move a packaging audit to the next cycle. The packaging entry should keep its original date and the reason; the welding entry should name the complaint. The team also tests a short-notice request from outside quality, which top management decides on.

Each planning decision can keep references forward. The scope entry can carry the audit reference that later findings cite, the assignment can name the auditor responsible, and the calendar can hold the dates against which follow-up evidence checks are planned. Ask the vendor to show these references on a planning record, so the chain does not depend on separate notes.
