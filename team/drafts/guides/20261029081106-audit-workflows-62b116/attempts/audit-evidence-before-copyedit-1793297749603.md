## Preparing workflow questions

Evidence collection starts before the auditor reaches the floor, and the checklist should carry the audit plan reference so later findings and follow-up evidence connect back to it. The [audit planning guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-Audit_Planning.pdf) advises reviewing available documented information before the audit and notes that checklists can improve audit quality if they are designed to promote it. For software, the practical question is how well the product helps an auditor prepare questions that follow the workflow, not just the list of requirements.

The same guidance advises planning around processes rather than clauses. A checklist built that way asks what the process does: who starts the work, what record is created, who checks it, and what happens when something goes wrong. Those are workflow questions, and they tie directly to the roles, inputs, decisions, records, and exceptions the team mapped earlier.

ICC publishes a [description of its audits and management review module](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/audits-management-review/) that mentions a checklist library and a single audit workspace for checklist, findings, and evidence. These are published descriptions only. Treat each as an evaluation question: ask to see how a checklist is created, edited, and attached to a specific audit, and apply the same questions to every other candidate.

The [internal audit guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-InternalAudit.pdf) says the organization defines audit scope and methods. A checklist library should therefore be a starting point the team can change. Ask whether the team can edit library items, add its own questions, retire questions and keep a history of changes.

### What to test in preparation

- Can the auditor open prior findings and relevant records for the process from the audit workspace?
- Can questions be organized by process step rather than only by requirement?
- Can a question carry the expected record, such as the form or log the auditor should look for?
- Can a question be added during preparation and during the audit, with a record of who added it?

### Roles, inputs and decisions for this step

| Item | What to define |
|---|---|
| Role | The workflow owner, here the quality manager, with the lead auditor preparing the questions |
| Inputs | The scope entry, prior findings, the process map and the records the process is expected to produce |
| Decision | Whether the checklist is ready; the quality manager accepts it when each question points to a process step and an expected record, and sends it back to the lead auditor otherwise |
| Record | The approved checklist, with its version, the decision-maker and the date of acceptance |
| Exception | A process with no suitable library items, or a question the auditee disputes; the quality manager decides whether to write a new question or revise it, and records the disposition on the checklist |

**Illustrative example:** An unnamed Canadian manufacturer asks a vendor to build a checklist for a machining audit in a demonstration, using representative records. The team asks for questions ordered by process step, each tied to an expected record, and then tests the exception: a library question that does not fit the process is replaced with a new one and the change is recorded. The scenario is hypothetical and describes no customer result.

## Recording observations

Once the auditor is at the process, the product has to capture what is seen and heard. The [audit planning guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-Audit_Planning.pdf) asks teams to consider evening and night shifts, so the recording method has to work away from a desk. Each observation should also carry the audit plan reference, so later findings and follow-up evidence trace back to it. A tool that needs a reliable connection and a laptop may fit the day shift and fail at night.

An observation is what the auditor saw, read or was told, with enough detail that someone else could check it. Judgments drawn from observations, such as findings, belong to a separate step, but the product should let a judgment point back to the observations behind it. Ask whether it keeps the two apart and links them.

Useful observation records usually have the following parts, and the product should be able to hold them in whatever form the team's procedure requires:

1. The process step or area observed.
2. What was seen, read or said, in plain language.
3. The source: a document, a record, a person, or a direct observation.
4. The date, time, and shift.
5. The auditor who recorded it.

The [internal audit guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-InternalAudit.pdf) says auditors of the internal audit process look at competence, objectivity, and impartiality. Neutral, checkable observations support those qualities, because a reader can see what the auditor relied on. The [audit planning guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-Audit_Planning.pdf) adds that areas without a standalone process, such as leadership commitment or competence, can be verified through related processes. A product should let an auditor note which related process an observation came from.

Test the offline case in the evaluation, since the plan may include evening or night work. Ask the vendor to show how an observation is recorded without a connection, how it reaches the audit record afterward and what happens if two people edit the same item. If there is no such mode, the team decides whether a paper capture step is acceptable and who enters it.

### Roles, inputs and decisions for this step

| Item | What to define |
|---|---|
| Role | The workflow owner, here the quality manager, who sets the recording method; auditors record observations |
| Inputs | The accepted checklist, the audit plan, the shift pattern and the devices available on the floor |
| Decision | Whether the recording method is acceptable; the quality manager accepts it when an observation can be captured on every planned shift with its source, time, and author, and rejects it otherwise |
| Record | A trial set of observations from a pilot audit, with the decision-maker's acceptance of the method |
| Exception | No connection or no device on a shift; the quality manager decides whether to use a paper capture step, who enters it and by when, and records the disposition |

**Illustrative example:** The hypothetical team asks a vendor to record four observations from a night-shift station using representative records, one of them taken with no connection. The team then tests the exception by asking what happens when two auditors edit the same observation. A tool that overwrites the first entry silently is marked as failed for that scenario.

## Linking evidence to criteria

An observation becomes useful when it is tied to something it is measured against. In audit terms, that is a criterion: a requirement, a procedure, or a stated expectation. The product should let the auditor link each item of evidence to the criterion it supports, and let a reviewer see the chain. Link evidence to the relevant criteria whether it supports or contradicts them, and review evidence that fits no criterion for relevance, recording a gap only if the review finds one.

The ICC [module description](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/audits-management-review/) mentions a single audit workspace for checklist, findings, and evidence, which suggests a place where the three are held together. Whether the workspace links them, and how, should be confirmed in a demonstration. Ask the vendor to open a finding and move to the evidence behind it, then to the criterion the evidence was measured against, without leaving the audit.

The [internal audit guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-InternalAudit.pdf) describes the organization as defining its own methods and says ISO 9001 does not require use of ISO 19011. In practice, the criteria in a team's audit may include ISO 9001 requirements, customer requirements, internal procedures, and work instructions. The product should accept all of them as criteria, not only a built-in list of standard clauses. If the product supports clause libraries, ask the vendor to state which edition each library reflects.

### Questions that show whether the link holds

| Question | A useful answer | A weak answer |
|---|---|---|
| Can one item of evidence support more than one criterion? | Yes, with each link visible | The item must be copied |
| Can a criterion be an internal procedure? | Yes, added by the team | Only built-in clauses are allowed |
| Can evidence be seen from the finding and the criterion? | Both views show it | Evidence appears on one screen only |
| Is a change to a link recorded? | Who changed it and when is visible | The link changes silently |

Treat this table as a prompt, not as a statement of what any product does. The link should also carry forward: if the audit later leads to an action, follow-up evidence can point back to the same criterion and the audit plan reference.

Also ask how the product shows evidence that does not support a criterion. An auditor should be able to link a record that contradicts a requirement as readily as one that supports it, and a reviewer should be able to filter for those links. A product that tracks only supporting evidence may tilt the record toward a clean result, so test this case with a representative nonconforming record.

### Roles, inputs and decisions for this step

| Item | What to define |
|---|---|
| Role | The workflow owner, here the quality manager, with auditors linking and a second reviewer checking |
| Inputs | The observations, the criteria list, the checklist and any attached evidence |
| Decision | Whether each link is sound; the second reviewer accepts a link when the evidence is relevant to the criterion and the relationship, whether supporting or contradicting, is accurately recorded, and sends it back to the auditor otherwise |
| Record | The evidence-to-criterion links, with the reviewer's name, the decision and the date |
| Exception | Evidence that fits no criterion, or a criterion with no evidence; the quality manager decides whether to add a criterion, collect more evidence or note a gap, and records the disposition |

**Illustrative example:** The hypothetical team tests a vendor by linking a torque log to two criteria: an internal work instruction and a customer requirement. It then tests the exception, a criterion with no evidence, and checks whether the product shows the gap and records the quality manager's disposition.

## Protecting sensitive records

Access rules also protect the chain from plan to finding to follow-up evidence, so a restricted item should remain traceable to authorized users. Audit evidence can include supplier terms, customer drawings, personnel information and defect data. Teams should decide who may see each kind, and the product should be able to enforce that. This guide does not give legal advice, and the sources behind it do not address privacy or confidentiality law, so confirm obligations with qualified advisers where they apply.

The [internal audit guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-InternalAudit.pdf) emphasizes objectivity and impartiality. Access rules support both. An auditor who can edit the records of their own area or a process owner who can alter an auditor's notes creates a risk the team may wish to prevent. Which rules are appropriate is the team's decision, and the product should be able to carry out the rules the team chooses.

Ask the vendor to demonstrate the following with representative records:

- A restricted evidence item is hidden from a user without access, while the related finding remains visible.
- A user's access is removed, and past entries remain attributed to that person.
- An evidence item is replaced or removed, and the product keeps a record of the action.
- A report or export respects the same restrictions as the screen.

The [audit planning guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-Audit_Planning.pdf) notes that audit plans should be developed with the auditee where possible. Check whether the product can share a plan or checklist with the auditee, if the team's procedure calls for it, without exposing the auditor's working notes or other areas' evidence.

### Roles, inputs and decisions for this step

| Item | What to define |
|---|---|
| Role | The workflow owner, here the quality manager, with the system administrator carrying out access changes |
| Inputs | The record types, the sensitivity of each, the list of user types and the team's access rules |
| Decision | Which user types may see or change each record type; top management approves the rules, and the quality manager accepts a configuration when it passes the test above |
| Record | The access rules, a test log with each result and the decision-maker |
| Exception | A user who needs temporary access, or a rule the product cannot enforce; top management decides whether to grant, narrow, or reject, and the disposition is logged |

**Illustrative example:** The hypothetical team creates an auditor, a process owner and a contractor in a vendor trial. It restricts a customer drawing, confirms the contractor cannot open it, and removes the contractor's access to see whether earlier entries stay attributed. The scenario is hypothetical and describes no actual product result.
