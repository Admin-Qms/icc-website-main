## Setting follow-up criteria

Follow-up is the stage that shows whether an audit changed anything. Before software is configured, the team should decide what counts as a completed response. Without that decision, closure becomes whatever the owner reports.

The [ISO 9001 Auditing Practices Group guidance on internal audits](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-InternalAudit.pdf) says the organization should have a process for using past audit results in planning future audits, and that the program should consider the results of previous audits. Follow-up criteria support that use. A closed finding that cannot show what was done gives the next planner little to work with.

As a proposed approach, write follow-up criteria for each finding category the team uses. A criterion states what evidence must exist, who checks it and by when. For example, a response to a nonconformity might require a description of the action taken, a record that the action was carried out, and a later check that the problem has not recurred. The team's procedure sets the exact wording.

The [audit planning guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-Audit_Planning.pdf) says areas with past nonconformity should be scheduled as a priority. That makes follow-up criteria a planning input too: the product should let a planner see which areas have open or recurring findings when the next program is built.

Questions worth settling in the criteria include the following:

- Must the cause be identified before an action is accepted?
- Is the action proportionate to the finding?
- Who checks the evidence, and by when?
- When is the effectiveness check due?
- What happens if the owner misses the date?

Due dates should allow for production cycles, which the planning guidance says can affect audit timing. A date set without regard to the plant calendar may be missed.

Ask the vendor how criteria are held. Can they be defined by category? Can they differ by finding category and still appear on each finding? Can a due date follow from the criterion? Can the team change a criterion for future findings without altering the criterion on findings already open?

### Roles, inputs and decisions for this step

| Item | What to define |
|---|---|
| Role | The workflow owner, here the quality manager, with process owners reviewing the criteria |
| Inputs | The finding categories, the team's procedure, past findings and the audit plan references |
| Decision | The criteria for each category; top management approves them, and the quality manager accepts a criterion when it names the evidence, the checker and the timing, and returns it otherwise |
| Record | The approved criteria with version, date and decision-maker; open findings keep the version that applied when they were raised |
| Exception | A finding that fits no criterion; the quality manager sets a criterion for that case, top management confirms it, and the disposition is recorded |

**Illustrative example:** An unnamed Canadian manufacturer asks a vendor to configure follow-up criteria for a nonconformity category, using representative records. The team then edits the criteria and checks that a finding already open keeps the earlier version. For the exception, the team asks the vendor to run a finding that fits no category, with the quality manager setting a criterion, top management confirming it and the outcome recorded. The scenario is hypothetical and describes no customer result.

## Checking action evidence

When an owner reports that an action is complete, someone has to check the evidence. The check is what separates a response that was planned from one that was carried out and that worked.

The ICC [module description](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/audits-management-review/) mentions findings with owners and closure status. These are published descriptions only, so treat them as questions. Ask the vendor to show an owner submitting evidence, a checker seeing it beside the finding, and the status change.

The [internal audit guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-InternalAudit.pdf) highlights objectivity and impartiality in the audit process. As a proposed approach, the person who verifies an action should not be the person who carried it out. In a small team that may not be possible, so the product should at least show who did each step, and the team should record any added control.

Checks usually have three questions. Was the action carried out as described? Does the evidence show it? Has the situation that caused the finding actually changed? The third question may need time. A contemporaneous record may document the action but normally cannot show on its own that the problem has stopped. Ask whether the product can hold a later effectiveness check and remind the checker when it is due.

Evidence takes different forms. For a changed work instruction, it might be the revised instruction, a record of who was told and a later observation at the process. The criteria should say what is expected, and the product should hold files and notes together with the finding.

Test the evidence itself. Ask the vendor to attach a file, replace it and show what the checker sees afterward. Ask for a rejection, and confirm the owner is told why and can resubmit. The rejection and the resubmission should both remain in the record.

### Roles, inputs and decisions for this step

| Item | What to define |
|---|---|
| Role | The workflow owner, here the quality manager, who assigns checkers; the action owner submits evidence |
| Inputs | The finding, the follow-up criteria, the owner's evidence and the audit plan reference |
| Decision | Whether the evidence meets the criteria; an independent checker accepts it when it shows the action was done and, where required, effective, and rejects it with a reason otherwise. The quality manager closes the finding on a dated record only after any required effectiveness check passes, holds it as pending while one is due, and keeps it open with a recorded reason if a check fails |
| Record | The submission, the checker's decision, the reason for any rejection, the owner's response and the dates |
| Exception | No independent checker is available; top management approves an alternative control, such as a second review, and the quality manager records the approval |

**Illustrative example:** The hypothetical team asks a vendor to show an owner submitting a calibration record for a machining finding, and a checker rejecting it because the date is missing. The owner resubmits, and both versions stay in the record. For the exception, the team asks the vendor to run the case where only the quality manager can check, so top management approves a reviewer from another department who assesses the evidence independently, and the vendor shows the approval recorded.

## Reopening unresolved findings

Some actions do not work. A problem recurs, an effectiveness check fails, or new evidence shows the original response was incomplete. The product should let the team reopen the finding, not create an unrelated new one, so the history stays together.

The ICC [description of findings with owners and closure status](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/audits-management-review/) suggests status is shown. It does not tell the reader how a closed finding is reopened. Ask the vendor whether a closed finding can be reopened, who can do it, what reason is required and whether the earlier submissions and decisions remain visible.

The [internal audit guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-InternalAudit.pdf) says higher-risk processes, or those with nonconformities, should have priority in the program. A reopened finding is a signal for that kind of review. The planner should be able to see reopened items when scheduling.

As a proposed team approach, decide in advance what triggers a reopening and who decides. Possible triggers include a failed effectiveness check on an already closed finding (a failed check before closure simply keeps it open), a repeat of the same problem, and evidence that the original verification was wrong. Also decide whether the owner stays or changes, and who is told.

Notification matters here. The owner, the checker and the planner should each see the reopened item in the place they normally work. Ask whether it appears in an owner's open list, and whether the product tells people or the team must send its own message.

Test with a representative case. Close a finding, then enter a recurrence, reopen it with a reason and confirm that the original response, the checker's decision and the new entry all appear in one history. Ask also whether the due date restarts, and whether the earlier date remains visible.

### Roles, inputs and decisions for this step

| Item | What to define |
|---|---|
| Role | The workflow owner, here the quality manager, who decides reopening; the process owner is informed |
| Inputs | The closed finding, its history, its audit plan reference, the recurrence or failed check and the team's reopening rules |
| Decision | Whether to reopen; the quality manager accepts the request when a trigger in the team's rules is met and the reason is recorded, and declines it with a reason otherwise |
| Record | The reopening or declined-request entry with trigger, reason, decision-maker, new owner and due date, linked to the original history and audit plan reference |
| Exception | The quality manager or the original checker is involved in the dispute over reopening; top management appoints an uninvolved person to decide, and the delegation is recorded |

**Illustrative example:** The hypothetical team asks a vendor to close a calibration finding, enter a repeat of the problem and reopen it with a reason. The history should show the original response and the checker's decision. For the exception, the team asks the vendor to run a case where the original checker disputes the reopening, and to show top management appointing an uninvolved person and the delegation recorded.

## Reporting the audit program

Individual findings answer the question for one audit. The team also needs to see across audits: which areas keep producing findings, which actions take longest and whether the program covers what the plan said it would.

The ICC [description](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/audits-management-review/) mentions a PDF report and audit trends. These published descriptions are questions for a demonstration. Ask the vendor to show a program report built from representative records, including open and reopened findings, and to show what the PDF contains.

The [audit planning guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-Audit_Planning.pdf) describes the audit program and the audit plan for a single audit as two levels of planning. Reports should show both levels: coverage against the program and results for a single audit. The [internal audit guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-InternalAudit.pdf) says auditors of the audit process look at how outcomes are used to evaluate QMS effectiveness and identify improvement. A program report is where that use can be shown, since management can review it.

Useful program views often include the following, with the exact set chosen by the team:

- Planned and completed audits by process and period.
- Findings by category, owner and status.
- Open findings past their due dates.
- Reopened findings.
- Areas with repeated findings.

Consider who sees each report, and ask whether views can be limited by role or process area.

A report should carry the audit plan references, so a reader can move from a program figure to the plan, the finding and the follow-up evidence behind it. Ask the vendor to demonstrate that path.

Be careful with trends. A rise in findings may mean a process has worsened or that auditors looked harder. The product should show counts with context, and any conclusion remains a human judgment. Ask whether a report can be filtered, exported and traced back to the underlying records.

### Roles, inputs and decisions for this step

| Item | What to define |
|---|---|
| Role | The workflow owner, here the quality manager, who prepares the report; top management reviews it |
| Inputs | The program, audit plan references, completed audits, findings, statuses and follow-up checks |
| Decision | Whether the report is accurate and complete; the quality manager accepts it when its figures trace to records and cover the intended audits, findings and follow-up records, and top management decides what follows from it |
| Record | The report with its date, the reviewer, the decision-maker and the actions agreed |
| Exception | A figure that cannot be traced to a record; the quality manager corrects the source or withdraws the figure, and the disposition is recorded |

**Illustrative example:** The hypothetical team asks a vendor to produce a program report from representative records and click through a figure to its audit plan, findings and follow-up evidence. For the exception, the team asks the vendor to run one untraceable count and to show the quality manager withdrawing it and the disposition recorded.
