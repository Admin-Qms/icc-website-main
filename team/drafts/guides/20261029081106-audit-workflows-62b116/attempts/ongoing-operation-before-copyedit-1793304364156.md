## Review the workflow and evidence

Once the system is running, the first job is to check that it still matches the work. Teams change processes, add shifts, and revise procedures, and a configuration that fit at rollout can drift.

The [audit planning guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-Audit_Planning.pdf) says an audit program can change with circumstances while still achieving its intent. A proposed practice is to schedule a regular review of the audit workflow in the system, at a frequency the team sets, and after any significant change to processes, roles or procedures.

A review can follow the chain the team has used throughout: audit plan, finding, action and follow-up evidence. Pick a sample of recent audits and trace each from its plan reference to its closure. Look for broken links, overdue items, findings without owners, evidence that cannot be opened, and records still kept in side files.

The ICC [module description](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/audits-management-review/) mentions audit trends and findings with owners and closure status. Treat these as questions about reports: do the reports the team relies on still draw from the system, and do their figures trace to records? Confirm what the product actually offers with the vendor before relying on any description.

Review the configuration as well as the records. Check roles and permissions after staff changes, category lists after procedure changes, and checklist content after process changes. Record each change with its reason, since the configuration is part of the audit record's meaning.

When a chain is broken, trace the cause before repairing the record. A missing owner may point to an assignment rule, and a missing reference to an import or an entry habit. Record the cause beside each correction, too.

Keep the review light enough to repeat. A sample of three or four audits, traced in an afternoon, is a possible starting point; the team can widen the sample after a change or when problems appear. Record what was sampled and why, so the next review can cover other areas.

Also review how the system is used on every shift. The audit planning guidance asks teams to consider evening and night work, so confirm that people on those shifts can still reach what they need and that their records arrive in the system without delay.

### Roles, inputs and decisions for this step

| Item | What to define |
|---|---|
| Role | The workflow owner, here the quality manager, with process owners confirming their areas |
| Inputs | A sample of recent audits, the configuration, the issue list and any changes to processes or procedures |
| Decision | Whether the workflow still fits; the quality manager accepts the review when each sampled chain traces from plan to follow-up evidence and any report, configuration or access problem is corrected or covered by an approved exception, and orders corrections otherwise |
| Record | The review report with sample, findings, corrections, owners, outcome and decision-maker |
| Exception | A broken chain or a role with outdated access; the quality manager decides the correction and its due date, and top management decides if the cause needs a wider change |

**Illustrative example:** An unnamed Canadian manufacturer asks a vendor to demonstrate a review using representative records: three audits traced from plan reference to follow-up evidence. The team then asks the vendor to run the exception, a finding whose plan reference is missing, and to show the correction and decision recorded.

## Evaluate improvements without invented ROI

Teams will want to know whether the system was worth it and what to change next. This guide states no savings, no payback period and no result for any product, and a team should be careful about producing such figures without a sound basis.

The [internal audit guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-InternalAudit.pdf) says auditors of the internal audit process look at how audit outcomes are used to evaluate QMS effectiveness and identify improvement. That suggests judging the system by what the team can now do with audit results, not only by cost. Ask whether past results are now used in planning, whether follow-up is checked, and whether management sees the program.

When the team does want numbers, set rules. State the question, the measure, the period, the method and the limits. Use the team's own records, label estimates as estimates, and compare like periods. Avoid attributing a change to the software when other changes happened at the same time, such as a new auditor or a revised procedure.

Consider the candidate improvements in a simple list. For each, write the problem it addresses, the evidence that the problem exists, the effort to change it and who decides. Possible items include a new report, a revised checklist, a changed permission, or a request to the vendor. The [audit planning guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-Audit_Planning.pdf) notes that areas with past nonconformity should be scheduled as a priority, which can help rank improvements: favor the ones that affect those areas.

Be wary of vanity measures. A count of findings or audits completed says little by itself, since a rise may mean more thorough auditing or a worse process. Pair each count with context, and let people who know the process interpret it. Revisit the list at each review, adding items.

Present the list to management with the evidence behind each item, and keep declined items with their reasons so the same proposal is not rebuilt later.

Improvements can also be requests to the vendor. Describe the problem, show the records, and ask whether the product already supports a solution before asking for a change. Treat answers about future features as intent, not current evaluation.

### Roles, inputs and decisions for this step

| Item | What to define |
|---|---|
| Role | The workflow owner, here the quality manager, who keeps the list; top management decides priorities |
| Inputs | Review reports, user feedback, the issue log and any measures with stated methods |
| Decision | Which improvements to pursue; top management accepts a proposal when the problem, evidence, effort and owner are stated, and returns it otherwise |
| Record | The improvement list with decision, decision-maker, rationale and dates |
| Exception | A claimed benefit with no stated method; the quality manager returns it for support, and top management decides whether to proceed without a figure |

**Illustrative example:** The hypothetical team asks a vendor to show a report of overdue actions and then asks what the vendor can state about method for any claimed time saving. For the exception, a claimed saving has no method, so the quality manager returns it, with the decision recorded.

## Frequently asked questions

These answers summarize the guide, drawing on the [internal audit guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-InternalAudit.pdf) and the ICC [module description](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/audits-management-review/). They do not replace the team's own procedures, and they make no claim about any product.

### What problem should internal audit software solve first?

Start with the broken link in the current workflow, often between findings and follow-up evidence. State it as a testable sentence and write acceptance criteria that a demonstration can show. The mapping chapter covers this step.

### Which records should be migrated?

Migrate what planners and auditors need: the current program, open findings, recent reports and linked evidence. Archive or reference the rest under the team's retention rules, and verify a pilot import first. The migration chapter gives the detail.

### How should responsibilities and permissions be assigned?

Assign decisions to roles with backups, and keep duties separate where the team requires it, such as raising a finding and verifying its closure. Test that the product enforces the chosen rules. Earlier chapters cover roles and permissions.

### How should approval decisions be tested?

Use representative records and an awkward case: an acceptance, a rejection with a reason and a decision by someone uninvolved. Confirm that each decision and its decision-maker are recorded where a later reviewer can find them.

### How should a team handle obsolete records?

Classify them during the inventory as move, archive, reference or retire under the team's retention rules, and record the owner. This guide gives no retention periods, and local obligations may apply.

### What evidence should be available during an audit?

The plan, checklist, observations, linked evidence, findings, actions and follow-up checks should be reachable from one starting point, with access limited by role. Test that an auditor can trace a finding to its evidence and criterion.

### How should training and rollout be evaluated?

Define measures and a review period before each stage, and track task completion, records kept in the system and issues with owners. Treat workload figures as the team's own estimates. The rollout chapter has the detail.

### Does software guarantee ISO certification?

No. Software can hold records and route decisions, but the organization defines its audit program and remains responsible for its quality management system. Certification depends on that system and the decision of its certification body.

### What should be prepared for a software demonstration?

Prepare the problem statement, acceptance criteria, a test script and representative records with exceptions, and record each scenario's result. The next section sets out the pack in full.

## Prepare a software demonstration

A demonstration is only as useful as its preparation. The team already holds much of the material.

Prepare four items, and share them with the vendor ahead of time. First, the problem statement and the acceptance criteria, ranked by priority. Second, a test script with one scenario per must-have and at least one exception for each. Third, representative records: a plan, a finding with evidence, an action that is rejected and a finding that is reopened. Fourth, the roles who will attend, including a planner, an auditor, an owner and a checker.

Prepare for the exceptions with equal care. A demonstration that covers only the normal path says little, so each exception in the script should name the expected result and the role that decides.

The ICC [description of its audits and management review module](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/audits-management-review/) gives a starting list of questions about checklists, a single audit workspace, owners and closure status, reports and trends. Use it as a prompt, not as a statement of what is available. The [internal audit guidance](https://committee.iso.org/files/live/sites/tc176/files/PDF%20APG%20New%20Disclaimer%2012-2023/ISO-TC%20176-TF_APG-InternalAudit.pdf) leaves frequency, duration and scope to the organization, so ask the demonstrator to follow the team's choices.

During the session, assign a note taker and mark each scenario as it ends. Do not accept a statement that a feature exists as a result; ask to see it run with the team's records. Hold a short debrief the same day and record each decision and its owner.
