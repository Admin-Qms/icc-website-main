## Inventory existing maps

A migration plan begins with knowing what the organization intends to move and why. Gather the maps and process descriptions used within the agreed scope, along with the people who can explain them. The process owner can identify the workflow and its boundaries; the quality lead can help locate the information the organization treats as current. The operations manager should be involved when the selection affects several teams or sites.

Put format, scope, and acceptance owner beside each candidate in the inventory. The [ICC process management page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/processes/) describes industry templates and review of supported PDF charts. Confirm support for the selected file and compare the result with its source; do not assume every relationship survives a conversion.

Create an inventory that identifies each candidate map, its purpose, scope, source location, and the person who can decide whether it is suitable for the move. Include related process records needed to interpret it. Where several copies appear to describe the same work, record their differences and ask the responsible people which one is applicable. Do not select a file based only on its name or the most recent date visible in a folder.

Classify the decisions the team needs to make. A current map may be ready for a pilot, another may need clarification, and a third may be retained for reference rather than used as the new working view. The classification is the organization's decision, supported by its own controls. Record unresolved ownership, inaccessible source files, and missing context so they do not disappear from the plan when the inventory is summarized.

For the inventory, identify which source establishes the selected map’s scope and which person can accept it. The [ISO auditing guidance collection](https://committee.iso.org/home/tc176/iso-9001-auditing-practices-group.html) includes educational material on evidence collection. If two copies conflict, preserve their references and ask the responsible owner to decide which is suitable for the pilot; record any unresolved scope difference rather than guessing from a filename.

**Illustrative example:** A hypothetical inventory lists two production maps with different site labels. Reconcile them against the source record: the supervisor recognizes one, while the other has an unknown scope. Pilot only the confirmed map and assign the unresolved copy for investigation. If the pilot output changes the site label, stop acceptance until the owner explains the mismatch.

Ask the people who use a map what would be lost if it were unavailable during the transition. The answer may reveal an important link, a local explanation, or an exception that is not obvious from the file alone. Record the dependency and decide how the information will remain available while the team prepares a new arrangement. This is planning for continuity, not an assertion that a software product provides a particular recovery feature.

The inventory should distinguish an accepted source from a candidate that still needs review. If a map is missing or its owner cannot be identified, assign the next decision and record any limitation on using that map in a pilot. A complete list is useful only if the team can also explain the status of the items on it.

## Prepare files and relationships

Preparation turns the inventory into a set of testable items. For each selected map, record the source file, intended process entry, relevant scope, and the person who will review the result. Identify relationships the team expects to preserve, such as a process name, an owner, or a connection to an instruction. Do not assume that a file's appearance alone captures all of those relationships.

The [ICC page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/processes/) describes a top-level map, flowcharts, owners, and links to controlled documents. Use those descriptions to ask how the proposed configuration will represent the organization's selected information. The published page does not establish that every relationship will be transferred automatically. Ask for a demonstration and record which parts require a decision or manual preparation.

Check that the selected file corresponds to the agreed scope. If two sites use different versions of a workflow, the team should decide whether the pilot represents one site or a common process with local detail. Keep the distinction in the preparation record. A file can be technically readable yet misleading if its label suggests a broader scope than the content actually covers.

Identify the information needed to interpret the file after a test. A reviewer may need the original document, an explanation of abbreviations, and a list of expected relationships. The process owner can provide context, while the quality lead records the baseline against which the result will be compared. Use appropriate controls for source files and samples; avoid exposing sensitive records merely to make a demonstration convenient.

If the source contains a link, ask whether its destination remains applicable and available to the intended reader. If the planned arrangement uses a different route, record the proposed destination and the person who will verify it. Do not infer that a link will survive a conversion unchanged or that the destination will be accessible to every role. These are concrete tests for the pilot.

**Illustrative example:** In a hypothetical preparation exercise, the process owner selects the hypothetical production map and the quality lead records its intended scope. The supervisor identifies an instruction that must remain findable from the relevant step. The team prepares an appropriate sample and records an open question about how the link will be represented. Check the sample link against its intended instruction before deciding whether preparation is complete.

Where an input is incomplete, identify the next decision before proceeding. A missing owner may require a temporary point of contact; an unclear relationship may need review by both affected roles. The team can decide that an item is unsuitable for the pilot until the question is answered. Record that decision rather than silently omitting the item and later assuming the inventory was fully migrated.

A preparation record should allow a reviewer to compare like with like. It identifies the source, expected scope and relationships, permitted sample, and any known limitations. The record also names the person who will accept the result or escalate a discrepancy. This gives the team a basis for evaluating the pilot without treating the preparation list as proof that the target is already correct.

## Pilot imports and review results

A pilot is a controlled test of selected items and decisions, not a declaration that the whole migration is complete. Choose a small representative sample that includes a normal process and at least one meaningful exception. Agree beforehand on what the team expects to inspect, who will perform each check, and what evidence will be retained. The process owner should be able to explain why the sample is relevant to the intended use.

The [ICC process management description](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/processes/) mentions review of supported PDF process charts before acceptance. Confirm the supported format and the proposed review procedure for the actual implementation. The published statement does not guarantee that all other file types, links, permissions, or layouts will be preserved. Ask those questions and distinguish a demonstrated result from a supplier's description.

Before testing, agree on observable comparisons. A reviewer can check whether the intended process is identifiable, whether the relevant text is legible, and whether the scope shown matches the source. The process owner can inspect the decisions and interfaces; an operator can follow a representative route. If a relationship to a document is part of the test, a suitable reader should try that route under the intended access conditions.

Record both the source and the result used in the comparison. A pilot output may appear similar while omitting a qualification, displaying the wrong site, or leading to an unexpected destination. Conversely, a visual difference may not affect the meaning of the map. The people responsible should classify the observation based on its consequence for the intended use rather than assuming that appearance alone determines acceptance.

**Illustrative example:** During the hypothetical pilot, the team tests its hypothetical production map and asks the supervisor to trace a decision point. The quality lead compares the result with the selected source, while the operator tries to find the related instruction. The process owner records a discrepancy in the site label and assigns the next decision. Compare the site label in the output with the selected source before accepting the pilot result.

If a test cannot be completed, state why and what remains unknown. A reader may lack access, a file may not be supported, or the source may be ambiguous. Do not treat an untested item as passed because other parts of the pilot looked correct. Assign someone to obtain the missing information or decide whether a different sample is needed.

The acceptance decision should identify the people who reviewed the result, the checks performed, the discrepancies, and any conditions for proceeding. If an issue is corrected, repeat the affected check using the organization's normal controls and retain the new observation. A correction to a label does not by itself prove that every relationship or permission is correct. Keep the scope of each result explicit.

A pilot can reveal a need to revise the preparation plan. For example, the team may find that a map depends on an instruction whose scope was not recorded. Update the inventory and assign the decision rather than treating the finding as an isolated formatting problem. The goal is to make the next step evidence-based, not to increase the number of items marked complete.

## Plan cutover and recovery

Cutover is the decision to rely on the new arrangement for an agreed scope. Before making that decision, the process owner and operations manager should know which items passed the pilot, which remain open, and where people will find the information needed for ongoing work. The quality lead can consolidate the evidence and identify any condition that must be resolved or explicitly accepted by the appropriate authority.

The [ICC page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/processes/) presents configuration and deployment activities in broad terms. It does not establish a guaranteed cutover sequence or recovery mechanism for an individual site. Ask the supplier what is proposed, what the organization must decide, and how a representative recovery scenario would be tested. Record the answers and their conditions rather than assuming the published description covers every case.

Define the scope of the transition: the processes, sites, readers, and information included. Identify who can authorize the change and who needs to know which source to use. If an item is excluded because evidence is missing, record the interim route and its owner. Avoid leaving users to infer whether an old map or a new view is authoritative.

Plan what the team will do if the intended information is unavailable or the pilot result does not hold under normal conditions. The recovery plan can identify a previously accepted source, the person who decides whether to use it, and how a discrepancy will be recorded. These are organizational decisions to agree and test; they are not claims that the software automatically restores a version or switches users to another source.

**Illustrative example:** Before the hypothetical transition, the process owner and operations manager review the production sample and its outstanding site-label question before accepting a limited transition. They agree which source workers should consult if the proposed view is unavailable and who will decide the next action. The quality lead records the decision and its scope. The transition decision should identify the accepted fallback source and the person authorized to activate it.

A practical readiness discussion asks whether the accepted map is understandable, whether intended readers can reach needed information, whether open discrepancies have owners, and whether the recovery route has been explained to affected roles. If an answer is unknown, document the limitation and decide whether the transition must wait. A schedule alone is not evidence that the organization is ready.

After the decision, the responsible person should check the agreed recovery route against the accepted source inventory and pilot conditions. If a later issue changes those assumptions, return the transition decision to its authorizer; do not quietly extend the accepted scope. The pilot observations and recovery instructions provide the evidence for that decision.

Before cutover, give the authorizing person a list of tested maps, excluded items, open discrepancies, and the agreed recovery route. If a source remains inaccessible, state what that prevents the pilot from proving. The [process management page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/processes/) describes the vendor's broad setup activities; the team must decide which conditions its own transition requires.
