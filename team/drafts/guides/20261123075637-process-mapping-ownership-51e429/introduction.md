## The operational problem

A process map should help people understand how work moves, who makes a decision, and where to find the information needed for the next step. For a Canadian quality or operations team, the practical question is whether that explanation matches the process people actually use. A map can look orderly while a handoff remains disputed, an owner is unclear, or a linked instruction applies to a different scope. Those possibilities need to be checked against the organization's own evidence, not assumed from a diagram or a vendor's description.

Managing the work in compliance management software can give a team a shared place to organize process information and support decisions about who reviews it.

Start with a decision the map must support. An operator may need to find the instruction for a production step; a process owner may need to resolve a question about a boundary; a quality lead may need to understand what evidence supports a completed record. Each person has a different task. The map and its supporting information should make the relevant route understandable to the intended reader, while showing where a decision remains open.

A process owner is the role accountable for decisions and review of a defined process. A process boundary is an agreed statement of where a mapped process starts and ends, including the triggering event, output, and recipient. A handoff is the point where one role passes an output to another; the receiving role needs enough information to act. These terms help the team discuss its own work, but a label alone does not prove that an assignment or interface is agreed.

The guide uses the [ICC process management page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/processes/) as a source for questions a team can bring to an evaluation. In its decision record, distinguish what the vendor publishes from what a representative user actually observes with a sample. Identify the scope of that observation and who can accept the result; if an important question remains unanswered, assign follow-up instead of treating a description as proof of the site's conditions or of a particular configuration.

The [ISO auditing guidance collection](https://committee.iso.org/home/tc176/iso-9001-auditing-practices-group.html) provides educational papers about quality management system auditing, including a process-based approach and evidence collection. The collection says its guidance is not definitive and is not intended as specified requirements, a benchmark, or mandatory audit criteria. Use it to frame questions about evidence and relationships, while assessing the requirements and arrangements that apply to the organization separately.

**Illustrative example:** An unnamed Canadian manufacturer evaluates a hypothetical map for a production workflow. The operator explains an input used at a decision point, the supervisor identifies the next role, and the process owner records a question about an exception. The quality lead checks the sample record and notes what remains uncertain. This scenario is hypothetical; it is not a case study or a measured customer result.

A useful starting record identifies the process scope, the people consulted, the evidence examined, and the next decision. If an instruction is unavailable or two participants disagree about responsibility, document the limitation and assign someone to resolve it. A proposed route should not be presented as settled until the people with authority have made the relevant decision. The pages that follow build on this distinction between an observed fact, a recommendation, and an evaluation question.

## How to use this guide

The guide follows a sequence from understanding the present work to maintaining an agreed explanation. Teams do not need to pretend that every process begins from a blank page. Use the sequence to identify the decisions already made, the evidence that supports them, and the questions that need further work before a map or software arrangement is relied upon.

1. **Map current processes.** Define why the map is needed, agree on the boundary, and examine inputs, outputs, handoffs, and exceptions with the people who do the work.
2. **Evaluate process mapping software.** Set observable criteria and test a representative route, separating a supplier statement from a demonstrated outcome and an unanswered question.
3. **Design a process architecture.** Group processes by purpose, define an overview, connect levels of detail, and review important interfaces.
4. **Assign process owners.** Describe decisions and limits of authority, agree on backups, resolve conflicts, and revisit assignments when circumstances change.
5. **Map workflows and decisions.** Use task evidence to draw steps and decision points, describe exceptions, and validate the explanation with workers.
6. **Connect documents and evidence.** Identify relevant information, test the relationship to a process step, and plan how revisions and inaccessible destinations are handled.
7. **Plan migration and configuration.** Inventory existing maps, prepare a representative sample, review a pilot, and agree on cutover and recovery decisions.
8. **Train teams and roll out.** Practice role-based tasks, use representative participants, stage the rollout, and evaluate feedback before expanding.
9. **Maintain process maps and improve.** Review the explanation after change, examine the review workflow, use the FAQs, and prepare a focused software demonstration.

Each chapter addresses a distinct decision. The early pages help establish what work is being described; the middle pages deal with ownership, workflow, and supporting information; the later pages address implementation and maintenance. Read them in order for a complete plan, or use the relevant chapter to investigate a specific open decision while preserving the context of its neighboring processes.

The guide uses the same hypothetical manufacturer to make questions concrete. It does not claim that this business exists or that a particular plant achieved a result. Where an example describes a role, record, or decision, adapt the question to the organization's actual evidence and authority. A process that spans several sites may require additional clarification about local scope; a shared name on a map does not settle that question by itself.

The final chapter includes nine frequently asked questions and a demonstration-planning section. The questions are starting points for a discussion, not substitutes for a review of actual records. A reader who finds an unanswered question should identify the person who can decide the next action and retain the limitation in the relevant record rather than assuming that a software feature resolves it.

## Planning evaluation and implementation

An evaluation is strongest when the team agrees on the task before seeing a demonstration. Select a representative process, an appropriate sample, the roles that need to use it, and an exception that matters. Ask a reader to follow the proposed route without relying on the presenter to explain every step. Record what was shown, what was described, what depended on configuration, and what remains unanswered.

The [ICC process page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/processes/) describes industry starting templates, review of supported PDF process charts before acceptance, and broad activities including process mapping, configuration, standards onboarding, and deployment with training. Confirm how those descriptions apply to the proposed implementation. They do not establish that every file, permission, link, or recovery action works in a particular way. Ask for a demonstration of the relevant scenario and keep any conditions with the evaluation decision.

The same source says AI output is a suggestion reviewed by a person before entry into a controlled record. Treat that description as a starting point for questions about the proposed use. A team should identify who reviews a suggestion, what evidence that person needs, and how the organization decides whether to use it. Do not assume that a generated suggestion is an approved record or that it supplies an unverified product capability.

For an implementation, identify the source maps and their owners, agree on which items are suitable for a pilot, and compare the result with the selected source and intended scope. Include a reader who will use the information and a responsible person who can decide what to do with a discrepancy. If a file, owner, or destination is unavailable, record that exception and assign follow-up. A pilot result applies to the sample and conditions tested; it does not automatically establish readiness for every process or site.

Training should give each role a task relevant to its work. The process owner may need to explain a decision boundary, while an operator may need to locate an instruction and identify whom to contact when it is unavailable. Capture where participants needed help and distinguish an access question from an unclear process explanation. The organization can then decide whether to proceed, narrow the rollout, or obtain more evidence.

After implementation, a changed role, instruction, or interface may require the map to be reviewed. Agree on who considers the change, what evidence is needed, and how an open issue is recorded. Ask the people who use the map to test the revised explanation. A published product description can frame the discussion, but the organization remains responsible for its own assignments, records, and acceptance decisions.

Use the published [process management description](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/processes/) alongside the guide's questions to prepare a representative demonstration. The useful outcome is a traceable decision: the team understands its need, the evidence examined, the limits of what was shown, and the next action for any unresolved issue.

Keep that decision accessible to the people who will carry it forward. A later review can then distinguish a condition that was accepted for a limited scope from a question that still needs evidence before the team expands its use.
