## Group processes by purpose

Place the processes under consideration on a working list before naming groups. The quality lead and operations manager can test whether each label helps a reader find work across departmental boundaries. A disputed entry needs a decision owner and an explanation of its scope; an overview should reveal useful relationships rather than duplicate the organization chart.

The quality lead can assemble a working list from existing maps and process records. The process owners and operations manager then examine the list together. For each item, identify its purpose, the people who depend on its output, and whether it belongs within the scope of the proposed architecture. Record disputed entries and the person who will resolve them. When two groups claim the same activity, identify the decision that belongs to each before deciding whether the overview needs one entry or two. A list compiled by one department may omit work that another department treats as an essential input.

The categories on the [ICC process management page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/processes/) provide one published reference for grouping. Test the proposed categories with a process that serves more than one purpose: decide where its primary entry belongs, then identify the other relationship as an interface. The team should confirm that its own labels work for intended readers.

Purpose is more useful than department alone when work crosses organizational boundaries. A production activity may depend on planning, maintenance, and quality input, even when those activities are managed by different people. The team can place the activity in a group that makes sense for the intended overview, then show its relationships to other processes. The grouping does not change who performs the work or settle every detailed responsibility.

Use a short description for each candidate group. State what the group helps a reader find and give examples drawn from the organization's own records. If two groups seem to include the same process, ask whether the process is duplicated in the list or serves more than one purpose. Record the decision and any remaining uncertainty rather than creating two inconsistent entries simply to fill both groups.

Test the grouping with an evidence trail: can a reviewer trace the production process to the support input it needs? The [ISO auditing guidance collection](https://committee.iso.org/home/tc176/iso-9001-auditing-practices-group.html) offers educational material on process auditing. If the overview hides that relationship, mark the gap, ask both affected owners to examine the sample, and record which representation they choose and why it helps the intended reader.

**Illustrative example:** Classify a hypothetical maintenance activity needed before production resumes. Option A places it under support with a connection to production; option B places it inside production. Ask a new reader to find it in both arrangements. The owners choose the grouping that makes scope and the interface understandable, and document which decision remains shared.

## Define the top-level map

The top-level map is the entry point to the architecture. Decide who will use it and what question it should answer before deciding how many boxes to display. A new team member may need to find the process relevant to a customer request; a process owner may need to identify an adjacent process for a review. Both should be able to understand the labels without relying entirely on a presenter.

The [ICC description](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/processes/) presents a top-level process map and detailed flowcharts. Use that description as a point of comparison when designing an overview. It does not establish the layout, navigation, or configuration of a particular installation. Ask for a demonstration of the proposed arrangement using the organization's own sample, and distinguish what is shown from what remains a design question.

Begin with the agreed groups and process names. For each entry, check that its name identifies the work in terms familiar to its intended reader. Where a local abbreviation is necessary, explain it in the surrounding documentation. A label can be concise without being ambiguous. If two processes have similar names, add enough context for a reader to choose between them without needing to know which department maintains the record.

The map should show meaningful relationships without attempting to reproduce the complete workflow. Consider which connections are essential for understanding how an output reaches another process. Too many lines can make an overview difficult to use, while no visible relationship may leave the impression that processes operate independently. The quality lead and process owners should agree on the conventions and record what a connection means.

A simple design review can use representative questions. Ask a reader to locate a process from its purpose, identify an adjacent process, and explain where they would go for more detail. Ask the process owner whether the overview omits a relationship needed to understand the example. If the answers conflict, record the issue and resolve it with the people who know the work. A tidy layout alone is not evidence that the architecture represents actual practice.

Show the scope of the overview. If it applies to a particular site or product line, identify that limitation in the accompanying record. If a shared process differs by location, decide whether the top-level entry refers to a common process with local details or whether separate entries are needed. Do not infer that either choice is automatically correct. The decision should make the route to the applicable detail clear to the reader.

**Illustrative example:** In the hypothetical overview exercise, the process owners sketch an overview with a production entry and its important neighboring processes. An operator unfamiliar with the sketch tries to find the sample workflow. The team notes where the label or connection caused confusion and revises the proposal before accepting it. Use the reader's route to locate the ambiguous label, then ask the relevant owners to agree on its scope.

Record the accepted overview, its scope, the people who reviewed it, and outstanding exceptions. If an important process has no agreed representation, mark the item as unresolved and assign a decision owner. The map should not present an uncertain relationship as settled merely because the diagram has space for a connecting line.

## Connect levels of detail

An overview is useful when a reader can move from a broad process entry to the detail needed for a task and return without losing context. Decide what each level is meant to explain. The overview can identify processes and their relationships; a more detailed view can explain a selected process. A supporting instruction or record may provide additional information, but it should not be mistaken for the architecture itself.

The [ICC process page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/processes/) describes a level-two flowchart for each process and links from processes to controlled documents and standard clauses. Those published descriptions provide a basis for questions about the route from overview to detail. They do not establish the exact behavior of every link, revision, or permission. Ask the supplier to show the proposed route and record the observed result.

Choose a representative process and trace it through the proposed levels. The quality lead checks that the name on the overview corresponds to the detail the reader reaches. The process owner checks that the detail belongs to the intended scope. A reader then tries to find the relevant starting point and explain where the process ends. If the name or scope changes between levels, record the difference and decide whether it reflects a deliberate distinction or an error.

Avoid placing all available information at the top level simply because it can be displayed there. A reader looking for a process should not have to inspect every detailed activity first. Equally, do not make the detailed view so detached that the reader cannot tell which process or site it represents. The team can test the balance with a realistic question and note where a user needs additional context.

Consider exceptions before treating the hierarchy as complete. A detailed process may be shared by several sites, while one site uses a different instruction. A process may also be undergoing revision, leaving an older detail and a proposed replacement. Decide how the organization will distinguish these situations in its own records and ask how the proposed arrangement supports that decision. The presence of a link is not proof that the destination is current or accessible to every intended user.

In the hypothetical manufacturer's example, the production entry leads the test reader to a flowchart for the selected workflow. The owner compares the heading and boundaries with the overview, and an operator checks whether the view is understandable. The team records a discrepancy when the detail refers to a different product line. The correction may be to clarify scope or provide the appropriate detail; the test itself does not decide the organization's future configuration.

## Review interfaces between processes

An architecture should make the important interfaces visible enough for the people involved to discuss them. Select an output that one process provides to another and ask what the recipient needs in order to act. The relationship can be represented at the overview level without duplicating every decision in either detailed workflow. Its usefulness depends on whether both sides recognize the connection and can identify an accountable person for an open issue.

The [ICC description](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/processes/) provides an example of a top-level process map. Use it to frame a question about how proposed relationships could be represented, not as proof that a specific handoff is automatically controlled. The organization must examine its own process evidence and decide how the relationship should be shown. A line on a diagram is a representation of a decision, not evidence that the work always follows it.

Bring the relevant process owners together with a sample record or other appropriate evidence. Ask the sending side what it considers complete and the receiving side what it needs to proceed. Record any difference in terms, timing expectations, or missing information. The example may reveal an operational issue, a documentation issue, or simply an unclear map label. Do not assume the cause before the people involved examine the evidence.

The review can consider normal and exceptional paths. If a planned input is unavailable or a record is incomplete, ask who is expected to make the next decision and how the issue is recorded under the organization's arrangements. The architecture need not contain every instruction for handling the exception, but it should not suggest an agreed route where none exists. Mark unresolved relationships so they can be addressed by the responsible people.

Use a small set of representative interfaces rather than attempting to validate the whole organization in a single meeting. Select examples with different types of relationship, such as a production output used by another process and a support input needed before work can proceed. The team should be able to state why each interface appears on the overview and where a reader can find further detail. If a line adds no useful information, reconsider it rather than retaining it solely for symmetry.

**Illustrative example:** Consider the hypothetical interface review: the production owner and the owner of an adjoining process review a sample output. One person describes the record that is sent; the other explains what must be understood before work can continue. The quality lead records agreement and identifies an open question about an unusual case. The team retains the open question with its decision owner instead of presenting the relationship as fully verified.

After the review, document the accepted changes and the exceptions that remain. Note the sample and the participants so a later reader can judge the reach of the decision. If one interface changes, check whether the top-level representation and its connected detail still describe the same scope. This check is an organizational review practice; it does not imply that a product automatically detects or updates the relationship.

Give the accepted overview to a reader who knows the work but did not design the grouping. Ask that person to locate the production process, identify its supporting interface, and explain which detailed view applies. Record any ambiguous label with its decision owner before approving the overview.
