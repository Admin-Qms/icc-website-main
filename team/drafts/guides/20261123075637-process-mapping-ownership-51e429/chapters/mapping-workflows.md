## Gather task evidence

A workflow map is a working explanation of how a selected process proceeds. To make it useful, start with evidence of the work and the people who perform it. The process owner can bring together an operator, a floor supervisor, and the quality lead around a representative record. Their task is to identify the sequence and decisions shown by that example, then note where additional evidence is needed before drawing conclusions.

Select a sample that matches the agreed process boundary. A completed record can show an input, an action, or an output, but it may not explain why a decision was made. Ask the people involved to explain what they looked for and what they did when information was incomplete. Keep the explanation separate from the record itself so that a later reviewer can distinguish observed evidence from a person's interpretation.

A flowchart is only as useful as the evidence chosen for its decisions. The [ICC process management page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/processes/) describes level-two process flowcharts and document links. For the selected step, collect a record showing the input and ask the worker what decision followed. The published description does not establish that the site's map or its linked instruction is accurate.

Gather examples from the roles that take part, not only the person who maintains the existing diagram. An operator may know how an unusual input is recognized, while a supervisor may explain where a decision is escalated. The quality lead can record each observation and identify the supporting material. If two accounts differ, do not choose one merely because it fits the existing map. Note the disagreement, identify what evidence could clarify it, and assign a person to decide the next action.

The [ISO auditing guidance collection](https://committee.iso.org/home/tc176/iso-9001-auditing-practices-group.html) includes educational material on evidence collection and says its guidance is not definitive or mandatory audit criteria. Here the useful question is specific: does the sample show why the operator selected a route, or is that explanation known only to a supervisor? Record the missing explanation and ask the responsible people to verify it.

**Illustrative example:** A hypothetical production record shows an incomplete input at a decision point. The operator places a marker on the draft where work stopped, while the supervisor identifies the person who can decide the next step. The quality lead retains the sample reference and the unresolved decision instead of drawing a route that nobody has confirmed.

Record enough context to make the evidence usable: the process and scope considered, the roles consulted, the sample examined, and any limits on what it shows. Use appropriate access controls for actual records. A missing or inaccessible record is itself an issue to document; it is not permission to invent the missing step. The owner can decide whether another sample, a discussion, or escalation is needed before the map is accepted.

Avoid confusing an intended procedure with an observed instance. A written instruction may describe the planned route, while a sample or worker may reveal a different route. Both are useful inputs, but the distinction matters. Record which route is described by which source, then ask the responsible people to determine whether the map should show the current practice, an intended change, or an unresolved discrepancy.

## Draw steps and decision points

Once the evidence is assembled, describe the actions in the order needed to follow the chosen process. Use verbs that identify what a role does, and connect each step to an input or output that a reader can recognize. The map need not contain every detail of a work instruction. It should give enough context for the intended reader to follow the sequence and know where further information is needed.

The [ICC page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/processes/) describes process flowcharts beneath a top-level map. Treat that as a published description of the vendor's approach, then ask to see how a representative flow could be represented in the proposed configuration. Do not assume particular symbols, editing controls, or automatic behavior unless demonstrated and accepted for the intended use.

At a decision point, state the question being answered and identify the possible routes relevant to the sample. A label such as "review" does not show what the reviewer is deciding. A clearer description identifies the information considered and the decision that follows. Where the criteria are maintained elsewhere, identify that the reader needs the applicable information rather than inventing a rule in the diagram.

The process owner should review the proposed sequence with the people who perform the work. Ask whether a step is missing, whether the role shown is correct, and whether the route makes sense when read without the author's explanation. The quality lead records the feedback and the proposed change. If the participants disagree about the actual sequence, mark the point as unresolved and identify who will determine the next action.

Consider the granularity of a step. "Prepare the record" may be sufficient on an overview if the reader can find the applicable detail; it may be too broad if the purpose of this map is to show who checks the record before a handoff. Conversely, including every keystroke may obscure the decision the map is intended to explain. Choose the level based on the map's stated purpose and the reader's question.

For the illustrative manufacturer, the operator and supervisor walk through the hypothetical sample. They describe the action that creates an output, the question used to decide whether it can proceed, and the next role that receives it. The owner compares those explanations with the draft and records one unclear decision for follow-up. The map remains a proposal until the responsible people accept it under their own process.

A small review record can identify the version examined, the sample used, the participants, and the decisions that remain open. If the proposed flow is later changed, the organization can see which evidence supported the earlier version. This is an organizational practice for making the explanation traceable, not a claim that the software automatically maintains a particular review history.

The team can also test whether the flow has a recognizable beginning and end within the agreed boundary. If an input originates in another process, show enough context to identify the interface without attempting to redraw the whole neighboring process. If the final output leaves the scope, identify its recipient or the point at which the next process takes responsibility. Record unresolved boundaries rather than forcing an artificial end.

## Describe exceptions and handoffs

A workflow that shows only the expected route may leave readers unsure what to do when an input is incomplete or a decision cannot be made. Select exceptions that the process owner and workers consider relevant to the sample. Describe the next responsible role and the information needed to decide what happens. The aim is to make an agreed route understandable, not to predict every possible event.

The [ICC process page](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/processes/) describes flowcharts and named process owners. It does not establish that a specific exception is automatically routed or that a notification will be sent. Ask how the proposed map can show the organization's agreed exception path and test the answer with a representative case. Record what was demonstrated and what remains a question.

A handoff is complete only when the receiving role has what it needs to act. On the draft, identify the output being passed, the recipient, and any decision needed before the work can continue. Ask the receiving person whether the description is sufficient. A label that says "send to quality" may be too vague if the reader cannot identify the record or the next decision. The map can point to relevant detail without reproducing the entire instruction.

If the sending and receiving roles describe different expectations, record both views. The owner can convene the people involved and determine whether the disagreement concerns the actual work, the terminology, or the map's scope. Until it is resolved, identify the interface as open in the review record. Do not draw a confident arrow and assume the underlying agreement exists.

An exception can return work to an earlier step, require a different decision-maker, or stop the process until information is available. These are possible patterns to discuss, not rules that apply to every site. The people responsible should determine the appropriate route using their own procedures and evidence. Where no route is agreed, record the decision owner and an interim action if one has been authorized.

For the hypothetical manufacturer, the sample output is missing information that the next role needs. The operator explains how the incomplete output would be recognized, and the supervisor identifies whom to contact under the agreed arrangement. The quality lead notes the record used and a question about an unusual case. The team does not assume that a software tool makes the decision on its own.

When several exceptions lead to the same next decision, the team may use a concise shared route if readers can still tell what information is required. When exceptions have materially different consequences, combining them may conceal an important choice. Test the proposed drawing with the people who would use it. If a reader cannot explain the next action, clarify the map or identify the information that needs a separate controlled instruction.

## Validate a map with workers

Validation is a structured check that the draft communicates the agreed process to its intended readers. The process owner can select a representative example and invite the roles involved to trace it from input to output. The quality lead records the observations, while the owner determines what changes or further evidence are needed. Acceptance should follow the organization's normal arrangements rather than the map author's preference alone.

The [ICC process management description](https://isocertificationconsultants.ca/solutions/processes/) says supported PDF process charts are reviewed before acceptance. That is a published vendor description, not proof that a specific site's map has been validated or that all file types follow the same path. Ask how the proposed workflow would handle the organization's sample and who is expected to review and accept it.

Give each participant a concrete task. An operator might identify the information needed at a decision point; a supervisor might follow an exception; the receiving role might locate the output it expects. Ask participants to explain the route in their own words before providing hints. The point is to find ambiguity in the map, not to test whether a worker can guess the author's intent.

Compare their answers with the evidence gathered earlier. If the map omits an action shown in the sample, ask whether that action belongs within the agreed scope. If a worker describes a different route, record the discrepancy and seek the information needed to resolve it. A disagreement can reveal a local variation, an outdated instruction, or an incomplete sample; do not assign a cause without evidence.

Include the exception and handoff in the walkthrough. Ask what happens when the information needed by the next role is unavailable, and who makes the next decision. If the participants cannot agree, record the open issue and its resolution owner. The team can decide whether the draft is suitable for a limited purpose while that issue remains, but it should not represent the disputed path as fully agreed.

For the illustrative manufacturer, the operator traces the hypothetical production record while the supervisor checks the exception route and the receiving role identifies the expected output. The process owner reviews their comments and the quality lead records changes and an unresolved question. The group then decides whether additional evidence is needed before accepting the map. The example is hypothetical and does not imply a customer result.

Record the map version or other identifier, the scope, the people who participated, the sample used, and the decision reached. Keep the record with the organization's other controlled information as appropriate. If validation identifies a needed correction, name the person responsible and verify the revised explanation with affected roles. An accepted map still needs review when the underlying work changes; a successful walkthrough is evidence about the tested sample, not a guarantee that all future cases are covered.

Hand the draft to a worker and ask for a walkthrough of the incomplete-input case. Note the precise step at which the worker needs help and whether the receiving role has enough information to act. The process owner can then accept a correction or assign further investigation.
