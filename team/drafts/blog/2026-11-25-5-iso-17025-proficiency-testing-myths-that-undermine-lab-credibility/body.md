> **Key Takeaways**
> - A proficiency testing plan should reflect the laboratory's scope, risks, available schemes, and applicable accreditation policies.
> - Provider accreditation is useful evidence, but the scheme must also suit the method, item, and measurement range.
> - An unsatisfactory result calls for a documented evaluation and response; its accreditation consequences depend on the facts and applicable policy.
> - Proficiency testing results can inform an uncertainty evaluation, but they do not automatically replace a laboratory's uncertainty budget.

How can a laboratory show that its results remain reliable between assessments? ISO/IEC 17025:2017 requires laboratories to monitor the validity of their results, including comparison with other laboratories when available and appropriate. Understanding proficiency testing requirements means examining the actual scope of work and the accreditation body's policy, not relying on a single calendar entry.

Proficiency testing (PT) evaluates participant performance against established criteria through interlaboratory comparisons. It can reveal a problem that routine checks miss, but one round tests only the particular item, measurand, method, and conditions involved. A defensible plan explains what is covered, what remains uncovered, and how the laboratory will respond to the evidence.

## Myth 1: One proficiency test a year is always enough

A date on a calendar is a reminder, not a coverage plan. ISO/IEC 17025 does not prescribe one universal annual frequency for every test and calibration activity. Accreditation bodies can impose policies for participation and coverage, so a laboratory needs to identify the rules that apply to its own scope.

[IMAGE: A technician wearing safety glasses, high-visibility vest and gloves examines a metal test specimen with a digital caliper beside guarded industrial testing equipment on a manufacturing floor]

A useful plan starts with the accredited activities and groups of technically related methods. It then considers the frequency of work, changes in personnel or equipment, previous results, the availability of suitable schemes, and the risk of an undetected error. The [ISO/IEC 17025 accreditation overview](/services/iso-17025) explains the wider context for laboratory competence and accreditation.

**A plan must connect participation to scope.** Record the parameter, matrix or item, measurement range, provider, expected round, and the method or activity represented. The laboratory should also identify activities for which no suitable round has been found, along with the alternative comparisons or other validity monitoring it will use as appropriate.

- Review the current scope and accreditation policy before committing to a schedule; a scheme outside the scope does not demonstrate coverage of an untested activity.
- Compare the planned rounds with actual work and previous performance; changes in a method or a recurring concern may justify revisiting the plan.
- Assign an owner to monitor openings and deadlines; document a missed round and assess its effect rather than silently moving the date.

**Illustrative example:** An unnamed testing laboratory plans a round for a frequently performed measurement and a separate comparison for another part of its scope. When a provider cancels one round, the laboratory records the gap, evaluates an appropriate alternative, and checks the relevant accreditation policy before changing the plan.

> **Important:** The applicable accreditation body's participation policy and the laboratory's actual scope both matter when setting coverage and timing.

A manager can use a coverage matrix to see which activities have recent comparison evidence and which need attention. The review should identify who will close each gap and when the plan will next be reconsidered, including any limits on the conclusions drawn from the available comparison. A blank cell should lead to a decision, not an unsupported claim of equivalence. Keep the reason for grouping activities and the evidence supporting that decision with the plan, so the next reviewer can understand how the laboratory reached it.

## Myth 2: Any accredited provider is suitable for every test

Provider accreditation can give confidence that a scheme operates under assessed requirements, but it does not establish that every offering matches the laboratory's needs. The scheme should address the intended measurement, item, range, and technique closely enough to provide meaningful evidence about the activity under review.

[IMAGE: A gloved inspector checks a machined steel specimen with a handheld gauge beside a guarded materials testing machine in an industrial workshop]

The [Canadian Association for Laboratory Accreditation](https://cala.ca/) provides laboratory accreditation and proficiency testing services. Before selecting any provider, a laboratory should examine the specific scheme description and any accreditation scope relevant to that scheme. The name of a provider alone is not a substitute for that review.

| Selection question | What the laboratory should examine |
|---|---|
| Is the item relevant? | Compare the material or matrix with routine work. |
| Is the measurement relevant? | Check the measurand, method, units, and range. |
| Can the result be interpreted? | Review the assigned value and performance criteria. |
| Is the schedule workable? | Confirm handling, reporting, and participation deadlines. |

**Document the reason the scheme fits.** A scheme may use an item that is stable and practical to distribute but different from a routine sample. The laboratory should consider whether that difference limits the conclusions it can draw, and whether other evidence is needed for the uncovered part of the work.

When a suitable commercial scheme is unavailable, a laboratory should investigate other interlaboratory comparisons or approaches appropriate to its activities and accreditation body's rules. The design needs a clear comparison, a technically sound basis for evaluation, and records that explain the outcome. Calling an informal exchange a proficiency test does not by itself make its results meaningful.

> **Key Consideration:** Check a scheme's technical fit before enrollment, then retain the selection rationale alongside the participation record.

The laboratory should also establish how the item will be received, stored, handled, tested, and reported. The aim is to assess routine performance, so the sample should be treated consistently with the laboratory's normal work as far as the scheme permits. Special handling, departures from the routine method, or problems with the item should be recorded and considered when interpreting the outcome.

## Myth 3: One failed round automatically suspends accreditation

An unsatisfactory PT outcome is serious evidence that needs attention, but it is not useful to promise a universal accreditation consequence. The response depends on the result, the laboratory's investigation, the scope affected, and the accreditation body's requirements and decisions. Laboratories should consult their own accreditation body and follow applicable notification obligations.

The first question is what the result means. Check the reported value and units, the item identity, the method, the provider's evaluation, and whether a transcription or calculation error occurred. Those checks form part of an investigation; they should not be used to dismiss an inconvenient result without evidence.

**A correction and corrective action answer different questions.** A correction addresses an identified problem, such as an incorrect transcription. Corrective action addresses the cause of a nonconformity and seeks to prevent recurrence; the laboratory should document the cause, action, responsible person, and how effectiveness will be checked.

- Assess potential impact on customer results and work performed since the last reliable check; record the scope and basis for that assessment.
- Contain affected work when necessary under the laboratory's procedures, and determine whether customers or the accreditation body must be informed.
- Investigate plausible causes using records, equipment checks, calculations, personnel information, and the provider's report rather than selecting a cause by assumption.
- Verify that the chosen action works and retain the supporting evidence; a completed training form alone may not demonstrate that a measurement problem is resolved.

**Illustrative example:** An unnamed laboratory receives an unsatisfactory result and finds that the reported unit was transcribed incorrectly. It checks the original calculation, evaluates whether the same reporting pathway affected customer work, corrects the control weakness, and uses a subsequent verification to test the revised process.

A repeat result may be useful evidence, but repeating the same test without investigation can hide the cause rather than resolve it. A satisfactory repeat also does not automatically prove that earlier customer results were unaffected. The [internal audit checklist for laboratories](/blog/iso-17025-internal-audit-checklist-10-areas-testing-laboratories-should-verify) can help a team examine whether its records tell a coherent story from finding to follow-up.

## Myth 4: PT results have no relationship to measurement uncertainty

PT results and measurement uncertainty answer related but different questions. An uncertainty estimate characterizes dispersion associated with a result, while a PT evaluation compares reported performance with a scheme's assigned value and evaluation criteria. A satisfactory score does not automatically validate every component of an uncertainty budget or demonstrate that the estimate is appropriate for all routine work.

[IMAGE: A technician in safety glasses, gloves and a high-visibility vest places a metal specimen into a guarded industrial measurement instrument on a factory floor]

A laboratory can examine relevant comparison data when evaluating its methods and uncertainty. For example, a persistent difference from suitable reference or comparison values may prompt investigation of bias, method conditions, or an omitted contribution. Whether and how those data affect an uncertainty estimate depends on the method, the data, and the laboratory's evaluation.

**Keep the performance score and uncertainty calculation distinct.** The scheme's performance statistic uses its own assigned value and evaluation parameters. Those parameters should not be copied into the laboratory's uncertainty budget without a technical justification for their relevance to the laboratory's measurement process.

The [measurement uncertainty checklist](/blog/iso-17025-measurement-uncertainty-11-point-compliance-checklist-for-ontario) describes the need to connect a method, its inputs, and the reported result. When reviewing PT data, record the item and range, the comparison basis, any known limitations, and whether the observation is representative of routine work. A single round may offer a useful signal without supporting a broad numerical conclusion.

> **Important:** An unusual result should trigger technical evaluation; it should not be used to alter an uncertainty estimate without understanding its cause and relevance.

Consider the measurement range as well as the average result. A comparison near one point may not cover behavior at another point where a method is used. If the laboratory groups activities for planning purposes, it should explain the technical basis for the grouping and revisit it when equipment, methods, or scope change.

## Myth 5: Only chemical and microbiological laboratories need comparisons

The need to monitor result validity is not confined to one scientific discipline. The standard applies to testing and calibration laboratories across many technical activities; the appropriate comparison and evidence depend on the work performed and the available opportunities. [ISO's page for ISO/IEC 17025:2017](https://www.iso.org/standard/66912.html) describes the standard's scope for laboratory competence.

A dimensional measurement laboratory, a materials testing facility, and a chemical laboratory will not necessarily use the same type of item or scheme. The plan should start with the activity and its risks, then identify an appropriate comparison rather than assuming that an unfamiliar field is exempt. Accreditation body policies and the laboratory's scope remain important to that decision.

**Illustrative example:** An unnamed laboratory performing dimensional measurements selects a comparison involving a suitable artifact and measurement range. It records the conditions, the comparison result, and the limits of what that artifact demonstrates; other dimensions outside the comparison remain visible in its coverage plan.

A well-managed record includes the enrollment decision, instructions, raw observations, reported result, provider report, technical review, and any follow-up. Linking those pieces makes it possible to distinguish a missed deadline from a technical failure, or a satisfactory result from coverage of an unrelated activity. Management should periodically review open gaps and decide whether the plan remains appropriate.
