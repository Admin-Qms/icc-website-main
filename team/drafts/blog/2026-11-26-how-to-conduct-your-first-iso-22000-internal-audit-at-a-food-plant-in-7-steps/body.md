> **Key Takeaways**
> - Set the audit scope around the plant's food safety processes, shifts and external providers.
> - Follow evidence from the food safety plan to the people and records that show how controls operate.
> - Record findings against clear criteria and assign action owners without delaying an urgent product-safety response.
> - Check whether completed actions worked before treating recurring issues as resolved.

An ISO 22000 internal audit at a food plant is a practical examination of whether the food safety management system works as planned. The audit follows ingredients, people, equipment and records through the operation; it is not simply a review of a binder before a certification visit.

A first audit can feel difficult because production, sanitation, maintenance and purchasing each hold part of the evidence. The [food and beverage industry overview](/industries/food-beverage) describes the operating context in which these teams work. A useful plan identifies those handoffs in advance, samples work across the plant and gives the audit team a reliable way to record what it finds. The [ISO 22000:2018 standard page](https://www.iso.org/standard/65464.html) describes the food safety management system framework that guides the audit.

> **Key Consideration:** An internal audit tests the system against agreed criteria; it does not replace routine monitoring or an immediate response to a food safety concern.

## Step 1: Define the audit scope and schedule

Start with the management system's stated scope and translate it into an audit plan. The [ISO 22000 food safety overview](/services/iso-22000) explains the broader management system context. Identify the products, activities, locations, shifts and interfaces with external providers that fall inside the system. Include management processes as well as production: decisions about resources, communication and follow-up affect how controls work on the floor.

[IMAGE: A food plant quality auditor in a hairnet and protective clothing observing a stainless steel processing line from a safe designated walkway]

Choose the criteria before asking questions. These may include applicable requirements from ISO 22000:2018, the plant's procedures, its food safety plan and relevant legal or customer obligations. A checklist can help organize questions, but a checklist item should point to a real criterion rather than become a requirement by itself.

Set a schedule that lets the auditor observe representative work. A sanitation activity before startup, a receiving check and a packaging changeover may happen at different times. If a process is inactive, record the limitation and arrange another way to gather sufficient evidence instead of assuming that a procedure reflects current practice.

- The scope identifies where the audit starts and ends, including relevant shifts and outsourced activities.
- The plan assigns audit areas, people to contact and enough time to examine records and observe work.
- The criteria identify the obligations or internal arrangements against which evidence will be assessed.

## Step 2: Gather and review documentation

Request the documents needed to understand the system before entering the production area. These may include the scope, process descriptions, food safety policy and objectives, hazard analysis, control measures, prerequisite programs, emergency arrangements and previous audit results. Ask for the approved versions and the records that demonstrate implementation.

Review records across a period that is meaningful for the activity. For a monitoring record, look at the stated limit or criterion, the result, the date and the action recorded when the result was outside the expected range. For maintenance or sanitation, compare the planned activity with evidence that it was completed and checked.

**A document is audit criteria only when it is the applicable approved version.** An obsolete instruction left at a workstation raises a different question from an incomplete record made against a current instruction. Keep those observations separate so the finding points to the actual problem.

> **Important:** A missing record is not proof that a control failed, but it can limit the evidence available to demonstrate that the control was carried out.

The [Canadian Food Inspection Agency's food safety resources](https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-safety-industry) help businesses identify federal food safety obligations relevant to their activities. The auditor should check the requirements that apply to the particular operation rather than assume that every facility has the same licensing or preventive-control obligations.

## Step 3: Plan the audit team and competencies

Select auditors who understand audit methods and have enough knowledge of food safety and the processes being examined. The team should be able to recognize the difference between a process requirement, an operating limit and an observation requiring further verification. Where specialized knowledge is needed, involve a competent technical resource within the audit arrangements.

**Objectivity matters more than familiarity with a form.** Assign auditors so they do not audit their own work, and identify any other relationship that could make an impartial assessment difficult. A smaller plant may need to arrange independent support or coordinate across functions; the appropriate arrangement depends on the people and competencies available.

Before the audit, agree on how the team will handle an urgent concern, keep records secure and escalate a potential product-safety issue. An audit finding can be documented later; a possible unsafe product needs to enter the plant's existing control and escalation process immediately.

- Each auditor knows the assigned process, audit criteria and limits of the sampling plan.
- The contact for food safety escalation is available during the audit and understands the notification route.
- Auditors agree how to record observations without interrupting a critical control activity.
- The team understands that a sampled record supports a conclusion about that sample, not a guarantee about every product.

## Step 4: Audit prerequisite programs

Prerequisite programs support the conditions needed to produce safe food. Depending on the operation, the audit may examine cleaning and sanitation, pest control, maintenance, personnel hygiene, allergen management, utilities or supplier controls. Use the plant's own program, applicable requirements and the chosen audit criteria to decide what belongs in the sample.

[IMAGE: A food safety inspector wearing a hairnet and protective clothing examines a stainless steel filling machine on a clean food production line]

Trace one activity from its planned instruction to its execution and verification. For sanitation, compare the approved method and schedule with the relevant completion record, then observe a suitable area or speak with the responsible person. If a deviation occurred, look for the documented response and any follow-up required by the plant's procedure.

Check interfaces rather than treating departments as separate islands. Maintenance work can affect cleaning readiness, and a supplier change can affect receiving checks. An auditor should ask how the responsible people know when a change has occurred and which documents or records need review.

**Look for evidence of implementation, not a perfect-looking checklist.** A signed form with no clear link to the area or activity may not answer the audit question. Equally, an unusual observation deserves context: confirm timing, the applicable instruction and the status of the activity before deciding whether it is a finding.

## Step 5: Review HACCP plans and control points

Review how the food safety team identifies hazards, selects control measures and determines how those measures are managed. Follow the plant's hazard analysis and food safety plan for the products and processes in scope. The [Codex Alimentarius resources](https://www.fao.org/fao-who-codexalimentarius/en/) provide background on food hygiene and HACCP principles; the audit still needs to assess the plant's actual documented arrangements.

Select a control measure in the audit sample and trace it through the applicable plan, monitoring instruction and records. Establish who performs the check, what is measured, what criterion applies and what happens when monitoring indicates a problem. Review the response against the procedure and the evidence available for the affected product.

Different activities answer different questions. Monitoring checks whether an operational control is being performed as specified; verification examines whether specified requirements have been fulfilled. Validation concerns evidence that selected control measures are capable of achieving the intended control before they are relied on, and after relevant changes where appropriate. Use the plant's plan and the standard's terms when deciding what evidence is needed.

> **Key Consideration:** Do not assume that a monitoring record alone proves that the underlying hazard analysis is current or that the control measure is suitable.

## Step 6: Interview staff and observe processes

Arrange interviews around real work. Ask personnel to explain their responsibilities, identify the instruction they use and describe what happens when a result is unexpected. Observe without disrupting production or creating a hygiene hazard. Where access is restricted, use the plant's approved entry and observation arrangements.

Compare the account with the applicable procedure and a sample of records. If a receiving operator describes holding material after a failed check, find out where the hold is recorded, who can change its status and how the next shift knows the material remains restricted. Do not infer a failure from a different choice of words; check whether the described action meets the actual criterion.

Auditing also requires listening for handoffs. A sanitation employee may complete a task, while another person verifies the result before production begins. The audit should establish how the second person receives the information and what happens if verification cannot be completed as planned.

**Illustrative example:** An unnamed food processor changes a cleaning chemical. The auditor follows the change from its authorization through the updated instruction, staff communication and relevant verification records. The example is hypothetical; actual evidence depends on the plant's process and the nature of the change.

## Step 7: Document findings and plan corrective actions

A useful finding identifies the criterion, the objective evidence and the gap between them. Record the location, process and sample details needed for the responsible team to understand what was examined. Avoid language that assigns blame or claims that every record is defective when the audit examined only a limited sample.

Discuss factual accuracy with the process owner before closing the audit. The owner can identify a relevant record or clarify the status of an activity; that does not mean the owner decides the audit result. The auditor should retain the evidence supporting the conclusion and explain any remaining limitations.

Separate immediate correction from corrective action. Correction addresses the detected issue; corrective action addresses the cause of a nonconformity and seeks to prevent recurrence. The plant's process should determine responsibility, timing, review and the evidence needed to decide whether an action worked.

For a potential product-safety issue, follow the established product-control and escalation arrangements without waiting for the final audit report. Management and the food safety team decide the appropriate response using the information available, applicable requirements and the plant's procedures.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How often should a food plant conduct ISO 22000 internal audits?

The organization establishes an audit program at planned intervals, considering the importance of processes, changes and previous audit results. The right schedule depends on the plant's scope and risks; the first audit plan should show how the required system activities will be covered.

### What areas should an internal audit examine?

The audit examines the food safety management system against the selected criteria. Depending on scope, that includes management arrangements, prerequisite programs, hazard controls, communication, verification and follow-up. It should follow the plant's actual processes and evidence rather than rely on a generic checklist alone.

### Does a plant still need external auditors?

An internal audit and an independent certification audit serve different purposes. If the organization seeks accredited certification, its certification body makes arrangements for external assessment. An internal audit does not itself grant certification or replace regulatory inspection.

### What kinds of nonconformances might an auditor find?

A finding might involve an outdated instruction, an incomplete monitoring record or a missed follow-up, provided objective evidence shows a gap against an applicable criterion. The auditor should not assume that these examples describe a particular plant or its most frequent issues.

### What happens when a finding may affect product safety?

Notify the designated food safety contact immediately and follow the plant's arrangements for controlling potentially affected product. Record the audit evidence, but do not wait for the report before the responsible people assess the concern and decide what action is appropriate.
