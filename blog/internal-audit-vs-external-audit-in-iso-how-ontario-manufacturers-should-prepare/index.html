Internal Audit vs External Audit in ISO: How Ontario Manufacturers Should Prepare

Understand internal audit vs external audit in ISO, including purpose, timing, auditor independence, findings, and how manufacturers can prepare.

ISO Certification Consultants Editorial Team·November 6, 2026·11 min read

Key Takeaways

  • An internal audit is the organization checking itself; an external audit is an independent party checking whether the system meets the standard.
  • Internal audits must be planned at intervals the organization defines, so they are a standing requirement, not a pre-certification exercise.
  • External audits run on the certification body's cycle, while internal audit results feed the evidence the external auditor reviews.
  • A nonconformity found internally is a chance to fix a gap quietly; the same gap found externally becomes a formal finding on record.
  • Ontario manufacturers need both, and the stronger the internal program, the fewer surprises on audit day.

The distinction between an internal audit and an external one involves more than who walks the floor. The comparison of internal audit vs external audit in ISO comes down to purpose, independence, timing and what happens to the findings afterward, and each of those changes how a shop should prepare.

This article explains the difference in plain terms for Ontario manufacturers working toward, or already holding, an ISO 9001:2026 certificate. Most of the points apply equally to ISO 14001:2026, ISO 45001:2018 and other management system standards. Many organizations certified to ISO 14001 remain on the 2015 edition during the transition.

What Internal and External ISO Audits Are Designed to Assess

An internal audit asks a practical question: is the management system doing what the organization said it would do, and does it still meet the standard? The requirement sits in clause 9.2 of ISO 9001:2015, which asks the organization to conduct audits at planned intervals and to report the results to management. Many certified organizations are still on the 2015 edition during the transition to ISO 9001:2026, which is now the current edition.

An external audit asks a different question: can an independent party confirm that the system conforms to the standard and is working? For certification, that party is a certification body. First-party audits (internal) and third-party audits (certification) share a method, but they answer to different readers.

Purpose in one sentence each

The internal audit exists so management learns about problems before anyone outside the company does. The external audit exists so customers, regulators and buyers can rely on a certificate that someone independent has verified.

Scope differences

An internal audit program usually covers every process over a defined cycle, with more attention on areas that carry higher risk or a history of problems. An external audit samples. The auditor cannot look at everything, so the sample follows the audit plan agreed with the certification body.

Who Conducts Each Audit and What Independence Requires

The standard asks that internal auditors be objective and impartial. In practice, that means an auditor does not audit their own work. A machinist can audit receiving, and a receiving clerk can audit the machine shop, but neither should be the sole auditor of the area they run every day.

Qualifications matter too. Guidance on auditing is found in ISO 19011:2026, the auditing guidelines, which describe auditor competence and how to manage an audit program. A plant is not required to follow it line by line, but it is a sound reference when deciding how to train internal auditors.

External auditors work for a certification body. These bodies are themselves assessed, and Standards Council of Canada accreditation is how that oversight works for certification bodies operating in Canada. An accredited body's auditors have to meet competence and impartiality requirements that a small plant's internal team does not face.

Important: Independence does not mean the internal auditor must be an outsider. It means the auditor has no stake in the result of the process being audited and can report what they find without pressure.

Small teams and shared roles

Illustrative example: A shop with 25 people may not be able to staff a separate audit function. Common workarounds include cross-auditing between departments, using a trained employee from a sister site, or hiring an outside consultant to conduct the internal audit. The last option is still an internal audit, because the organization owns the program and the report.

How Audit Timing, Scope, and Evidence Differ

Timing is where the two audits feel most different. Internal audits follow a schedule the plant sets, based on the importance of each process and the results of earlier audits. External audits follow the certification cycle: an initial certification audit, periodic surveillance audits and a recertification audit.

Evidence is gathered the same way in both cases: interviews, observation and records. What changes is who judges it. The internal auditor is judging against the plant's own procedures and the standard. The external auditor judges against the standard, the certification scheme rules and the scope of the certificate.

What the schedule looks like in practice

A common approach is to spread internal audits across the year, so that every process is covered once a cycle and weak areas get a second look. The audit plan then lines up with the dates the plant already knows for its surveillance visits. Some plants like to finish a full cycle shortly before the external visit, so the corrective actions are already closed when the auditor arrives.

Did You Know? ISO 9001:2015 requires the organization to retain documented information as evidence of the audit program and the audit results, which is the first thing an external auditor will ask to see.

Comparing Findings, Corrective Actions, and Follow-Up

Both audits produce findings, and both lead to corrective action under the nonconformity requirements of clause 10.2 of ISO 9001:2015. The difference is the audience. An internal finding goes to management, who decide on the fix. An external finding goes into the certification body's report, and depending on its seriousness it can affect whether the certificate is issued or maintained.

External audits commonly sort findings by severity. A minor finding usually means a single lapse or an isolated gap, while a major finding points to a missing requirement or a system failure. The exact terms and timelines belong to the certification body, so read the scheme rules before the visit.

Closing the loop

The strongest internal programs treat each finding the same way an external one is treated. The steps are:

  1. State the finding clearly, with the requirement and the evidence.
  2. Contain the problem so it does not spread.
  3. Investigate the cause, not just the symptom.
  4. Fix the cause and record what was done.
  5. Check later that the fix held.

An internal finding closed with a verified fix is evidence of a working system. An external auditor reading that record sees a plant that finds and corrects its own problems, which is exactly what the standard intends.

Key Consideration: Do not hide minor internal findings to make the report look clean. A report with no findings invites more questions from an external auditor than a report with a few well-handled ones.

How Ontario Manufacturers Can Prepare for Both Audits

Preparation for the two audits overlaps heavily, because the external audit samples the same system the internal audit tests. The plant that runs a disciplined internal program is already doing most of the external preparation. For deeper detail, the 12-point ISO 9001 internal audit checklist for manufacturing plants walks through what to examine, and the 9-point surveillance audit preparation checklist covers the weeks before a visit.

Illustrative example: A family-owned fabricator in southwestern Ontario completes its internal audits early in the year and finds that two work instructions on the weld line no longer match the process. The shop revises both, retrains the operators and verifies the change on the next shift. When the surveillance auditor later asks about the weld line, the plant can show the finding, the fix and the follow-up in one record.

A practical preparation list

  • Confirm the audit program covers every process and shift. Night and weekend operations are easy to miss and easy for an external auditor to sample.
  • Train internal auditors and keep their competence records. Auditors who can explain a requirement in plain words make better findings than those who read from a checklist.
  • Review open corrective actions before the visit. Overdue actions are a common source of avoidable findings.
  • Brief the people who will be interviewed. Employees should know the quality policy, how their work affects it and where to find their instructions; they should not memorize scripts.
  • Keep records retrievable. If a record takes twenty minutes to find, the auditor will notice.

Which Audit Should a Manufacturer Prioritize?

Management system standards require internal audits, but do not require an organization to obtain an external certification audit. A better question is where the next hour of effort pays off most.

Use these situations as a guide:

  • Heading toward first certification: build the internal audit program first, since the certification body will expect to see at least one full cycle of internal audits and a management review.
  • Already certified and facing a surveillance visit: close open internal findings and check that the audit schedule matches the cycle.
  • A customer has raised a concern: run a targeted internal audit on the affected process, then share the corrective action record.
  • Moving to a new edition of the standard: use internal audits to test the changes in your own system before the external auditor does.

Checking an external certificate

Buyers sometimes verify a supplier's certificate rather than take it on trust. The IAF CertSearch database lets a customer confirm that an accredited certificate is valid, which is one more reason to keep the external side of the program in good standing.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often does an ISO 9001 internal audit need to be conducted?

The standard requires internal audits at planned intervals rather than a fixed number of times a year. The plant decides the frequency based on the importance of each process, changes in the business and results of earlier audits. Many plants cover the whole system once a year, with extra attention on weaker areas.

Can an employee conduct an internal ISO audit of their own work?

No. The standard asks for objective and impartial auditors, so an employee should not audit the work they perform or manage. A colleague from another department, a trained employee from another site or an outside consultant can take the audit instead.

Does an external ISO audit replace internal audits?

No. The external audit samples the system and supports certification, but it does not satisfy the requirement for internal audits. The certification body will expect to see the internal audit program and its results as part of its review.

What happens if an external auditor finds a nonconformity?

The plant must respond with containment and corrective action, and the certification body reviews the response. How soon and how thoroughly depends on whether the finding is minor or major, and a major finding can delay certification or put an existing certificate at risk until it is closed.

How should a manufacturer prepare employees for an ISO certification audit?

Explain what an audit is, what the auditor will ask and why honest answers are best. Employees should know the quality policy, understand how their job affects product quality and be able to show where their work instructions and records live. Practicing with a short internal audit interview is more useful than rehearsing answers.
