FSSC 22000 Certification Requirements in Canada: 10 Checks for Food Manufacturers

Understand FSSC 22000 certification requirements in Canada, from food safety controls and audits to documentation, site readiness, and ongoing certification.

ISO Certification Consultants Editorial Team·October 30, 2026·9 min read

Key Takeaways

  • FSSC 22000 builds on ISO 22000:2018, adds prerequisite program rules and extra scheme requirements, and is audited by an approved certification body.
  • FSSC 22000 Version 7 is the current edition, released on 1 May 2026, and audits against Version 6 are permitted until 30 April 2027.
  • Canadian food manufacturers still carry federal duties under the Safe Food for Canadians Regulations, and the certification system has to line up with them.
  • Gaps between written procedures and daily records are a common source of audit findings, so the ten checks below focus on evidence a site can show.

Food manufacturers weighing the FSSC 22000 certification requirements in Canada often begin because a retailer or brand owner asks for a certificate that the Global Food Safety Initiative recognizes. The scheme itself is straightforward to describe. It combines the food safety management system of ISO 22000:2018, a set of prerequisite program requirements, and additional rules set by the scheme owner.

The harder part is rarely the idea of the scheme. It is the gap between a written food safety plan and what operators actually record on the line. The ten checks below follow the order a site can work through them, and each one names the evidence an auditor is likely to ask for.

1. Confirm the Applicable FSSC 22000 Scheme Version and Scope

Start with the scheme documents, not with the procedures. The current edition is FSSC 22000 Version 7, and the owner publishes the requirements and guidance on its scheme documents page. Your certification body confirms which version applies to your audit date.

Check the food chain category that matches your products, because it decides which prerequisite program requirements apply. Sites that make both ready-to-eat and raw products may need to treat them as separate scopes or justify a single scope. Write that decision down before anything else depends on it.

The scope statement also feeds the certificate wording. A vague scope leads to a vague certificate, and a customer reading it may not be able to tell which products are covered. Agree the exact wording with the certification body early, and keep the supporting rationale in your management system records.

Important: Version 6 audits remain permitted until 30 April 2027, so a plant mid-way through certification should ask its certification body which version its audit will use.

2. Define the Food Safety Management System Boundaries

Clause 4.3 of ISO 22000:2018 asks the organization to determine the scope of its food safety management system. That means naming the products, processes, production sites and activities covered, and recording any exclusion with a reason.

Auditors test the boundary by walking it. If a warehouse, a rework area or an off-site storage unit handles your product but is missing from the scope, expect a question. Common boundary decisions include the following.

  • Shared sites: A building shared with another food business needs a clear statement of where your responsibility starts and stops.
  • Outsourced steps: A contract sanitation crew or an outside lab sits inside your system even though you do not employ them.
  • Seasonal lines: A line that runs only part of the year still needs to be in scope if its output carries your certificate.

3. Meet the Relevant Prerequisite Program Requirements

Prerequisite programs are the basic conditions that keep a plant hygienic enough to make safe food. Clause 8.2 of ISO 22000:2018 requires them to be established and maintained, and FSSC 22000 points to a specific technical specification for the food chain category of your site.

Programs differ by site, but typical ones cover building construction, air and water quality, pest control, cleaning and sanitizing, equipment maintenance, waste handling and personal hygiene. Treat these programs as living routines rather than a binder on a shelf. For each one, keep the procedure, the schedule, the record and a way to show someone reviewed the result. A cleaning schedule with no verification record is a finding waiting to happen.

4. Complete Hazard Analysis and Establish Operational Controls

Clause 8.5 of ISO 22000:2018 sets out hazard analysis and the control of hazards, building on the HACCP principles in the Codex Alimentarius. The team lists biological, chemical, physical and, where relevant, allergen hazards at each step, then decides which need control measures.

A hazard analysis that was written once and never revisited is a weak point. Keep it tied to the real flow diagram, confirm the diagram on the floor, and update it whenever a product, ingredient or piece of equipment changes. Operational controls and critical limits should be monitored by people who understand why the limit exists.

Did You Know? ISO 22000:2018 distinguishes between operational prerequisite programs and critical control points, and the hazard analysis is where the team decides which category each control measure belongs in.

5. Document Food Defense and Food Fraud Assessments

FSSC 22000 asks sites to assess two threats that a classic hazard analysis can miss: deliberate contamination and economic adulteration. Food defense looks at who can reach product, and food fraud looks at which ingredients are vulnerable to substitution or dilution.

Food defense and fraud work is often handled by the same cross-functional team that runs the hazard analysis, which keeps the findings consistent. Each assessment needs a documented method, a named team and a set of mitigation measures. Keep the reasoning visible, because the same logic will be tested again at every surveillance audit. An auditor wants to see why a particular raw material was rated low or high risk, not only the final score, and wants to see that the assessment is reviewed when suppliers or ingredients change.

6. Establish Traceability, Recall, and Emergency Procedures

Clause 8.3 of ISO 22000:2018 covers the traceability system, and clause 8.4 covers emergency preparedness and response. In Canada these also connect to licensing, preventive control plans and traceability duties under the Safe Food for Canadians Regulations.

A mock recall can help assess the traceability and recall process. Pick a finished lot and trace it back to ingredient lots and forward to customers, then see how long it takes and whether the numbers balance. Run the exercise yourself before the certification audit, and record what it showed and what you changed.

7. Verify Competence, Training, and Food Safety Culture

Clause 7.2 of ISO 22000:2018 requires the organization to determine the competence people need and to confirm they have it. FSSC 22000 also expects management to build and measure a food safety culture, which goes beyond training certificates.

Competence records should connect to the job, and they should be reviewed whenever a role changes. An operator who monitors a critical limit needs training on the limit, the reason for it and the action to take when it is missed. Culture shows up in smaller ways: whether operators report a near miss, whether a supervisor stops a line without fear, and whether leaders attend the food safety meetings they call.

8. Control Suppliers, Services, and Outsourced Processes

A food safety system is only as strong as the ingredients and services entering the plant. ISO 22000:2018 requires control of externally provided processes, products and services, which covers raw material suppliers, packaging, laboratories, pest control and transport.

Build an approved supplier process that matches the risk. A supplier of a high-risk ingredient may need a certificate recognized by the Global Food Safety Initiative or an on-site audit, while a low-risk packaging supplier may need only a questionnaire. Keep the approval criteria, the review schedule and the incoming checks in one place, and make sure purchasing staff know that a change of source needs food safety review before the first order. For help linking this to the wider quality system, see the ISO 22000 food safety service.

9. Complete Internal Audits and Management Review

Clause 9.2 of ISO 22000:2018 requires internal audits at planned intervals, and clause 9.3 requires top management to review the system. Both must be finished, with records, before the certification body arrives. Plan them early enough that corrective actions have time to close, because an open action from last week reads very differently from one closed and verified.

Internal audits work best when they test the floor as well as the paperwork. Auditors should be independent of the area they audit, and findings should reach a corrective action with an owner and a date.

Management review needs real inputs, such as audit results, complaints, monitoring trends, supplier performance and resource needs. The outputs should be decisions, not a summary of the meeting.

10. Prepare for Certification Audits and Ongoing Surveillance

An initial certification audit runs in two stages. The first reviews readiness and documentation, and the second tests whether the system works in practice at the site. After certification, surveillance audits and recertification follow on a cycle set out in the scheme rules, and the scheme includes unannounced audits, so the plant must stay ready at all times.

Before the first stage, close the gaps your own audit found and agree the audit plan with your certification body. A food and beverage plant can compare its current system with the requirements through a free ISO readiness assessment, and the food and beverage industry page explains how ISO Certification Consultants approach plants like yours. The Canadian Food Inspection Agency remains a separate regulator, so keep federal inspection findings in the same corrective action process as certification findings.

Key Consideration: Treat surveillance as a year-round routine. Keep records current, close corrective actions on time, and review changes in products or suppliers as they happen, not in the weeks before an audit.

Prerequisite programsProcedures, schedules and verification records

A site that can produce each item on demand is in a much stronger position. The ones that cause delay are often the quiet items, such as an out-of-date flow diagram or a supplier file that was never reviewed.
