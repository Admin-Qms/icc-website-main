ISO 13485 and MDSAP Canada Requirements: What Medical Device Firms Need to Know

Understand ISO 13485 and MDSAP Canada requirements, how audits differ, which records to prepare, and how medical device manufacturers can stay ready.

ISO Certification Consultants Editorial Team·November 14, 2026·11 min read

Key Takeaways

  • ISO 13485:2016 is the quality management system standard for medical devices, and MDSAP is an audit program that checks that system against the rules of several regulators at once.
  • In Canada, manufacturers of higher-risk devices need an MDSAP certificate, not only an ISO 13485 certificate, to hold a device license.
  • An MDSAP audit follows a defined audit model and adds regulatory checks that an ISO 13485 audit alone does not cover.
  • Complaint handling, adverse event reporting, corrective action and supplier control are the areas where findings tend to cluster.
  • Readiness is a matter of keeping records connected and current, not of a cleanup sprint before the auditor arrives.

Plenty of medical device makers hold an ISO 13485 certificate and still discover, when a Canadian license application or renewal comes up, that the certificate on the wall is not the one Health Canada asks for. Understanding ISO 13485 and MDSAP Canada requirements together is what separates a smooth license cycle from a scramble.

The sections below start with who needs which certificate, then follow an audit from the first question to the closure of a finding.

How ISO 13485 and MDSAP Fit into Canada's Medical Device Framework

ISO 13485:2016 sets out the requirements for a quality management system for organizations involved in the life cycle of medical devices. The standard is published by ISO, and the official ISO 13485:2016 page describes its scope. It was reviewed and confirmed in 2025, so the 2016 edition remains current.

MDSAP, the Medical Device Single Audit Program, is different in kind. It is not a standard. It is an audit program in which one audit, run by a recognized auditing organization, produces a result that several participating regulators can use. Health Canada is one of those regulators, alongside the regulators of Australia, Brazil, Japan and the United States.

The legal basis in Canada sits in the Medical Devices Regulations. The Medical Devices Regulations set the licensing and quality system requirements, and Health Canada requires evidence of a quality system certificate for devices above the lowest risk class.

Important: An MDSAP certificate is issued after an audit that assesses a manufacturer against ISO 13485:2016 plus the regulatory requirements of the participating jurisdictions. A certificate issued only against ISO 13485:2016 does not take its place for a Canadian device license.

A practical consequence: the same procedure can satisfy the standard and still fail the audit, because the audit also tests it against a regulator's own wording.

Which Manufacturers Need to Consider MDSAP

Health Canada classifies devices by risk, from Class I (lowest) to Class IV (highest). The Health Canada overview of medical devices explains how classification and licensing work.

In practical terms, the obligation falls like this:

  • Class II, III and IV manufacturers must hold a valid MDSAP certificate to obtain and keep a Canadian medical device license for those devices.
  • Class I manufacturers do not need an MDSAP certificate for a license, although many still build to ISO 13485:2016 because customers and distributors ask for it.
  • Contract manufacturers and suppliers to a licensed manufacturer are usually not certified themselves, but they are examined through the manufacturer's supplier controls, so their records matter.

The Healthcare & Medical Devices industry page on this site, healthcare and medical devices, covers how these pressures show up in different segments. For the certification side, the ISO 13485 medical devices service page outlines how the standard is put in place.

Manufacturers that sell only in Canada can still be pulled into the program, because the Canadian license requirement alone is the trigger. Firms that also sell in the other participating markets gain the benefit of one audit serving several regulators.

Key ISO 13485 Requirements Auditors Assess

An auditor reviewing a medical device quality system looks first at whether the system is established, documented and maintained, and then at whether it is actually used. Several parts of ISO 13485:2016 draw close attention.

The medical device file and document control

Clause 4.2.3 of ISO 13485:2016 requires a medical device file for each type or family of device. The file holds the specifications, manufacturing and testing procedures, and labeling that define the product. Auditors often pick one device and ask to see that every part of the file is current and approved.

Risk management across the life cycle

Clause 7.1 of ISO 13485:2016 requires the organization to document processes for risk management in product realization. The auditor expects to see risk thinking in design, purchasing, production and complaint handling, not as a one-time exercise.

Design, purchasing and production

Clause 7.3 covers design and development, clause 7.4 covers purchasing, and clause 7.5 covers production and service provision. These are the places where traceability and validation evidence are tested hardest, because a gap here can affect patient safety directly.

A useful practice for the purchasing side is described in the guide on how to build ISO 13485 supplier controls for medical device purchasing, which walks through the evidence an auditor expects to see.

Feedback, complaints and corrective action

Clause 8.2.2 covers complaint handling, clause 8.2.3 covers reporting to regulatory authorities, and clause 8.5.2 covers corrective action. Together they form the loop that shows whether the quality system responds when a device performs differently in the field.

Did You Know? ISO 13485:2016 requires the organization to document procedures for internal audit in clause 8.2.4, and management review in clause 5.6 must look at inputs such as feedback, complaint handling and reporting to regulators.

What to Expect During an MDSAP Audit

An MDSAP audit is run by an auditing organization recognized under the program. It follows a published audit model that organizes the work into chapters, so auditors across different organizations cover the same ground in a consistent way.

The model covers the following areas:

  1. Management, including the quality manual, management review and resources.
  2. Device marketing authorization and facility registration, which checks that the manufacturer holds the licenses and registrations its markets require.
  3. Measurement, analysis and improvement, including internal audit, nonconforming product and corrective action.
  4. Medical device adverse event and advisory notice reporting, which tests whether the manufacturer reports to regulators on time.
  5. Design and development, where the auditor traces a design from inputs to transfer.
  6. Production and service controls, including validation, sterilization where it applies, and traceability.
  7. Purchasing, which examines how suppliers are selected, evaluated and monitored.

The regulatory layer is the difference. The auditor does not only ask whether the procedure meets the standard; the auditor also asks whether the procedure meets what each participating regulator, including Health Canada, requires, such as the way a mandatory problem report is made.

Audit cycle and grading

The audit cycle generally begins with an initial certification audit in two stages, followed by regular surveillance audits and a recertification audit before the certificate cycle ends. Findings are graded by their significance, and the grade determines how quickly the manufacturer must respond and whether the certificate is at risk.

Key Consideration: Ask the auditing organization early how it schedules the stages and how it handles the grading of findings. Knowing the response deadlines before an audit begins is far easier than learning them afterward.

Records and Processes to Prepare

Auditors do not accept a description of how the system works; they ask for the records that prove it. Preparation is mostly a matter of knowing where each record lives and who owns it.

Records an auditor commonly asks to see include:

  • Medical device files with current specifications, procedures and labeling for the products in scope.
  • Design and development files showing inputs, outputs, reviews, verification, validation and transfer, with the approvals dated.
  • Complaint files that show each complaint was received, evaluated, investigated where needed, and assessed for reporting to the regulator.
  • Supplier evaluation records that show how critical suppliers were approved, how performance is monitored and what happens when it slips.
  • Internal audit reports and management review minutes that show the system is checking itself and that top management acts on what it learns.
  • Corrective action records that follow a problem from description through root cause, action and verification of effectiveness.

Illustrative example: A small contract manufacturer of disposable device components receives a customer complaint about a cracked housing. The complaint is logged, linked to the lot, assessed against the risk file, and a corrective action is opened. When the auditor asks to follow the thread, the manufacturer can show each step in order without searching through several folders.

Illustrative example: For a Canadian license, an auditor might pick one complaint about a device that could have contributed to a serious deterioration in health. The auditor asks when the manufacturer became aware of it, whether it was judged reportable to Health Canada, and where the report and its timing are recorded. If the complaint number, the risk file entry and the regulator report all carry the same reference, the answer takes minutes. If the complaint log and the regulatory file use different identifiers, the manufacturer must reconstruct the link while the auditor waits.

How to Address Findings and Maintain Readiness

Findings are a normal part of any audit cycle, and the way a manufacturer responds matters as much as the finding itself. A strong response states the root cause, the immediate containment and the date on which effectiveness will be verified; a weak one only says the issue was fixed.

A few habits keep a team out of trouble:

  • Treat the root cause as the work. A finding closed by retraining one operator, when the real issue was an unclear procedure, usually comes back at the next audit.
  • Check effectiveness on purpose. Clause 8.5.2 of ISO 13485:2016 requires the organization to review the effectiveness of corrective action taken, so build a date and an owner for that review into every action.
  • Run internal audits against the MDSAP model. Using the same chapter structure internally means the team practices on the ground an external auditor will cover.
  • Track regulatory changes. Reporting timelines and licensing details are set by the regulators, so someone should own the job of watching for updates from Health Canada and the other participating authorities.

Management review, internal audit and supplier reviews all feed the same picture, so put them on a fixed calendar with named owners rather than gathering them when an audit date is announced.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a Canadian medical device manufacturer need both ISO 13485 certification and MDSAP?

A manufacturer of Class II, III or IV devices needs an MDSAP certificate for a Canadian device license. The MDSAP audit assesses the quality system against ISO 13485:2016 and regulatory requirements, so the work of meeting ISO 13485 is the foundation. Class I manufacturers do not need the certificate for a license, though they may still choose ISO 13485 certification for customer reasons.

What does an MDSAP audit assess beyond ISO 13485 requirements?

It adds the regulatory requirements of the participating jurisdictions. Examples include checking that marketing authorizations and facility registrations are in place and that adverse events and advisory notices are reported as each regulator requires. The audit model also gives the auditor a fixed structure to follow.

How often does a medical device manufacturer undergo an MDSAP audit?

After the initial certification audit, the certificate cycle includes surveillance audits at regular intervals and a recertification audit before the cycle ends. The auditing organization sets the exact schedule within the program rules, so a manufacturer should confirm timing with its own auditing organization.

Which records should a manufacturer prepare for an MDSAP audit?

Prepare the medical device files, design and development records, complaint and adverse event files, supplier evaluations, internal audit reports, management review minutes and corrective action records. The goal is for each record to link to the others so an auditor can trace one issue from start to finish.

Can an ISO 13485 certificate replace an MDSAP certificate in Canada?

No. For Class II, III and IV devices, Health Canada looks for an MDSAP certificate. An ISO 13485 certificate alone does not meet that expectation, even though the two share the same quality system foundation.
