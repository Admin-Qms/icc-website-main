FSSC 22000 vs SQF Comparison: Which Food Safety Standard Fits Your Plant?

Understand this FSSC 22000 vs SQF comparison to select the right GFSI-recognized food safety certification for your manufacturing plant and buyer mandates.

ISO Certification Consultants Editorial Team·October 18, 2026·12 min read

Key Takeaways

  • Both schemes are benchmarked by the Global Food Safety Initiative, so the choice rarely comes down to which one is "better". It comes down to which one your buyers accept and which one fits how your plant already works.
  • FSSC 22000 is built on ISO 22000:2018, so plants that already run an ISO management system find its structure familiar. SQF is a standalone food safety code with its own module numbering.
  • The audit result looks different. SQF produces a numerical score and rating, while FSSC 22000 grades findings as minor, major or critical.
  • Cost and effort depend more on plant size, site count and existing prerequisite programs than on the logo on the certificate.
  • Ask your top customers in writing which scheme they accept before you commit to either one.

A food plant manager who has just received a supplier questionnaire from a new retail customer often meets the same line: "GFSI-recognized certification required." That line starts every FSSC 22000 vs SQF comparison, because both schemes qualify and both are respected. The real question is which one suits your facility, your buyers and your team.

This guide walks through how the two are built, how the audits differ, what each demands of the plant, and a simple way to decide.

Architectural Differences: ISO Foundation vs. Standalone Food Safety Code

FSSC 22000 is a certification scheme that sits on top of ISO 22000:2018, the international standard for food safety management systems. The scheme adds two layers: a sector-specific prerequisite program specification for the type of operation, and a set of additional requirements written by the scheme owner. The current edition is FSSC 22000 Version 7, released on 1 May 2026, and audits against Version 6 are permitted until 30 April 2027. The scheme owner publishes the FSSC 22000 scheme documents so you can read the exact requirements before you decide.

SQF takes a different route. It is a food safety and quality code, currently in Edition 9, with its own modules for system elements and for good manufacturing practices by sector. A processing plant typically works to the manufacturing module together with the system elements module. The code does not require an ISO management system, although it asks for many of the same disciplines.

Because of that difference, the two feel different on the floor:

  • FSSC 22000 follows the ISO high-level structure, with context, leadership, planning, support, operation, performance evaluation and improvement. A plant that already runs ISO 9001:2026 (though many certified organizations are still on the 2015 edition during the transition) will recognize the layout and can often share internal audit and management review processes.
  • SQF follows its own code, with numbered elements covering management commitment, document control, verification and food safety plans. Plants that never adopted an ISO standard often find this layout easier to read as a checklist.
  • Both are HACCP-based. The hazard analysis principles from the Codex Alimentarius sit under each scheme, so the technical core of your food safety plan is the same either way.

If you want a refresher on how the ISO side relates to hazard analysis, the article on ISO 22000 vs HACCP differences explains how the framework and the method fit together.

Did You Know? ISO 22000:2018 requires both a hazard analysis and operational prerequisite programs, and it expects the organization to manage them as part of one system rather than as separate binders.

GFSI Recognition and Buyer Acceptance: Canadian Retailers vs. Global Export

The Global Food Safety Initiative benchmarks certification programs against a common set of requirements. FSSC 22000 and SQF both hold that recognition, which means a buyer who accepts GFSI-benchmarked certification can accept either one on paper.

In practice, buyers write their own supplier requirements. Some name a single scheme, some list several, and some accept any benchmarked scheme. Acceptance also varies by channel. A plant selling private-label product to a grocery chain, a plant supplying ingredients to a multinational manufacturer and a plant exporting to Europe or Asia may each hear a different preference.

Treat buyer acceptance as a fact-finding task rather than a guess:

  1. List your five largest customers and your top prospects. Include distributors, co-manufacturing partners and food service accounts.
  2. Ask each one for their supplier requirements document. Look for the exact scheme names and the edition they reference.
  3. Check export markets separately. A scheme that satisfies a domestic retailer may not be the one a foreign importer names.
  4. Record the answers in one table. If the answers split evenly, favor the scheme that costs less to maintain alongside your existing systems.

Certification does not replace federal requirements. Plants that manufacture food for sale across provincial borders or for export still answer to the Safe Food for Canadians Regulations, including licensing, preventive controls and traceability. The Safe Food for Canadians Regulations set that baseline, and a GFSI certificate supports compliance with it but does not stand in for it.

Audit Methodology and Scoring: Pass/Fail Assessments vs. Numerical Grading

The two audit models answer the same question in different ways.

An SQF audit produces a numerical score. The auditor starts from a full score and deducts points for each nonconformity according to its severity, and the final number maps to a rating. Plants that like measurable progress often appreciate this, because a score gives management something to improve year over year.

An FSSC 22000 audit grades each nonconformity as minor, major or critical. The certification decision depends on whether those findings are closed within the required time, not on a score. A critical finding can suspend or withdraw the certificate, and the scheme also includes unannounced audits as part of the certification cycle.

Important: A score and a grade measure different things. A high SQF score does not mean the plant would clear an FSSC 22000 audit without findings, and the reverse is true as well. Do not compare the two results as if they sat on one scale.

Here is how the main points line up:

The practical effect is that SQF rewards plants that document and verify everything cleanly, while FSSC 22000 rewards plants that run a joined-up management system with a working improvement loop.

Implementation Overhead and Prerequisite Program Requirements

Neither scheme is light work, and neither is easy for a plant that has only a basic food safety plan today.

Both expect strong prerequisite programs: sanitation, pest control, supplier approval, allergen management, training, maintenance and traceability. The difference is where the requirements are written. FSSC 22000 pulls its prerequisite requirements from sector specifications and its scheme additions. SQF writes them into the sector module of the code.

Plants moving toward either scheme tend to spend their effort in the same places:

  • Hazard analysis and the food safety plan. The team needs a documented analysis that matches the actual products and lines, not a template copied from another facility.
  • Food defense and food fraud assessments. Both schemes expect a documented, reviewed assessment, and both expect the plant to act on the results.
  • Verification and validation. Records must show that the controls work and that someone checked, which is where many first-time audits find gaps.
  • Management commitment and review. Leadership has to be visibly involved, with documented reviews and resourcing decisions.

Key Consideration: If your plant already holds an ISO certificate, map your existing internal audit, corrective action and management review procedures against the new scheme before writing anything new. Reuse is the largest time saver, and it keeps the system to one set of procedures instead of two.

A plant with no ISO background can still choose FSSC 22000, but it will build the management system layer at the same time as the food safety content. That is more to learn, though it also leaves the plant with a structure that can later support other standards. The ISO 22000 food safety service page describes how that build usually runs.

Total Cost of Certification and Multi-Site Surveillance Demands

Certification cost has several parts: the certification body's audit fees, the scheme's licensing and registration fees, internal time, consultant support if used, and any capital work the gap analysis uncovers. Fees vary by certification body, plant size, shift pattern and product complexity, so ask for written quotes from at least two accredited bodies for each scheme.

Several factors move the total more than the scheme choice:

  • Audit duration. More lines, more shifts and more product categories mean more audit days.
  • Site count. A company with several plants has to decide whether each site is certified on its own or whether the scheme's multi-site rules apply, and eligibility depends on how centrally the sites are managed.
  • Surveillance cycle. Both schemes require yearly surveillance and a full recertification cycle, so budget for the ongoing cost, not only the first audit.
  • Remediation. If a gap analysis shows the plant needs a new washroom layout, allergen segregation or metal detection upgrade, that capital cost can dwarf the audit fees.

Illustrative example: A food co-packer with a single plant and one shift is deciding between the two schemes. Its largest customer accepts either one. The plant already runs ISO 9001, and its quality manager has years of internal audit experience. In that case, the shared management system structure can make FSSC 22000 cheaper to maintain, because the same internal audit program covers both. If the same plant had no ISO system and relied on a customer-driven checklist culture, SQF might feel faster to adopt.

Decision Framework: Choosing the Right Standard for Your Processing Facility

Work through these questions in order. The first one with a firm answer usually settles the decision.

  1. Does a key customer name one scheme? If so, choose that scheme unless there is a strong reason not to.
  2. Do you export? Check what the importing market and its buyers require before deciding.
  3. Do you already run ISO 9001, ISO 14001:2026 (though many organizations remain on the 2015 edition during the transition) or ISO 45001:2018? If yes, FSSC 22000 usually lets you extend a system you already have.
  4. Does your team prefer a code-based checklist with a score? If yes, SQF may suit how your auditors and supervisors think.
  5. Do you operate several plants? Compare how each scheme treats multi-site certification before you commit.
  6. Is a second certificate likely within three years? If so, choose the scheme that can be reused without rebuilding the system.

If the answers stay mixed, a short gap assessment against both schemes will show which one sits closer to where your plant is today. The free ISO readiness assessment is a useful first step for plants weighing that kind of question.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is FSSC 22000 more difficult to implement than SQF Edition 9?

Neither is easy, and difficulty depends on where the plant starts. A plant with an existing ISO management system usually finds FSSC 22000 familiar, while a plant without one has to build that layer. A plant used to customer checklists may find the SQF code easier to follow line by line.

Do major Canadian grocers prefer SQF or FSSC 22000 certification?

Preferences vary by buyer and change over time, so no single answer applies. Both schemes are GFSI-benchmarked, and many buyers accept either. Ask each customer for its current supplier requirements document and record which scheme and edition it names.

Can a food processing facility transition from SQF to FSSC 22000 without starting over?

Yes, in most cases the plant keeps its hazard analysis, prerequisite programs, training records and traceability system. What it adds is the management system structure, the scheme's additional requirements and any items the new audit model treats differently. A gap assessment against the new scheme shows how much work remains.

How does the audit scoring system differ between FSSC 22000 and SQF?

SQF produces a numerical score that maps to a rating, with points deducted for nonconformities by severity. FSSC 22000 grades findings as minor, major or critical, and certification depends on closing them within the required time. The results should not be compared as if they used the same scale.

Which standard is more cost-effective for small-to-midsize food manufacturers?

It depends on what the plant already has. A family-owned operation with an ISO system in place may spend less on FSSC 22000 because procedures are shared. A regional processor with no ISO history may find SQF a more direct path. Request written quotes for both from accredited certification bodies and compare the full cost over the three-year cycle.
