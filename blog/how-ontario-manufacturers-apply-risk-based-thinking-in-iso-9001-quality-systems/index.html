How Ontario Manufacturers Apply Risk-Based Thinking in ISO 9001 Quality Systems

Discover real risk-based thinking examples for manufacturing. Learn how Ontario factories integrate ISO 9001 risk principles into operations and planning.

ISO Certification Consultants Editorial Team·November 27, 2026·11 min read

Key Takeaways

  • Risk-based thinking connects uncertainty to decisions about quality processes and customer requirements.
  • Manufacturers can examine supplier changes, equipment, training, production and customer feedback when deciding where controls are needed.
  • A useful response has an owner, a proportionate action and a way to check whether it worked.
  • Records should support decisions and follow-up; a long risk register is not an end in itself.

A supplier changes the material used in a component. Before the next production run, a manufacturer needs to decide whether existing inspection and process settings still provide enough confidence that the finished part will meet its requirements. That decision is one practical place to apply risk-based thinking in an ISO 9001 quality system.

Risk is not limited to a line on a management review agenda. It can appear when a plant accepts an order, introduces a process, trains an operator or decides what evidence to obtain from a supplier. The point is to connect the uncertainty to a decision and check the outcome, rather than wait for a defect to show where the process was vulnerable.

What risk-based thinking means in a quality system

ISO 9001:2026 provides a framework for a quality management system; many certified organizations remain on the 2015 edition during the transition. The ISO explanation of ISO 9001 describes the standard's focus on consistent products and services, customer requirements and improvement. A manufacturer should use the edition and certification arrangements that apply to its own system when determining its obligations.

Risk-based thinking means considering uncertainty when planning and operating processes. A team identifies what could affect intended results, decides whether action is needed, integrates the response into the work and evaluates what happened. Opportunities can also inform a decision, provided the team understands the conditions and consequences of the proposed change.

Key Consideration: The decision should be traceable to a process and an intended result, not just a score in a spreadsheet.

A quality concern may affect a particular dimension on a part, the availability of a calibrated instrument or the consistency of an outsourced process. The process owner needs enough information to decide what to do, who will do it and what evidence will show the decision remains appropriate. The ISO 9001 quality management overview provides context for the wider system in which those decisions sit.

The useful question is what could prevent the process from meeting its requirements. It should be followed by a second question: what existing control would detect or prevent that outcome? Those answers help the team identify a gap before it chooses a response.

Why the exercise becomes disconnected from production

A register can make ownership and review easier, but a list of risks does not by itself change how a plant works. If an action is recorded in a meeting and never reaches the operator, buyer or maintenance planner, the document may be complete while the process remains unchanged.

Another problem is assigning a number without agreeing what it means. A team may use scales for likelihood and impact, but the criteria and decision rules need to be clear enough that the people using them can explain the result. A simple comparison can be more useful than an elaborate formula when reliable data is limited.

Consider the handoff from purchasing to quality. A buyer might know that an approved source has changed its process, while the inspection team continues using the previous sampling arrangement. The issue is not a particular department's form; it is whether the change reaches the people who need to assess its effect.

Important: A risk assessment is not a substitute for an immediate response when nonconforming product is found. Follow the plant's product-control process first.

Five practical manufacturing examples

The examples below are hypothetical and describe decisions a manufacturer could assess. The appropriate action depends on the product, process, customer requirements and evidence available to the plant.

1. A change in a supplied component

Illustrative example: An unnamed Ontario manufacturer learns that a supplier intends to move a machining operation to different equipment. Purchasing notifies quality before the next delivery. The team compares the change with the approved requirements, considers which characteristics could be affected and decides whether to request additional evidence or adjust receiving checks.

The decision record identifies the change, the people responsible and the evidence expected before the material is released. A later review compares inspection results with the original concern. The response can be reduced or revised when the team has enough information; it should not become a permanent extra task without an owner.

2. A production setup depends on one person

Illustrative example: An unnamed plant finds that a complex setup is normally performed by one experienced operator. A supervisor and the operator identify the steps that affect product quality, review the current instruction and arrange for another qualified person to learn the process. The team checks the instruction against an actual setup before relying on it.

The concern is continuity and consistency, not the employee's performance. An appropriate control might include an available work instruction and evidence of competence. The effectiveness question is whether another authorized person can perform the task under normal conditions and recognize when to stop.

3. A measurement device becomes unavailable

Illustrative example: An unnamed manufacturer uses a measuring device to confirm a characteristic before releasing a part. Maintenance identifies that the device is unavailable. Quality and production check the documented process for identifying affected work and determine whether a suitable verified alternative is available.

The team should not assume that a substitute device measures the same way. It needs the appropriate capability and status for the intended use. The decision may involve holding affected product until the responsible person can confirm the evidence needed for release.

4. A process change affects a production handoff

Illustrative example: An unnamed fabricator changes the sequence in which parts move between finishing and final inspection. The process owners review identification and handling arrangements to prevent work from being mistaken for material that has already passed inspection. They update the relevant instruction and check the first handoff against it.

A status label is useful only if the next person understands and uses it. The team should examine how material is identified, where it waits and who has authority to change its status. The plant's own process determines the specific control; the example does not prescribe a universal layout.

5. Customer complaints point to an emerging pattern

Illustrative example: An unnamed manufacturer reviews several customer reports about packaging damage. The quality team compares products, shipments and handling conditions before deciding whether the reports share a cause. It assigns follow-up to the process owner and checks later shipments against the action taken.

Similar descriptions warrant investigation, but they do not establish a shared root cause. A process for capturing complaints and reviewing their status helps the team distinguish an isolated event from a pattern that needs a broader response. The manufacturing industry overview outlines the range of quality pressures such teams must manage.

Finding risk across production and the supply chain

Begin at the points where information, material or responsibility changes hands. Examples include order review, supplier approval, incoming inspection, setup, production, measurement and delivery. Ask what each process is expected to achieve and what information the next process needs.

A short review can follow this sequence:

  • Describe the result. Identify the product requirement or process outcome the team is trying to protect.
  • Name the uncertainty. Explain the condition or change that could affect that outcome without assuming it has already caused a defect.
  • Examine existing controls. Identify how the process prevents, detects or responds to the concern today.
  • Choose an action and owner. Decide what is proportionate, who will carry it out and when the decision will be reviewed.
  • Check the result. Agree what evidence would show whether the action worked or needs adjustment.

The team can use existing records where they answer these questions. For example, a change request, maintenance plan or supplier review may already contain the decision and owner. Duplicating information across disconnected systems can make it harder to know which action is current.

The NIST Manufacturing Extension Partnership connects manufacturers with assistance through a network of centers. An outside resource can help a team examine its process, but the manufacturer still owns its decisions, controls and evidence.

Build decisions into quality documentation

Start with the document or process where the decision is made. If the concern relates to a supplier change, the relevant purchasing or supplier-control process may be the right place to define who is notified and how the change is assessed. If it relates to setup, the work instruction or training arrangement may need attention.

Keep the evidence close to the decision. A useful record explains the issue, the criteria considered, the owner, the chosen response and how follow-up will be assessed. The level of detail should fit the issue and the organization's own requirements; a routine decision does not need the same record as a complex process change.

A shared system can make it easier to find an open action and its supporting records. Before adopting any software workflow, check how the organization will assign responsibility, control document versions and confirm that people use the current information. The quality objectives article discusses how measurable objectives can support management review.

Review the decision when circumstances change. A supplier response, new equipment, a recurring complaint or a revised customer requirement may make the earlier assessment incomplete. The owner should record the reason for a meaningful change and communicate any revised instruction to the people affected.

Common pitfalls and practical checks

A frequent difficulty is treating every uncertainty as equally important. A team can focus its attention by considering the potential effect on requirements, the controls already in place and the evidence available.

Another difficulty is closing an action when the task is complete but before anyone checks its effectiveness. Installing a control, training a person or updating an instruction can be necessary; the later check asks whether the concern has actually been addressed under the conditions that matter.

The following checks help keep review practical:

  • The person responsible for each open action can explain the next step and the expected evidence.
  • The affected process has received any new instruction or communication it needs.
  • A change in the original assumptions prompts a review of the decision.
  • Management receives relevant information about unresolved issues and results.

A completed action and an effective action are different conclusions. The first can be confirmed by a task record; the second requires evidence appropriate to the concern. The plant should decide what that evidence looks like before the action is closed.

Frequently Asked Questions

What counts as risk-based thinking in ISO 9001?

It is the consideration of uncertainty when planning and operating the quality management system. In practice, a manufacturer connects a concern to the intended result, decides whether action is needed and reviews its effect. The specific evidence depends on the process and applicable system requirements.

Does a manufacturer need to document every identified risk?

The organization should determine the documented information it needs for an effective system and to meet applicable requirements. A separate entry for every uncertainty is not necessarily the most useful way to support a decision. Existing process records may provide the evidence needed for planning and follow-up.

How does risk-based thinking differ from quality control?

Quality control checks whether results meet specified requirements. Risk-based thinking considers what could affect intended results and informs the design and operation of controls. The two support each other: inspection results can inform a risk review, and that review can inform an inspection plan.

Can a smaller manufacturer use the same approach as a larger plant?

Both can identify uncertainty, choose responses and evaluate results, but their methods and resources may differ. A smaller team can use a proportionate process with clear responsibilities and accessible records. It should still consider customer and applicable system requirements.

What happens if an auditor cannot find evidence of risk-based thinking?

An auditor assesses the available evidence against applicable audit criteria and discusses the observation with the organization. The outcome depends on the actual requirement, evidence and circumstances. A missing standalone register should not automatically be treated as proof that risk was ignored.
